The residual stream: one model, \(L\) identical blocks

Page 03 gave you attention. A decoder-only language model is that attention layer plus a per-position MLP, each wrapped in a residual connection 残差连接, stacked \(L\) times between a token embedding and a projection back to the vocabulary. Nothing else. Following the course convention, a token's hidden state \(x_t \in \R^d\) is a row vector and linear maps are \(xW\). For a batch of \(B\) sequences of \(T\) token ids, the whole forward pass is:

\[ \begin{aligned} x^{(0)} &= E[\text{ids}] \;(+\;P) && \in \R^{B \times T \times d} && \text{embedding lookup, plus a position table if there is one}\\ x^{(l)} &= \text{Block}_l\big(x^{(l-1)}\big), \quad l = 1, \dots, L && \in \R^{B \times T \times d}\\ \text{logits} &= \RMSNorm\big(x^{(L)}\big)\, W_U && \in \R^{B \times T \times V} && \text{final norm, then the LM head} \end{aligned} \]

Every position predicts the next token, so the loss is the mean over all \(B \cdot T\) positions of \(-\log \softmax(\text{logits}_t)[\text{id}_{t+1}]\) (page 01). One forward pass therefore yields \(B \cdot T\) training examples. A pre-norm block, the modern default, is two lines:

\[ h = x + \text{Attn}\big(\RMSNorm(x)\big), \qquad x' = h + \text{FFN}\big(\RMSNorm(h)\big) \]
Pre-norm decoder block. Attn is causal (page 03); the feed-forward network 前馈网络 FFN acts on each position separately.

The useful mental picture, due to [Elhage et al. 2021], is the residual stream 残差流: the vector each token carries through the network. Each sublayer "reads" from the stream (a linear projection of a normalized copy) and "writes" to it by adding its output. Attention is the only place where positions exchange information. The FFN is position-wise: the same function, with the same weights, applied to every \(t\) independently. Unrolling the recursion shows that the final state is the embedding plus \(2L\) additive writes:

\[ x^{(L)} = x^{(0)} + \sum_{j=1}^{2L} f_j\big(\text{Norm}(x^{[j]})\big) \]

where \(x^{[j]}\) is the stream just before the \(j\)-th sublayer.

Why residual connections make depth trainable

With identity skip connections, the loss gradient at layer \(l\) splits into a term that flows straight back and a term that flows through the weights [He et al. 2016, Eq. 4-5]:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x_l} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial x_L}\left(I + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_l}\sum_{i=l}^{L-1} F_i(x_i)\right) \]

The additive identity means, in He et al.'s words, that "the gradient of a layer does not vanish even when the weights are arbitrarily small." They also test breaking the shortcut. On a 110-layer ResNet, scaling it by 0.5, gating it, or replacing it with a 1×1 convolution all did worse than the plain identity (6.61% test error, against 8.70% to 12.86% for the variants that converged), and the unrescaled 0.5 shortcut and a shortcut with dropout failed outright (error above 20%) [He et al. 2016, Table 1]. Their conclusion, "keep the shortcut clean," is the design principle behind everything in the next section.

If your tagger used the standard encoder recipe, its block was this block. Three things change. First, the attention mask is causal: position \(t\) sees only positions \(\le t\), so the model is deliberately not permutation-invariant and needs positional information (page 05). Second, instead of pooling to one class token and a small head, every position emits a distribution over \(V \approx 128{,}000\) classes. Third, the norm moves in front of the sublayers, and if you followed the original recipe you used post-norm with a warm-up schedule, which is exactly what the next section explains.

Where to put the norm: Post-LN versus Pre-LN

Normalization is not optional: a Transformer with no normalization at all failed to train in the RMSNorm paper's WMT14 experiment (BLEU 0) [Zhang & Sennrich 2019, Table 4]. The design question is where the norm goes relative to the residual connection. The original Transformer normalizes after the addition, and this is called Post-LN 后归一化 [Vaswani et al. 2017, §3.1]:

\[ \textbf{Post-LN:}\quad y = \LayerNorm\big(x + F(x)\big) \]

Modern LLMs normalize the input of each sublayer instead, called Pre-LN 预归一化, and add one more norm after the last block [Xiong et al. 2020, Table 1]:

\[ \textbf{Pre-LN:}\quad y = x + F\big(\LayerNorm(x)\big), \qquad \text{output} = \LayerNorm\big(x^{(L)}\big) \]

GPT-2 made the same move ("layer normalization was moved to the input of each sub-block ... and an additional layer normalization was added after the final self-attention block") [Radford et al. 2019, §2.3], and Llama's architecture section lists "pre-normalization" first among its changes to the original design [Touvron et al. 2023, §2.2].

Why the placement changes optimization

Read the two equations through the lens of the previous section. In Pre-LN, the residual path is untouched: \(x^{(L)} = x^{(0)} + \sum_j F_j(\LayerNorm(\cdot))\), so the identity term of the gradient survives all \(L\) layers. In Post-LN, every layer's output passes through a LayerNorm, so backpropagating to layer \(l\) multiplies \(L - l\) normalization Jacobians, each of size \(O(\sqrt{d}/\|x\|)\) [Xiong et al. 2020, Lemma 3]. There is no clean path.

Xiong et al. make this quantitative with a mean-field analysis at initialization (a simplified model: single-head attention with zero-initialized query and key matrices, ReLU FFN, Gaussian inputs). For the weight \(W_{2,L}\) of the last FFN layer they prove, with high probability [Xiong et al. 2020, Thm. 1]:

\[ \Big\|\tfrac{\partial \tilde{\mathcal L}}{\partial W_{2,L}}\Big\|_F \le O\big(d\sqrt{\ln d}\big) \ \ \text{(Post-LN)}, \qquad \Big\|\tfrac{\partial \tilde{\mathcal L}}{\partial W_{2,L}}\Big\|_F \le O\Big(d\sqrt{\tfrac{\ln d}{L}}\Big) \ \ \text{(Pre-LN)} \]
Post-LN's bound does not shrink with depth; Pre-LN's shrinks as \(1/\sqrt{L}\)

Their extended analysis and measurements show the layer-wise picture: in Post-LN the expected gradient grows toward the output layers, while in Pre-LN it is roughly constant across layers [§3.3-3.4, Fig. 3]. The mechanism is the norm of the stream: at initialization the Post-LN state entering each LayerNorm has a depth-independent scale, whereas in Pre-LN the stream norm grows with depth (\((1 + l/2)\,d \le \E\|x_l\|^2 \le (1 + 3l/2)\,d\), Lemma 2), so the final LayerNorm divides the gradients by roughly \(\sqrt{L}\).

Large gradients on the parameters nearest the output mean that a large learning rate at step 0 is unsafe, which is the standard explanation for why Post-LN needs a learning-rate warm-up. The experiments agree:

  • IWSLT14 De-En with Adam: Post-LN without warm-up reaches BLEU 8.45, against about 34 with warm-up [Xiong et al. 2020, §3.2].
  • Pre-LN without warm-up reaches about 34 BLEU and a comparable validation loss on the same task, and on BERT pre-training the authors report a roughly 40% speed-up [§4].

What Pre-LN costs

  • The stream norm grows with depth. Later sublayers write into a larger and larger vector, so their relative contribution shrinks. Sun et al. (2025) find that many deep layers of Pre-LN LLMs (Llama 2, Qwen3, DeepSeek) can be pruned with small MMLU loss, and argue that the growing output variance pushes deep blocks toward identity maps [Sun et al. 2025, §2-3]. That explanation is one paper's, though the pruning observation itself is cited there from several earlier studies.
  • A final norm is mandatory, because the stream reaching the LM head is otherwise unnormalized and of depth-dependent scale.
  • It does not remove warm-up in practice. Llama 1 warms up for 2,000 steps [§2.3] and Llama 3 405B for 8,000 steps [§3.4.1]. Xiong et al. show warm-up can be dropped on their tasks, and note that the benefit of warm-up has also been attributed to variance in Adam's early adaptive learning rates [Related work].

Newer variants (page 11 has the full treatment)

Two recent open recipes keep the norm out of the residual path but add norms elsewhere. Gemma 2 applies RMSNorm to both the input and the output of every sublayer, \(x + \RMSNorm(F(\RMSNorm(x)))\) [Gemma Team 2024, §2, Table 1]. OLMo 2 drops the input norm and instead normalizes the sublayer outputs, \(h = x + \RMSNorm(\text{Attn}(x))\) and \(x' = h + \RMSNorm(\text{FFN}(h))\), together with QK-norm. In their ablation neither change helped alone, but together they cut the gradient-norm "spike score" from 0.108 to 0.069 [OLMo Team 2025, §2.1, §3.3.2]. Both are consistent with He et al.'s principle: the norm only rescales what a branch adds, and the bus stays clean.

LayerNorm versus RMSNorm

Normalization acts on the last axis of one token's vector \(x \in \R^d\) and never mixes tokens. LayerNorm 层归一化 [Ba et al. 2016] subtracts the mean, divides by the standard deviation, then applies a learned per-channel gain and bias:

\[ \LayerNorm(x) = \frac{x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}} \odot \gamma + \beta, \qquad \mu = \frac1d \sum_i x_i, \quad \sigma^2 = \frac1d \sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 \]

RMSNorm 均方根归一化 keeps only the rescaling [Zhang & Sennrich 2019, Eq. 4]:

\[ \RMSNorm(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{d}\sum_i x_i^2 + \epsilon}} \odot g \]
No mean subtraction, no bias. The gain \(g \in \R^d\) starts at 1. \(\epsilon\) is inside the square root.

Two things are dropped: the re-centering (subtracting \(\mu\)) and the bias \(\beta\). The norm therefore has \(d\) parameters instead of \(2d\). The two are closely related, since \(\sigma^2 = \tfrac1d\sum_i x_i^2 - \mu^2\): LayerNorm is RMSNorm applied to the centered vector, and when \(\mu = 0\) they coincide exactly [Zhang & Sennrich 2019, §4].

Why dropping the mean is nearly free

Zhang and Sennrich's hypothesis is that the useful property of LayerNorm is re-scaling invariance, not re-centering invariance. RMS is homogeneous, \(\text{RMS}(\alpha x) = \alpha\,\text{RMS}(x)\), so the normalized output does not change if the input, or the weight matrix that produced it, is rescaled (their Eq. 6-7). Subtracting the mean removes exactly one direction of \(\R^d\), the all-ones vector. RMSNorm keeps that direction, and a plausible reason this is harmless is that the linear layer that follows can learn to ignore it. That last step is my reasoning, not a theorem, and the evidence is empirical:

  • Translation. A Transformer-base on WMT14 En-De reached 26.8 / 27.7 BLEU (newstest2014 / 2017) with RMSNorm against 26.6 / 27.7 with LayerNorm, and trained 6.9% faster per step (231 s versus 248 s per 1k steps on a V100) [Zhang & Sennrich 2019, Table 4]. Across all their models and frameworks the speed-up ranged from 7% to 64%. It is small for Transformers because they have "significantly fewer sequential normalization operations" than RNNs.
  • LLM practice. Llama adopted RMSNorm [Touvron et al. 2023, §2.2]. PaLM used bias-free LayerNorm [Chowdhery et al. 2022, §2]. OLMo 2 switched from non-parametric LayerNorm to RMSNorm and reports that "ablations show no difference between the two" [OLMo Team 2025, §3.3.1]. Quality is not sensitive to this choice.
  • Where the speed comes from. A norm costs about \(5d\) to \(7d\) FLOPs per token, so two per layer are about \(10d\) against roughly \(26d^2\) for the matmuls of a block: at \(d = 4096\) that is 0.01% (my estimate). The saving is in memory traffic and kernel simplicity, since one fewer reduction means one fewer pass over the activations.

Details that interviewers check

Epsilon goes inside the square root. The reference code is x * torch.rsqrt(x.pow(2).mean(-1, keepdim=True) + eps) with \(\epsilon = 10^{-5}\) in the Llama 3 config [Llama 3 reference code]. This keeps the map smooth and finite at \(x = 0\) (the output is exactly 0, not NaN). It also matters numerically at initialization: token embeddings start with standard deviation 0.02, so the mean square is \(4 \times 10^{-4}\) and \(\epsilon = 10^{-5}\) is 2.5% of it, shrinking the output by 1.2%. Putting \(\epsilon\) outside the root (\(x/(\text{rms} + \epsilon)\)) gives visibly different values for small inputs.

Compute the statistics in float32. Squaring and averaging 4,096 bfloat16 values loses precision. The reference implementation upcasts, normalizes, casts back to the input dtype, and then multiplies by the gain [Llama 3 reference code].

import torch, torch.nn as nn

class RMSNorm(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, eps=1e-5):
        super().__init__()
        self.eps = eps
        self.weight = nn.Parameter(torch.ones(dim))        # gain g; no bias

    def forward(self, x):                                   # x: (..., dim)
        dtype = x.dtype
        x = x.float()                                       # statistics in fp32, even for bf16 input
        x = x * torch.rsqrt(x.pow(2).mean(-1, keepdim=True) + self.eps)   # eps INSIDE the sqrt
        return (self.weight.float() * x).to(dtype)          # Llama casts before the gain: same for bf16 weights

The feed-forward network: from ReLU MLP to SwiGLU

The original FFN is two linear maps with a ReLU between them, applied to each position independently, with a hidden width \(d_{ff} = 4d\) (2,048 for \(d = 512\)) [Vaswani et al. 2017, §3.3]:

\[ \text{FFN}(x) = \max(0,\, xW_1 + b_1)\,W_2 + b_2, \qquad W_1 \in \R^{d \times d_{ff}},\ W_2 \in \R^{d_{ff} \times d} \]

This has \(2 \cdot d \cdot 4d = 8d^2\) weights, and therefore \(16d^2\) forward FLOPs per token. It is also the largest block of parameters in the model, as the count in the parameter section will show.

Gated linear units and SwiGLU

A gated linear unit 门控线性单元 multiplies two linear projections of the input component-wise, one of which is first passed through an activation. Shazeer replaced the first linear layer and activation of the FFN with such a pair. With the SiLU (also called Swish\(_1\)) activation \(\text{SiLU}(z) = z\,\sigma(z)\), this is SwiGLU [Shazeer 2020, Eq. 6]:

\[ \text{FFN}_{\text{SwiGLU}}(x) = \big(\text{SiLU}(x W_1) \odot (x W_3)\big)\, W_2, \qquad W_1, W_3 \in \R^{d \times d_{ff}},\ W_2 \in \R^{d_{ff} \times d} \]
Three matrices. \(W_1\) is the gate, \(W_3\) the up projection, \(W_2\) the down projection (Llama's names). No biases.

Why \(d_{ff} \approx \tfrac{8}{3}d\)

A three-matrix FFN with the same \(d_{ff}\) as the two-matrix one has 50% more parameters and FLOPs. To compare fairly, Shazeer shrinks the hidden layer by a factor of \(\tfrac23\) "to keep the number of parameters and the amount of computation constant" (T5-base: \(d_{ff}\) from 3,072 to 2,048 at \(d = 768\)) [Shazeer 2020, §2-3.1]. In general:

\[ 2 \cdot d \cdot 4d = 3 \cdot d \cdot d_{ff} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d_{ff} = \tfrac{8}{3}\,d \approx 2.67\,d \]

Check with T5-base: \(3 \cdot 768 \cdot 2048 = 4{,}718{,}592 = 2 \cdot 768 \cdot 3072\). Llama 1 states this rule directly: "We use a dimension of \(\tfrac23 4d\) instead of \(4d\) as in PaLM" [Touvron et al. 2023, §2.2]. The PaLM comparison is instructive: PaLM used SwiGLU but kept \(d_{ff} = 4d\) [Chowdhery et al. 2022, §2, Table 1], so its MLP has \(3 \cdot 4d^2 = 12d^2\) weights per layer, 50% more than the classical MLP. Both choices exist in the wild, and they differ in how much of the budget goes to the MLP.

Llama's rounding, and what Llama 3 actually does

The Llama reference code computes the hidden width in three steps [Llama 3 reference code, FeedForward]: take \(\tfrac23\) of \(4d\) and truncate to an integer, optionally scale by a multiplier, then round up to a multiple of multiple_of.

def llama_ffn_dim(dim, multiple_of, ffn_dim_multiplier=None):
    hidden = int(2 * (4 * dim) / 3)                  # 8d/3, truncated (the reference code is passed 4*dim)
    if ffn_dim_multiplier is not None:
        hidden = int(ffn_dim_multiplier * hidden)
    return multiple_of * ((hidden + multiple_of - 1) // multiple_of)   # round UP to a multiple

llama_ffn_dim(4096, 1024, 1.3)   # 10922 -> 14198 -> 14336  (Llama-3-8B)

With Llama-3-8B's settings, multiple_of = 1024 and ffn_dim_multiplier = 1.3, the chain is \(16384 \to 10922 \to 14198 \to 14336 = 14 \times 1024\), which matches Table 3 of the Llama 3 paper [Llama Team 2024, Table 3]. Two lessons follow.

  • Llama 3 does not use \(\tfrac83 d\). Its FFN widths are 14,336, 28,672 and 53,248 for 8B, 70B and 405B, i.e. \(3.5d\), \(3.5d\) and \(3.25d\). The MLP has \(3 \cdot 3.5\,d^2 = 10.5\,d^2\) weights per layer: 31% more than the classical \(8d^2\) and 12.5% less than PaLM's \(12d^2\). The 8/3 rule is what you derive when asked to match a 4d MLP, not a law.
  • The rounding is for hardware. OLMo 2 states the same practice ("approximately \(\tfrac83 d\), but increased to the closest multiple of 128 (11,008 for our 7B model) to improve throughput") [OLMo Team 2025, §2.1]. The usual reasons are GEMM tile sizes and divisibility by the tensor-parallel degree (14,336 = \(2^{11} \cdot 7\)); the sources above give the practice and the throughput motive, not a breakdown.

A common intuition is that a data-dependent, per-feature gate gives each parameter more expressive power, but that is an intuition, not an established result. import torch.nn.functional as F

class SwiGLU(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, d_ff, bias=False):
        super().__init__()
        self.w1 = nn.Linear(d_model, d_ff, bias=bias)    # gate
        self.w3 = nn.Linear(d_model, d_ff, bias=bias)    # up
        self.w2 = nn.Linear(d_ff, d_model, bias=bias)    # down

    def forward(self, x):                                # x: (B, T, d)
        return self.w2(F.silu(self.w1(x)) * self.w3(x))  # (B, T, d_ff) gated, then back to (B, T, d)

Embeddings, the LM head, biases, dropout and initialization

Embedding, position table and LM head

The token embedding 词嵌入 \(E \in \R^{V \times d}\) turns each id into a row: a gather, with \(V \cdot d\) parameters and zero FLOPs. The LM head (unembedding) \(W_U \in \R^{d \times V}\) is a real matmul costing \(2dV\) FLOPs per token: 1.05 GFLOPs for Llama-3-8B, 7.0% of its forward FLOPs. The logits tensor is also the largest single activation of the model in fp32: for \(B = 1\), \(T = 8192\), \(V = 128{,}256\) it is \(8192 \times 128256 \times 4\) bytes = 3.9 GiB, and the cross-entropy loss must keep it for the backward pass [Korthikanti et al. 2022, §4.3].

Position enters in one of two ways. A learned absolute table \(P \in \R^{T_{\max} \times d}\) is added to the token embeddings (GPT-2: \(1024 \times 768 = 786{,}432\) parameters); Vaswani et al. found learned and sinusoidal positions nearly identical (25.7 versus 25.8 BLEU, Table 3 row E). Modern models use RoPE inside attention, which has no parameters (page 05), so their parameter counts have no position term. The drill's max_seq_len is the number of rows of the learned table, and 0 means none.

One bookkeeping detail: Llama 3's paper lists a vocabulary of 128,000, while the config has 128,256 rows. The difference is 256 reserved special tokens added by the reference tokenizer (num_reserved_special_tokens = 256) [Llama 3 tokenizer code]. Count parameters from the config, not from the paper's table.

From your jet tagger

Your inputs were continuous features per particle, mapped to width \(d\) by a linear layer, a matmul. An LLM's input is a lookup in a \(V \times d\) table, a gather that costs no FLOPs but holds 6.5% of Llama-3-8B's parameters. The output side is the mirror image: a classification head with 128,256 classes at every position, where yours had a handful of classes for the whole jet.

Weight tying

Weight tying 权重共享 sets \(W_U = E^\top\) so one \(V \times d\) matrix serves as input embedding and output classifier [Press & Wolf 2016]. Their argument is about gradients. In an untied model, the input embedding updates only the row of the current input token, so rare words are updated rarely; the output embedding updates every row at every step, because the softmax normalizes over the whole vocabulary. A tied matrix receives both updates and, in their words, "evolves in a more similar way to the output embedding" than to the input embedding (abstract and §3). Results (all 2016-era LSTM language models and NMT models): a large PTB LSTM went from 66M to 51M parameters with test perplexity falling from 78.4 to 74.3 (Table 5), and translation models lost 28% of their parameters (52% with three-way tying of encoder input, decoder input and decoder output) at similar BLEU (Table 8). The original Transformer adopted it, multiplying the tied weights by \(\sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}\) in the embedding layer [Vaswani et al. 2017, §3.4].

Why large models tend to untie. Labs rarely publish this ablation, so the following are the standard explanations, not measured results. (1) The saving shrinks with scale: tying Llama-3-8B would remove 525M parameters (6.5%) and zero FLOPs, since the LM head still multiplies. (2) The two matrices do different jobs. Input rows must keep the early residual stream well scaled (OLMo 2 found that weight decay shrinking the embeddings inflates early-layer gradients, because the Jacobian of a norm is inversely proportional to the input norm [§3.4.2]), while output rows act as class weights whose norm sets the logit scale. A tied matrix must satisfy both, and the patches in tied models show the tension: Vaswani multiplies embeddings by \(\sqrt{d}\), and PaLM initializes embeddings at \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) and scales the logits by \(1/\sqrt{d}\) [PaLM, §5]. (3) With pipeline parallelism the embedding sits on the first stage and the LM head on the last, so tying forces a gradient synchronization across stages. The counterexamples show it is a tendency: Gemma 2 27B and PaLM 540B tie, while Qwen2 and Qwen3 switch to untied at 7B and 8B respectively. A useful rule of thumb is that models tie when the embedding matrix is more than about a fifth of the parameters.

Biases

Llama 3's config sets attention_bias: false and mlp_bias: false, and RMSNorm has no bias. PaLM was explicit about why: "No biases were used in any of the dense kernels or layer norms. We found this to result in increased training stability for large models" [Chowdhery et al. 2022, §2]. OLMo excludes all bias terms as well [OLMo Team 2025, §2.1], and Shazeer's T5-style FFNs have none [§1].

The reason is not the parameter count. For Llama-3-8B, biases on every attention and FFN projection would add \(32 \times (4096 + 1024 + 1024 + 4096 + 2 \cdot 14336 + 4096) = 1{,}376{,}256\) parameters, 0.017% of the model. Two of the four attention biases are in fact mathematically redundant. A key bias adds \(q_t \cdot b_K\) to every score of query \(t\), the same constant for all keys \(j\), which the softmax cancels. A value bias adds \(\sum_j a_{tj}\, b_V = b_V\) to the attention output (the weights sum to 1), which the output projection can absorb into its own bias. Only the query bias and the MLP biases can change what the network computes. The practical case for dropping them is simplicity, one fewer set of tensors to shard and initialize, and PaLM's unquantified stability claim. Not everyone agrees: Qwen2 kept QKV biases [Qwen2, §2.2.1], and Qwen3 removed them again and added QK-Norm instead [Qwen3, §2].

Dropout

The original Transformer used dropout 0.1 on every sublayer output and on the embeddings, and its ablation shows why it was there: removing it dropped WMT14 En-De BLEU from 25.8 to 24.6 [Vaswani et al. 2017, §5.4, §6.2, Table 3 row D]. That setting is heavily multi-epoch: 100,000 steps of about 25,000 source tokens is about \(2.5 \times 10^9\) source tokens drawn from 4.5M sentence pairs (on the order of \(10^8\) tokens at typical sentence lengths), so each example is seen on the order of 20 times. LLM pre-training is different. PaLM "was trained without dropout, although dropout of 0.1 is used for finetuning in most cases" [Chowdhery et al. 2022, §5]; Shazeer's T5-style runs used "no dropout during pre-training" and "find this to produce superior results" [§3.2]; Llama's config has attention_dropout: 0.0. The reason is the data regime: with a trillion-token corpus, most tokens are seen once (Llama 1: "each token is used only once during training, with the exception of the Wikipedia and Books domains," which get about two epochs [§2.1]), so the training loss is an unbiased estimate of the validation loss. Dropout is a regularizer against overfitting, and a model that cannot overfit only pays for it in slower fitting. The caveat is data-constrained training, where repeated data returns overfitting risk (page 13).

Initialization

The default is all weight matrices and embeddings \(\sim \mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)\), biases zero, norm gains one. Llama's config carries initializer_range: 0.02, nanoGPT's _init_weights does the same, and OLMo 2 initializes every parameter from a truncated normal with standard deviation 0.02 [OLMo Team 2025, §3.2]. GPT-2 added a depth correction: "We scale the weights of residual layers at initialization by a factor of \(1/\sqrt{N}\) where \(N\) is the number of residual layers" [Radford et al. 2019, §2.3]. In nanoGPT this is implemented as std = 0.02 / math.sqrt(2 * n_layer) on each block's output projections (c_proj), because each block has two residual additions, so \(N = 2L\).

The reasoning is a variance budget. If each of the \(2L\) sublayer writes has variance \(\sigma^2\) and they are roughly independent, the stream variance after \(L\) blocks is about \(\sigma_{\text{emb}}^2 + 2L\sigma^2\), which grows linearly with depth (Xiong et al.'s Lemma 2 shows exactly this linear growth of \(\E\|x_l\|^2\) at standard initialization). Scaling each write's output projection by \(1/\sqrt{2L}\) makes the total \(O(1)\) regardless of depth. It is not settled practice. OLMo 2 removed depth-scaled initialization: plain \(\mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)\) everywhere cut the gradient-norm spike score from 0.40 to 0.03 in their setup and showed properties associated with hyperparameter transfer across widths [§3.2]. Note that their old baseline was more aggressive than GPT-2's: it scaled input projections by \(1/\sqrt{d}\) and output projections by \(1/\sqrt{2 \cdot d \cdot \text{layer\_idx}}\), so later layers started with smaller weights. That is evidence against strong depth-scaling, not proof that GPT-2's constant \(1/\sqrt{2L}\) is wrong. Width-aware rules (\(\mu\)P) are on page 14.

A free sanity check: with small random weights the logits are near zero, so the initial loss should be close to \(\ln V\). That is \(\ln 128{,}256 = 11.76\) nats for Llama 3 (\(\ln 50{,}257 = 10.82\) for GPT-2). A run that starts far above it usually has a scale bug, for example logits that are too large from a missing norm or a wrong tie.

The 12Ld² rule and why it is only a rule of thumb

FLOPs: the 6ND rule and what it leaves out

Forward 2N, backward 4N

Count a multiply-add as 2 FLOPs. A linear layer \(y = xW\) with \(W \in \R^{d_{in} \times d_{out}}\), applied to one token, does \(d_{in} d_{out}\) multiplications and as many additions: \(2\,d_{in} d_{out}\) FLOPs, which is 2 FLOPs per weight. Summed over every weight matrix, the forward pass costs \(\approx 2N\) FLOPs per token. The backward pass of \(Y = XW\) needs two matmuls, each the same size as the forward one: \(dX = dY\,W^\top\), to continue backpropagation, and \(dW = X^\top dY\), the weight gradient. So backward is twice the forward cost, and the total is three forward passes:

\[ \underbrace{2N}_{\text{forward}} + \underbrace{2N}_{dX = dY\,W^\top} + \underbrace{2N}_{dW = X^\top dY} = 6N \ \text{FLOPs per token} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C \approx 6\,N\,D \]
The factor 3 is one forward matmul plus two backward matmuls per weight matrix

This is Kaplan et al.'s accounting ("Accounting for the backwards pass (approximately twice the compute as the forwards pass), we then define the estimated non-embedding compute as \(C \approx 6N\) floating point operators per training token") [§2.1], and PaLM's ("6N matmul FLOPs per token seen (2N for forward and 4N for backward)") [Appendix B]. Only matmul weights produce FLOPs here. The embedding lookup costs nothing; norms, SiLU, softmax and RoPE cost \(O(d)\) per token per layer, about 0.01% for norms. If you use activation recomputation, the hardware executes an extra forward pass (about \(8N\) in total), but by PaLM's definition MFU counts only the \(6N\) of model FLOPs, so recomputation lowers hardware efficiency without changing MFU's numerator [§4.1].

The attention term, and when it matters

Weights do not account for the attention-score matmuls. For one token attending to \(T\) keys, \(QK^\top\) computes a length-\(d_h\) dot product per head per key, \(2\,n_h d_h T = 2dT\) FLOPs, and multiplying the weights by \(V\) costs the same. Over \(L\) layers:

\[ F_{\text{attn}} = 4LTd \ \ \text{(every token counted against all } T \text{ keys)} \qquad\text{or}\qquad 2LTd \ \ \text{(causal: the average token sees } T/2 \text{ keys)} \]

Different sources use different conventions for the same quantity. Kaplan writes \(C_{\text{fwd}} \approx 2N + 2\,n_{\text{layer}} n_{\text{ctx}} d_{\text{model}}\) (Eq. 2.2, the causal count). PaLM writes \(6N + 12LHQT\) per training token (App. B, the full count, \(3 \times 4LTd\)). Megatron-LM's per-token training count is \(72Lh^2 + 12Lsh + 6Vh\) [Korthikanti et al. 2022, App. A, Eq. 7]: the first term is \(6 \times 12Lh^2\), the second is the attention term, the third is the LM head (\(3 \times 2Vh\)). Setting attention equal to the linear layers gives the rule of thumb

\[ 4LTd = 2N \approx 24Ld^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; T \approx 6d \ \ \text{(full count)}, \qquad T \approx 12d \ \ \text{(causal count)} \]
Kaplan drops the attention term when \(d_{\text{model}} > n_{\text{ctx}}/12\), which is the causal form of the same inequality

For Llama-3-8B the exact numbers use the matmul weights \(N_{mm} = 32 \times 218{,}103{,}808 + 525{,}336{,}576 = 7{,}504{,}658{,}432\) (blocks plus LM head, no embedding table, no norms), so the linear layers cost \(2N_{mm} = 15{,}009{,}316{,}864\) FLOPs per token:

The honest answer is "somewhere between 20K and 60K tokens for an 8B model, depending on the counting convention." An 8K pre-training run therefore spends 12.5% to 22% of its forward FLOPs on attention scores, and a 128K run spends 70% to 82%. That is why Llama 3 trains at 8K for most of pre-training and adds context in stages at the end: "We do not train on long sequences earlier because the compute in self-attention layers grows quadratically in the sequence length," and for the 405B the long-context stage used about 800B tokens, roughly 5% of its 15.6T [Llama Team 2024, §3.4.2].

Three caveats. FLOPs are not time: attention kernels have lower arithmetic intensity than large GEMMs and run at lower utilization (page 16). Sliding-window and sparse attention change the \(T\) factor (page 09). And the causal count is what a FlashAttention-style kernel really executes, since it skips fully masked blocks.

A jet has at most a few hundred constituents, and \(T\) never approached \(6d\) for typical widths, so attention scores were a rounding error next to the MLPs and \(6N\) per example was a fine estimate. In a long-context LLM the sequence is 30 times longer than the model is wide, and the quadratic term is the main cost. The same block, in a different regime.

Which N? The embedding convention

For Llama-3-8B there are three defensible values of \(N\): the total, 8,030,261,248; the count that drops only the input table, 7,504,924,672; and the "non-embedding" count that drops both vocabulary matrices, 6,979,588,096. At \(D = 15\text{T}\), \(6ND\) is \(7.23\), \(6.75\) or \(6.28 \times 10^{23}\) FLOPs: a 7% to 13% spread. The FLOP-correct choice is the matmul weights (this count less the 266,240 norm gains), plus attention. Kaplan defines \(N\) as "the number of model parameters, excluding all vocabulary and positional embeddings" because the scaling laws are cleaner [§1.3, §3.2]; Chinchilla uses totals. Pearce and Song argue that much of the gap between the two papers' compute-optimal exponents (0.73 versus 0.50) comes from Kaplan's non-embedding convention at small scale [Pearce & Song 2024]. In an interview, say which \(N\) you are using. Page 13 returns to this.

Activation memory (preview of page 15)

Backpropagation needs what the forward pass computed. Per layer, the stored tensors are the inputs of each matmul, the norm inputs, the gated FFN intermediates, and (with naive attention) the attention probabilities. Korthikanti et al. count, for a GPT-style block with 16-bit activations, dropout masks included, 激活值

\[ \text{activation memory per layer} = s\,b\,h\left(34 + 5\,\frac{a\,s}{h}\right) \ \text{bytes} \]

with \(s\) the sequence length, \(b\) the micro-batch, \(h\) the hidden size and \(a\) the number of heads [Korthikanti et al. 2022, Eq. 1]. The first term scales with \(B \cdot T \cdot d\) per layer. The second is the attention matrices, which scale with \(T^2\) if the score matrix is materialized. With Llama-3-8B's shapes at \(b = 1\), \(s = 8192\): the factor is \(34 + 5 \cdot 32 \cdot 8192/4096 = 354\), so one layer stores \(354 \times 8192 \times 4096 = 11.9\) GB (11.1 GiB) and 32 layers store 354 GiB for a single sequence, 90% of it from the \(T^2\) term. A kernel that never stores the score matrix (FlashAttention, page 16) leaves the 34 term: 1.06 GiB per layer, 34 GiB in total. Full recomputation keeps only each layer's input, \(2sbh\) = 64 MiB per layer. Treat the constants as GPT-2-style: SwiGLU stores a wider intermediate, GQA stores smaller keys and values, and there is no dropout, so Llama's constant differs from 34. The scaling does not. ZeRO/FSDP, tensor and sequence parallelism, and recomputation policies are page 15.

A complete minimal GPT

This is the reference solution of the drill (about 100 lines with the RMSNorm and SwiGLU above). Positions use a learned absolute table here. Page 05 replaces it with RoPE inside attention, and page 06 adds the KV cache for generation: this forward pass is the training and prefill path. Attention uses F.scaled_dot_product_attention with is_causal=True (page 03), and KV heads are expanded with repeat_interleave (page 07 shows the mapping and the enable_gqa alternative).

import math, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class CausalSelfAttention(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, n_heads, n_kv_heads=None, head_dim=None, bias=False):
        super().__init__()
        self.n_heads = n_heads
        self.n_kv_heads = n_heads if n_kv_heads is None else n_kv_heads
        self.head_dim = d_model // n_heads if head_dim is None else head_dim
        self.wq = nn.Linear(d_model, n_heads * self.head_dim, bias=bias)
        self.wk = nn.Linear(d_model, self.n_kv_heads * self.head_dim, bias=bias)
        self.wv = nn.Linear(d_model, self.n_kv_heads * self.head_dim, bias=bias)
        self.wo = nn.Linear(n_heads * self.head_dim, d_model, bias=bias)

    def forward(self, x):                                                       # x: (B, T, d)
        B, T, _ = x.shape
        q = self.wq(x).view(B, T, self.n_heads, self.head_dim).transpose(1, 2)      # (B, n_h, T, d_h)
        k = self.wk(x).view(B, T, self.n_kv_heads, self.head_dim).transpose(1, 2)   # (B, n_kv, T, d_h)
        v = self.wv(x).view(B, T, self.n_kv_heads, self.head_dim).transpose(1, 2)
        g = self.n_heads // self.n_kv_heads
        k, v = k.repeat_interleave(g, dim=1), v.repeat_interleave(g, dim=1)         # (B, n_h, T, d_h)
        y = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=True)                 # (B, n_h, T, d_h)
        return self.wo(y.transpose(1, 2).reshape(B, T, self.n_heads * self.head_dim))

class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, cfg):
        super().__init__()
        d, eps, bias = cfg["d_model"], cfg.get("norm_eps", 1e-5), cfg.get("bias", False)
        self.attn_norm = RMSNorm(d, eps)
        self.attn = CausalSelfAttention(d, cfg["n_heads"], cfg.get("n_kv_heads"), cfg.get("head_dim"), bias)
        self.ffn_norm = RMSNorm(d, eps)
        self.ffn = SwiGLU(d, cfg["d_ff"], bias)

    def forward(self, x):
        x = x + self.attn(self.attn_norm(x))        # each sublayer ADDS to the residual stream
        return x + self.ffn(self.ffn_norm(x))

class GPT(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, cfg):
        super().__init__()
        d, V, L = cfg["d_model"], cfg["vocab_size"], cfg["n_layers"]
        self.tok_emb = nn.Embedding(V, d)
        max_len = cfg.get("max_seq_len", 0)                                     # learned positions; 0 = none (RoPE, page 05)
        self.pos_emb = nn.Embedding(max_len, d) if max_len > 0 else None
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList([Block(cfg) for _ in range(L)])
        self.final_norm = RMSNorm(d, cfg.get("norm_eps", 1e-5))
        self.lm_head = nn.Linear(d, V, bias=False)
        for m in self.modules():                                                # N(0, 0.02^2) everywhere, zero biases
            if isinstance(m, (nn.Linear, nn.Embedding)):
                nn.init.normal_(m.weight, mean=0.0, std=0.02)
                if getattr(m, "bias", None) is not None:
                    nn.init.zeros_(m.bias)
        for blk in self.blocks:                                                 # GPT-2 residual scaling, N = 2L writes
            for w in (blk.attn.wo.weight, blk.ffn.w2.weight):
                nn.init.normal_(w, mean=0.0, std=0.02 / math.sqrt(2 * L))
        if cfg.get("tie_embeddings", False):
            self.lm_head.weight = self.tok_emb.weight                           # one shared (V, d) Parameter

    def forward(self, idx, targets=None):                                       # idx: (B, T) int64
        B, T = idx.shape
        x = self.tok_emb(idx)                                                   # (B, T, d)
        if self.pos_emb is not None:
            x = x + self.pos_emb(torch.arange(T, device=idx.device))
        for blk in self.blocks:
            x = blk(x)
        logits = self.lm_head(self.final_norm(x))                               # (B, T, V)
        loss = None
        if targets is not None:
            loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.reshape(-1, logits.size(-1)), targets.reshape(-1))
        return logits, loss

def count_params(cfg):
    d, nh, V, L, dff = cfg["d_model"], cfg["n_heads"], cfg["vocab_size"], cfg["n_layers"], cfg["d_ff"]
    nkv, dh = cfg.get("n_kv_heads") or nh, cfg.get("head_dim") or d // nh
    attn = d * nh * dh + 2 * d * nkv * dh + nh * dh * d
    mlp = 3 * d * dff
    if cfg.get("bias", False):
        attn += nh * dh + 2 * nkv * dh + d
        mlp += 2 * dff + d
    emb = V * d * (1 if cfg.get("tie_embeddings", False) else 2)
    return emb + cfg.get("max_seq_len", 0) * d + L * (attn + mlp + 2 * d) + d

Common misconceptions

  • "Pre-norm means you can drop the warm-up." Xiong et al. showed warm-up can be removed on their translation and BERT tasks, and no more. Llama 1 warms up for 2,000 steps and Llama 3 405B for 8,000. The benefit of warm-up has also been attributed to variance in Adam's early adaptive learning rates, not only to the gradient scale at initialization.
  • "SwiGLU means \(d_{ff} = \tfrac83 d\)." That is the value that matches the parameters of a 4d two-matrix MLP. PaLM used SwiGLU with \(4d\), and Llama 3 uses \(3.5d\) (14,336 at \(d = 4096\)). Read \(d_{ff}\) from the config, never from a rule.
  • "6ND counts everything." It omits the attention-score matmuls (the term that grows with \(T\), 12% to 22% of forward FLOPs at 8K for Llama-3-8B and about 70% to 82% at 128K), and if you plug in the total parameter count it includes the input embedding table, which costs no FLOPs (the LM head matmul is real and does belong in \(N\)). Say which \(N\) and which \(T\) you assume.
  • "RMSNorm is faster because it does fewer FLOPs." Norm FLOPs are about 0.01% of a block either way. The saving is one fewer reduction pass over memory and half the norm parameters, and it measured at 7% in a Transformer-base, not 2x.
  • "Biases are dropped to save parameters." They are 0.017% of Llama-3-8B. The key bias is exactly redundant (it adds the same constant to every score, and softmax cancels it), the value bias folds into the output projection, and the case for dropping the rest is simplicity and PaLM's stability claim. Qwen2 kept QKV biases.
  • "Large models never tie embeddings." Gemma 2 27B and PaLM 540B tie. Ties are common when the embedding matrix is a fifth or more of the parameters (small models with 128K to 256K vocabularies) and rarer above roughly 8B, but it is a tendency.
  • "An 8B model has 8.00B parameters, and embeddings are negligible." Llama-3-8B has 8.03B, and 13.1% of that is the two vocabulary matrices. The \(12Ld^2\) shortcut undershoots its non-embedding count by 7.7% because GQA and the 3.5d SwiGLU change the coefficients.
  • "MFU is achieved FLOP/s over the H100's 1,979 TFLOP/s." 1,979 is the with-sparsity figure. Dense BF16 peak is about 989, and MFU counts model FLOPs (\(6N\) plus attention), not the recomputation a checkpointed run actually executes.

Interview questions

Warm-up Walk through a decoder-only forward pass with shapes. Where do the parameters live, and where do tokens interact?

Token ids \((B, T)\) index the embedding table, giving \((B, T, d)\) (plus a learned position table if there is one). Each of \(L\) blocks maps \((B, T, d) \to (B, T, d)\): \(h = x + \text{Attn}(\text{Norm}(x))\), then \(x' = h + \text{FFN}(\text{Norm}(h))\). A final norm and the LM head give logits \((B, T, V)\). For training, the targets are the input ids shifted left by one position, and the loss is cross-entropy averaged over \(B \cdot T\) positions.

Parameters: \(Vd\) in the embedding, about \(13d^2\) per layer in Llama-3-8B (attention \(2.5d^2\), FFN \(10.5d^2\)), and \(Vd\) in an untied head. Tokens exchange information only in attention. Embedding, FFN, norms and the head all act on each position separately.

Warm-up What loss should a freshly initialized LLM report at step 0, and what does a deviation tell you?

With \(\mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)\) weights and a final norm, the logits are small and nearly random, so the predicted distribution is close to uniform and the loss is close to \(\ln V\): 11.76 nats for \(V = 128{,}256\), 10.82 for GPT-2's 50,257. If the initial loss is far above \(\ln V\), the logits are too large: a missing final norm, a wrong tie, or a bad init scale. If it is far below and falls to near zero within a few steps, suspect leakage: the causal mask is missing or the targets were not shifted by one, so the model can copy the answer from its input.

Warm-up What is the residual stream, and why do residual connections make deep transformers trainable?

The residual stream is the \(d\)-dimensional vector each token carries through the network. Every sublayer reads a normalized copy and adds its output, so after \(L\) blocks the state is the embedding plus \(2L\) additive writes: \(x^{(L)} = x^{(0)} + \sum_j f_j(\cdot)\). Differentiating gives \(\partial x_L / \partial x_l = I + \partial(\sum F)/\partial x_l\), so a gradient term reaches every layer without passing through any weights, and it "does not vanish even when the weights are arbitrarily small" (He et al.). Anything multiplicative on the skip path, such as scaling, gating or dropout, breaks this, which is why Pre-LN keeps norms off the path.

Warm-up Why do modern LLMs drop linear biases, and why is there no dropout in pre-training?

Biases. Not for the parameter count: biases on every projection would add 1,376,256 parameters to Llama-3-8B, 0.017%. A key bias adds the same \(q_t \cdot b_K\) to every score of a query, which softmax cancels, and a value bias adds \(b_V\) to the attention output because the weights sum to 1, which \(W_o\) can absorb. PaLM reported "increased training stability for large models" without biases; the rest is simplicity. Qwen2 kept QKV biases and Qwen3 removed them.

Dropout. It fights overfitting, and with a trillion-token, roughly single-epoch corpus there is none: the training loss estimates the validation loss. PaLM used no dropout in pre-training (0.1 in fine-tuning), Shazeer found none superior, and Vaswani's dropout mattered (24.6 versus 25.8 BLEU without it) in a setup that makes on the order of 20 passes over 4.5M sentence pairs (my estimate). The exception is data-constrained, multi-epoch training.

Core Count the parameters of Llama-3-8B from its config.

Config: \(L = 32\), \(d = 4096\), \(n_h = 32\), \(n_{kv} = 8\), \(d_h = 128\) (so \(n_{kv}d_h = 1024\)), \(d_{ff} = 14{,}336\), \(V = 128{,}256\), untied, no biases.

Embedding: \(Vd = 525{,}336{,}576\), and the LM head is the same again. Per layer: \(W_q\) and \(W_o\) are \(4096^2 = 16{,}777{,}216\) each, \(W_k\) and \(W_v\) are \(4096 \times 1024 = 4{,}194{,}304\) each, so attention is 41,943,040. The FFN has three matrices of \(4096 \times 14{,}336 = 58{,}720{,}256\), so 176,160,768. Two RMSNorm gains add 8,192. Per layer: 218,112,000, and times 32: 6,979,584,000. Final norm: 4,096.

Total: \(2 \times 525{,}336{,}576 + 6{,}979{,}584{,}000 + 4{,}096 = 8{,}030{,}261{,}248\). Quick check: attention plus FFN is exactly \(13d^2\) per layer, so \(N \approx 13Ld^2 + 2Vd = 8.03 \times 10^9\). Things worth saying: the MLPs are 70.2% of the parameters, the two vocabulary matrices are 13.1%, and tying would give 7.505B.

Core Derive "training costs about 6N FLOPs per token." Where does the factor 3 come from?

A weight matrix \(W \in \R^{d_{in} \times d_{out}}\) applied to a token does \(d_{in}d_{out}\) multiply-adds, which is \(2\,d_{in}d_{out}\) FLOPs, so the forward pass is 2 FLOPs per weight: \(2N\) per token over all matmul weights. For \(Y = XW\) the backward pass computes \(dX = dY\,W^\top\), needed to keep backpropagating, and \(dW = X^\top dY\), the weight gradient. Each is the same size as the forward matmul, so backward is \(4N\). Forward plus backward is \(6N\), i.e. three matmuls of forward size per weight, hence the 3. Multiplying by \(D\) tokens gives \(C \approx 6ND\).

What it leaves out: the attention-score matmuls (\(\approx 4LTd\) forward per token, causal half of that), a few \(O(d)\) elementwise ops, and, if you use activation checkpointing, one extra forward pass (\(8N\) executed, but MFU counts \(6N\)). Embedding lookups cost nothing, so the right \(N\) is the matmul weights. For Llama-3-8B, \(6 \times 8.03 \times 10^9 \times 15 \times 10^{12} = 7.2 \times 10^{23}\). Sanity check: Llama 3 405B, \(6 \times 405\text{B} \times 15.6\text{T} = 3.79 \times 10^{25}\), matches the paper's \(3.8 \times 10^{25}\).

Core Why pre-norm instead of post-norm?

Post-LN, \(y = \text{LN}(x + F(x))\), puts a normalization on the residual path at every layer, so there is no clean identity route for gradients. Pre-LN, \(y = x + F(\text{LN}(x))\), leaves the path untouched. Xiong et al. show the consequence at initialization: the last-layer FFN weight gradient is \(O(d\sqrt{\ln d})\) for Post-LN, independent of depth, and \(O(d\sqrt{\ln d / L})\) for Pre-LN, and per-layer gradients grow toward the output in Post-LN but stay roughly equal across layers in Pre-LN. Large gradients near the output make a big early learning rate unsafe, hence Post-LN's warm-up dependence: on IWSLT14, Post-LN without warm-up reached 8.45 BLEU against about 34 with it, while Pre-LN without warm-up reached about 34. On BERT, Pre-LN was about 40% faster.

Costs: the stream norm grows with depth (linearly at init), so deep layers contribute less (Sun et al. find many deep layers of Pre-LN LLMs prunable), a final norm is mandatory, and warm-up is still used in practice. Variants (Gemma 2's post-norms, OLMo 2's output norms) add norms on the branch while keeping the bus clean.

Core RMSNorm versus LayerNorm: what is dropped, and why does it still work?

LayerNorm computes \((x - \mu)/\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon} \odot \gamma + \beta\). RMSNorm computes \(x / \sqrt{\text{mean}(x^2) + \epsilon} \odot g\). It drops the mean subtraction (re-centering) and the bias \(\beta\), so it has \(d\) parameters instead of \(2d\) and one fewer reduction. Since \(\sigma^2 = \text{mean}(x^2) - \mu^2\), LayerNorm equals RMSNorm of the centered vector, and they coincide when \(\mu = 0\).

It still works because the valuable property is re-scaling invariance, \(\text{RMS}(\alpha x) = \alpha\,\text{RMS}(x)\), which RMSNorm keeps, while re-centering removes only the all-ones direction, which the following linear layer can learn to ignore (my reasoning; the evidence is empirical). Zhang and Sennrich: 26.8 vs 26.6 BLEU on WMT14 with a 6.9% faster Transformer-base. OLMo 2 saw no difference against its prior norm. Implementation details: \(\epsilon\) goes inside the square root (Llama 3 uses \(10^{-5}\)), statistics are computed in float32, the result is cast back, then multiplied by the gain. The speed gain is memory traffic, not FLOPs (norms are about 0.01% of a block's FLOPs).

Core Why SwiGLU, and why is dff about 8/3 of d instead of 4d?

SwiGLU replaces \(\sigma(xW_1)W_2\) by \((\text{SiLU}(xW_1) \odot xW_3)W_2\). In Shazeer's compute-matched T5-base experiments it lowered held-out log-perplexity from 1.997 (ReLU) to 1.944 at 65K steps and from 1.677 to 1.636 at 524K, several times the run-to-run noise, and the activation-free bilinear variant already beat GELU, so the multiplicative gate is what helps. There is no established mechanism ("divine benevolence").

It has three matrices instead of two, so to keep parameters constant the width must satisfy \(3 \cdot d \cdot d_{ff} = 2 \cdot d \cdot 4d\), giving \(d_{ff} = \tfrac83 d\) (T5-base: 3072 to 2048). Llama 1 uses exactly this ("\(\tfrac23 4d\)"), rounded up to a hardware-friendly multiple (OLMo 2's 7B: 11,008). It is a matching rule, not a law: PaLM used SwiGLU with \(4d\) (12\(d^2\) MLP weights) and Llama 3 uses \(3.5d\) (14,336 = the reference code's \(\tfrac23 \cdot 4d\), times 1.3, rounded up to a multiple of 1024), i.e. 10.5\(d^2\), 31% more than the classical MLP.

Core Estimate how long it takes to train a 7B model on 2T tokens on 1,024 H100s at 40% MFU.

Compute: \(C = 6ND = 6 \times 7 \times 10^9 \times 2 \times 10^{12} = 8.4 \times 10^{22}\) FLOPs. Cluster throughput at 40% MFU with the dense BF16 peak of about 989 TFLOP/s: \(1024 \times 0.4 \times 989 \times 10^{12} = 4.05 \times 10^{17}\) FLOP/s. Time: \(8.4 \times 10^{22} / 4.05 \times 10^{17} = 2.07 \times 10^5\) s = 57.6 hours, about 2.4 days.

Sanity check in tokens: \(4.05 \times 10^{17} / (6 \times 7 \times 10^9) = 9.6\) million tokens/s, about 9.4K tokens/s per GPU, plausible for a 7B on H100s. What I would add: attention adds roughly 8% to 16% at 4K to 8K context for a 7B (\(2LTd\) against \(2N\)), so I would quote about 2.6 to 2.8 days; real runs lose more to restarts and slow nodes; and 40% is a large-cluster number, since Llama 3's 8B run implies about 16% from its model card.

Core At what sequence length do attention FLOPs rival the MLP FLOPs for an 8B model?

Per token, forward, one layer: attention scores plus the weighted sum cost \(4Td\) (each token against all \(T\) keys, \(2dT\) for \(QK^\top\) and \(2dT\) for \(AV\)), or \(2Td\) for causal attention where the average token sees \(T/2\) keys. The MLP costs \(2 \cdot 3\,d\,d_{ff}\). For Llama-3-8B (\(d = 4096\), \(d_{ff} = 14{,}336\)) equating them gives \(T = 1.5\,d_{ff} = 21{,}504\) (full count) or \(3\,d_{ff} = 43{,}008\) (causal count). Against all linear layers (\(2N_{mm} = 15.0\) GFLOPs) the crossover is \(T = N_{mm}/(2Ld) = 28{,}628\) (full) or 57,256 (causal); the generic rule is \(T \approx 6d\) or \(12d\).

So the honest answer is "roughly 20K to 60K tokens, depending on the convention". At 8K attention is 12.5% (causal) to 22% of forward FLOPs; at 128K it is 70% to 82%, which is why long context is trained only in a late stage. Caveat: FLOPs are not time, since attention kernels run at lower utilization than GEMMs.

Core Why do big models untie the input and output embeddings?

First, honesty: labs rarely publish the ablation, and it is a tendency, not a rule (Gemma 2 27B and PaLM 540B tie; Qwen2 unties at 7B and Qwen3 at 8B). The standard reasons: (1) tying saves \(Vd\) parameters and no FLOPs, and that saving shrinks with scale: 6.5% of Llama-3-8B (525M of 8.03B) and 4.3% of Gemma 2 27B, against 31% of GPT-2 small and 21% of Llama 3.2 1B, where it pays. (2) The two matrices do different jobs. Input rows must keep the early stream well scaled (OLMo 2 found that decaying the embeddings inflates early-layer gradients), while output rows are class weights whose norm sets the logit scale. A tied matrix serves both, and the patches in tied models show the strain: Vaswani's \(\sqrt{d}\) embedding multiplier, PaLM's \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) embeddings with logits scaled by \(1/\sqrt{d}\). (3) With pipeline parallelism, tying forces a cross-stage gradient synchronization (unverified here).

The case for tying, from Press and Wolf: the input matrix only updates rows of tokens present, the output matrix updates every row every step, so tied embeddings get the richer output-side gradient, and rare tokens benefit. Rule of thumb: tie when the embedding matrix is more than about a fifth of the parameters.

Deep-dive Design question: a colleague proposes doubling the vocabulary of a 1B-class model from 128,256 to 256,512 tokens. Quantify the cost and decide whether to tie.

Take Llama-3.2-1B's shape: \(L = 16\), \(d = 2048\), \(d_{ff} = 8192\), 32 heads with 8 KV heads of dimension 64. The non-embedding parameters are 973,146,112. One embedding matrix is \(128{,}256 \times 2048 = 262{,}668{,}288\) now and 525,336,576 after doubling.

  • Parameters, tied: 1,235,814,400 \(\to\) 1,498,482,688 (+21.3%), and the embedding share goes from 21.3% to 35.1%. Untied: 1,498,482,688 \(\to\) 2,023,819,264 (+35.1%). Tying saves 525M parameters, about 8.4 GB at roughly 16 bytes per parameter for mixed-precision Adam (page 15).
  • FLOPs: the LM head costs \(2dV\) per token whether tied or not. Forward FLOPs per token rise from 2.471 to 2.997 GFLOPs (+21.3%), and the head grows from 21.3% to 35.1% of them. The fp32 logits for \(B = 1\), \(T = 8192\) double from 4.2 GB to 8.4 GB.
  • Benefit: fewer tokens per document. Cost per character is cost per token divided by characters per token, so the doubling pays off in training FLOPs only if compression improves by more than 21% (for example from Llama 3's 3.94 characters per token to about 4.78). Going from 128K to 256K usually buys far less than that, apart from coverage of specific languages or scripts. Rarely seen rows also get few updates (Press and Wolf's argument).

Recommendation: keep 128K unless a target language needs the coverage, and if you go to 256K, tie, since embeddings would otherwise be over half the model. Also account for inference: a bigger vocabulary makes the per-token LM head and sampling heavier, though fewer tokens are generated (page 02 covers the tokenizer side).

Deep-dive State Xiong et al.'s gradient result, explain why large recipes still warm up, and say what the "curse of depth" adds.

At initialization (mean-field analysis, single head, zero-initialized \(W_Q, W_K\), ReLU FFN), the hidden state entering each LayerNorm in Post-LN has a depth-independent scale, so the gradient of the last FFN weight is \(O(d\sqrt{\ln d})\) whatever \(L\) is, and gradients grow toward the output. In Pre-LN the stream norm grows about linearly with depth (\((1 + l/2)d \le \E\|x_l\|^2 \le (1 + 3l/2)d\)), so the final LayerNorm divides gradients by about \(\sqrt{L}\), giving \(O(d\sqrt{\ln d / L})\), roughly equal across layers. Post-LN's near-output parameters therefore see gradients too large for the learning rate that is fine elsewhere, and warm-up shrinks the step early.

Large recipes still warm up (Llama 1: 2,000 steps, Llama 3 405B: 8,000) because gradient scale at step 0 is not the only reason: Adam's early second-moment estimates are noisy (Xiong et al. cite Liu et al.'s RAdam argument), and the theory's assumptions are simplified. The curse of depth is the same mechanism read at the other end: Pre-LN's growing stream variance makes each deep block's Jacobian close to the identity, so deep layers add little (pruning Llama 2, Qwen3 and DeepSeek layers costs little MMLU). Sun et al. propose LayerNorm Scaling, multiplying each LN output by \(1/\sqrt{\ell}\). That is one group's fix; Gemma 2 and OLMo 2 pursue stability with extra norms on the branch outputs instead.

Deep-dive Your run reports 52% MFU on H100s. List everything that could make that number wrong.

MFU \(= (\text{tokens/s per GPU} \times \text{model FLOPs per token}) / \text{peak}\). Each factor has traps.

  • Peak. Dense BF16 is about 989 TFLOP/s; 1,979 is the with-sparsity figure (and for FP8 the dense peak is 1,979, with 3,958 for sparsity). Using the wrong one is a factor of 2 either way. For reference, Llama 3 405B reports 430 TFLOP/s = 43% on 8,192 H100s and PaLM 46.2% on TPUs, so 52% on H100s deserves an audit (Megatron-LM reported 54.2% on A100s, so it is not impossible [Korthikanti et al. 2022, abstract]).
  • Numerator. Model FLOPs (\(6N\) plus attention), not hardware FLOPs. Counting recomputed forward passes inflates it: with full recomputation by \(8/6 = 1.33\), turning a real 39% into 52%. PaLM's 46.2% MFU corresponds to 57.8% hardware utilization for this reason.
  • Attention convention. Counting the full \(T \times T\) score matrix instead of the causal half overstates the numerator, and by a lot at long context (82% versus 70% attention share at 128K for an 8B model).
  • The parameter count. Using total \(N\) (including the input embedding table) rather than matmul weights adds 7% for Llama-3-8B and more for small models.
  • Tokens. Padding or masked tokens counted as useful, or packing that repeats tokens.

The check is to rebuild the number from first principles, as in the Llama 1 65B cross-check: 380 tokens/s/GPU \(\times\) \(6 \times 65.2\text{B}\) = 148.7 TFLOP/s = 47.6% of an A100's 312.

Deep-dive Derive why GPT-2 scales residual output projections by 1/√(2L). Is it still needed?

Each block adds two residual writes, so the stream is \(x^{(L)} = x^{(0)} + \sum_{j=1}^{2L} f_j\). If the writes are roughly independent with zero mean and variance \(\sigma_w^2\), then \(\text{Var}(x^{(L)}) \approx \sigma_e^2 + 2L\sigma_w^2\), which grows linearly in depth (Xiong et al.'s Lemma 2 shows the same linear growth of \(\E\|x_l\|^2\) at standard initialization). The output projection's weight standard deviation scales each write's std linearly, so multiplying it by \(1/\sqrt{2L}\) divides \(\sigma_w^2\) by \(2L\) and leaves the total \(O(1)\) independent of depth. GPT-2 writes it as \(1/\sqrt{N}\) with \(N\) the number of residual layers, and nanoGPT implements \(N = 2\cdot n_{\text{layer}}\).

Is it needed? It only shapes the stream at step 0. Sun et al. show that Pre-LN with scaled init still develops large deep-layer output variance during training (about 175 in the final layer of their 130M-parameter runs), because scaled init only acts at initialization. OLMo 2 removed depth-scaled init and got fewer gradient spikes (spike score 0.40 to 0.03), but its old baseline was a more aggressive per-layer scheme than GPT-2's, so the result does not settle GPT-2's constant. Practical answer: \(\mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)\) with a \(1/\sqrt{2L}\) output-projection scale is a safe default, \(\mu\)P (page 14) is the principled width-and-depth answer, and always verify with the \(\ln V\) initial loss and a depth-vs-gradient-norm plot.

Cheat sheet

  • Block (pre-norm): \(h = x + \text{Attn}(\RMSNorm(x))\), \(x' = h + \text{FFN}(\RMSNorm(h))\); after \(L\) blocks: final norm, then logits \(= \RMSNorm(x^{(L)})W_U\). Initial loss \(\approx \ln V\) (11.76 for \(V = 128{,}256\)).
  • Post-LN vs Pre-LN: \(\text{LN}(x + F(x))\) vs \(x + F(\text{LN}(x))\). Xiong Thm. 1: last-layer gradient \(O(d\sqrt{\ln d})\) vs \(O(d\sqrt{\ln d / L})\). Pre-LN: no norm on the residual path, stream norm grows with depth, needs a final norm. Warm-up is still used (Llama 1: 2,000 steps; Llama 3 405B: 8,000).
  • RMSNorm: \(x/\sqrt{\text{mean}(x^2) + \epsilon} \odot g\). Drops mean and bias. \(\epsilon\) inside the root, float32 statistics. 26.8 vs 26.6 BLEU, 6.9% faster (Transformer-base). Quality about equal.
  • SwiGLU: \((\text{SiLU}(xW_1) \odot xW_3)W_2\). \(d_{ff} = \tfrac83 d\) matches a 4d MLP. Llama-3-8B: 14,336 (\(3.5d\), from \(\tfrac23 \cdot 4d \times 1.3\), rounded up to a multiple of 1024); PaLM used \(4d\). Shazeer: 1.944 vs 1.997 log-ppl.
  • Parameters: \(N = Vd\,(\times 2\ \text{untied}) + L(2d\,n_h d_h + 2d\,n_{kv}d_h + 3d\,d_{ff} + 2d) + d\). Llama-3-8B \(= 8{,}030{,}261{,}248\): MLP 70.2%, attention 16.7%, embeddings 13.1%, per layer \(13d^2 + 2d\). GPT-2 small \(= 124{,}439{,}808\) (tied). Classic \(N \approx 12Ld^2\).
  • Tie when the embedding matrix is more than about 20% of the parameters (GPT-2 31%, Gemma 2 2B 22.6%, Llama 3.2 1B 21%); Llama 3 8B is untied (6.5% per matrix). Gemma 2 27B and PaLM tie anyway.
  • Small choices: no biases (key bias cancels in softmax, value bias folds into \(W_o\), PaLM stability); no dropout in single-epoch pre-training; init \(\mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)\), residual output projections scaled by \(1/\sqrt{2L}\) (GPT-2, nanoGPT; OLMo 2 dropped it).
  • FLOPs: forward \(2N_{mm}\), backward \(4N_{mm}\), \(C \approx 6ND\). Attention adds \(4LTd\) (full) or \(2LTd\) (causal) per token, forward. Crossover with linear layers at \(T \approx 6d\) or \(12d\): Llama-3-8B 28.6K / 57.3K (MLP alone: 21.5K / 43.0K). Attention share at 8K: 22% / 12.5%; at 128K: 82% / 70%.
  • Time: Llama-3-8B, 15T tokens: \(7.23 \times 10^{23}\) FLOPs; at 40% MFU on H100 (989 TFLOP/s dense) 507K GPU-hours = 20.6 days on 1,024 GPUs. A 7B on 2T tokens, 1,024 H100s, 40%: 57.6 hours.
  • Cross-checks: 405B \(6ND = 3.79 \times 10^{25}\) vs the paper's \(3.8 \times 10^{25}\); Llama 1 65B: 380 tok/s/GPU \(\Rightarrow\) 148.7 TFLOP/s = 47.6% of an A100; Llama 3 8B model card: 1.3M H100-hours \(\Rightarrow\) 15.6% implied MFU.
  • Activation memory per layer (GPT-style, 16-bit): \(sbh(34 + 5as/h)\) bytes. Llama-3-8B shapes at \(s = 8192\), \(b = 1\): factor 354, 11.1 GiB per layer with naive attention, 1.06 GiB without the \(T^2\) term.
