Mixture of Experts

P0 ~75 min Prereqs: 04 Transformer block, 07 KV-efficient attention

A mixture-of-experts layer replaces the transformer's single feed-forward network with many, and a router sends each token to only a few of them, so a model can store 671B parameters while spending the FLOPs of 37B. This page covers the router, the load-balancing problem that dominates MoE training (auxiliary losses, and DeepSeek's auxiliary-loss-free bias), fine-grained and shared experts, expert parallelism, and the serving costs that decide which design wins.

Why MoE: parameters for capacity, activated parameters for compute

In a dense transformer (page 04) every token passes through every weight. The FFN holds about 70% of Llama-3-8B's parameters (page 04), so it is the natural place to add capacity. A mixture of experts 混合专家 keeps attention dense and replaces the FFN sublayer by \(n_r\) routed experts (each an ordinary FFN) plus \(n_s\) always-on shared experts. A small router 路由 picks \(k\) routed experts per token. With \(x_t\) a row vector and \(g_{t,i}\) the gate (zero unless expert \(i\) was chosen):

\[ y_t = \sum_{j=1}^{n_s} \mathrm{FFN}^{(s)}_j(x_t) \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^{n_r} g_{t,i}\,\mathrm{FFN}^{(r)}_i(x_t), \qquad h_t = x_t + y_t \]

The decoupling is the whole point. Forward FLOPs of the FFN sublayer per token are \(2 \cdot 3\, d\, d_e\,(k + n_s)\) for SwiGLU experts of hidden width \(d_e\) (multiply-add counted as 2), independent of \(n_r\). Parameters, on the other hand, grow linearly in \(n_r\). Training compute stays \(C \approx 6\,N_{\text{act}}\,D\) with the activated parameters 激活参数 \(N_{\text{act}}\) in place of \(N\) (page 04).

Worked example. DeepSeek-V3 was pretrained on 14.8T tokens. Compute \(\approx 6 \cdot 37\text{B} \cdot 14.8\text{T} = 3.3 \times 10^{24}\) FLOPs. A dense 671B model on the same data would need \(6 \cdot 671\text{B} \cdot 14.8\text{T} = 6.0 \times 10^{25}\), which is 18× more. The rule ignores attention's \(T^2\) term and the MTP module, so treat it as a first estimate. V3's report gives the realized cost: 180K H800 GPU hours per trillion tokens [DeepSeek-V3, §4.4.2].

The evidence is old and consistent. Shazeer et al. (2017) got a 39% lower test perplexity than a compute-matched baseline with a 68B-parameter MoE layer (65,536 experts), while 131,072 experts degraded [Shazeer et al. 2017, §5.2]. Switch states the hypothesis: parameter count, independent of computation, is a separate scaling axis [Fedus et al. 2021, §2]. Qwen3's MoE bases match dense ones with about 1/5 of the activated parameters [Qwen3 report, base-model evaluation], and DeepSeekMoE 145B matched dense DeepSeek 67B with 28.5% of the computation [Dai et al. 2024, §7].

What you pay instead:

  • Memory: all experts stay resident. V3 needs about 0.67 TB for weights at one byte per parameter.
  • Communication: tokens travel to their experts, two all-to-alls per layer in the forward pass (below).
  • Small per-expert batches: each expert sees about \(T k / n_r\) tokens, which hurts arithmetic intensity, worst at decode (below).
  • Training difficulty: balancing, router instability, dropped tokens in classic designs. And sparse models overfit small fine-tuning sets more easily [Zoph et al. 2022, §4.1].

Four trends run through the table. (1) Many small experts: early systems used 8 to 64 full-size experts with \(k \in \{1,2\}\); since DeepSeekMoE, flagships use 128 to 384 smaller ones, mostly with \(k\) of 6 to 8 (gpt-oss uses 4; Llama 4 Maverick uses 1 routed plus a shared expert). The router and the MoE layer

From logits to gates

The router is one matrix, \(W_r \in \R^{d \times n_r}\). Token \(x_t\) gets logits \(z_t = x_t W_r\), then affinities \(s_t\) (softmax over all experts, or an independent sigmoid per expert), a selected set \(S_t\) (the \(k\) largest), and gates \(g_{t,i}\) for \(i \in S_t\). Models differ in three independent choices:

  • Renormalize over the selected experts or not. Mixtral and gpt-oss take the softmax over the \(k\) selected logits, \(g_i = e^{z_i} / \sum_{j \in S} e^{z_j}\) [Jiang et al. 2024, §2.1]. Switch and DeepSeek-V2 take a softmax over all experts and keep the raw values, so gates sum to less than 1 [DeepSeek-V2, §2.2.1]. Renormalizing the raw values over \(S\) cancels the partition function and gives exactly the top-\(k\)-then-softmax form, so these are one family.
  • Softmax or sigmoid affinities. DeepSeek-V3 uses \(s_i = \sigma(x w_i)\) (\(w_i\) the \(i\)-th column of \(W_r\), the paper's expert centroid \(e_i\)) and \(g_i = s_i / \sum_{j \in S} s_j\) [DeepSeek-V3, §2.1.2, Eq. 13-15]. Sigmoid scores do not compete, which suits bias-based balancing: Wang et al. found a sigmoid baseline beating a softmax one, and a bias is harder to tune under softmax because normalization couples every pair of experts [Wang et al. 2024, §4.1, App. C].
  • A constant scale. The released V3 code multiplies the normalized gates by 2.5 (route_scale), and the released V2 code multiplies its raw softmax gates by 16 (routed_scaling_factor): configuration constants that are not in the papers' equations.

Renormalizing keeps the output scale independent of the probability mass on unpicked experts. One trap: at \(k = 1\) renormalization makes the gate identically 1 and the router gets no gradient from the language-modeling loss. Switch keeps the raw probability \(p_i(x)\) as the gate for exactly this reason [Fedus et al. 2021, §2.1].

Shared experts, dispatch, and what gets a gradient

A shared expert 共享专家 is an FFN every token uses with gate 1. Several shared experts of width \(d_e\) equal one dense FFN of width \(n_s d_e\) (a gated FFN with concatenated hidden units is the sum of its parts), and V3's reference code implements them that way. The routed part is the one to implement: group the \((t, \text{slot})\) pairs by expert, run each expert on its own tokens, and scatter-add the gate-weighted outputs.

import torch

def topk_router(x, W_r, k, bias=None):
    # x: (T, d)   W_r: (d, n_r)   bias: (n_r,) or None.   V3 style: sigmoid, normalized over the chosen k
    s = (x @ W_r).sigmoid()                        # (T, n_r) affinities, independent per expert
    sel = s if bias is None else s + bias          # the bias steers WHICH experts win ...
    idx = sel.topk(k, dim=-1).indices              # (T, k) chosen experts (a discrete choice, no gradient)
    g = s.gather(1, idx)                           # ... but gates use the UNBIASED scores
    return idx, g / g.sum(-1, keepdim=True)        # (T, k) each; gates sum to 1

def moe_forward(x, W_r, bias, experts, shared, k):
    # x: (T, d); experts: n_r FFNs (d -> d); shared: always-on FFNs
    idx, g = topk_router(x, W_r, k, bias)
    y = torch.zeros_like(x)
    for e, ffn in enumerate(experts):
        tok, slot = torch.where(idx == e)          # tokens that chose expert e, and in which slot
        if tok.numel() == 0:
            continue
        out = ffn(x[tok])                          # (n_e, d): each expert sees only its own tokens
        y.index_add_(0, tok, out * g[tok, slot, None])   # scatter-add the gate-weighted output
    for ffn in shared:
        y = y + ffn(x)                             # shared experts: every token, gate 1
    return y, torch.bincount(idx.flatten(), minlength=len(experts))   # output, and load per expert (sums to T*k)

What gets a gradient. Selection is discrete, so nothing flows through the choice. The router learns only through the gate values of chosen experts (and balance losses), and unchosen experts get nothing from that token. Switch notes that Shazeer et al. conjectured \(k > 1\) was necessary for non-trivial router gradients, and shows \(k = 1\) works [Fedus et al. 2021, §2.1]. The layer also has the shrinking batch problem: with \(b\) tokens each expert sees about \(k b / n_r\) of them, inefficient unless batches are combined across devices (expert parallelism) [Shazeer et al. 2017, §3.1].

From your jet tagger: why not let experts pick tokens?

Expert Choice routing flips the assignment: each expert picks its top-\(T'\) tokens, so balance is perfect by construction [Zhou et al. 2022]. With all particles visible in an encoder that is fine. In an autoregressive model, whether token \(t\) is picked depends on scores of later tokens in the batch, which leaks the future. Wang et al. show that the assignment pattern can carry more than \(k \log_2\frac{1-R}{R}\) bits per token per layer (\(R = k/n_r\)), and saw an abnormal loss drop of about 10% when shrinking the selection chunk from 8,192 to 512 tokens [Wang et al. 2024, §5.2]. Any balancing scheme for a causal LM must itself be causal, for the same reason as the causal mask.

Load balancing: the heart of MoE training

Why imbalance hurts

Load balancing 负载均衡 asks that every expert receive a similar number of tokens. There are three separate reasons, and good answers keep them apart.

  1. Routing collapse 路由坍塌. The router converges to favoring a few experts, and this is self-reinforcing: favored experts train faster, so they are favored more [Shazeer et al. 2017, §4]. Without a balancing loss their 256-expert model had test perplexity 39.8 with the busiest expert at 17.8× the mean load, against 35.6 and 1.14× with both losses at weight 0.1 [Tab. 6]. Idle experts are parameters paid for and never used, and uneven usage shrinks the effective expert count even short of collapse.
  2. Stragglers. Under expert parallelism the step time is set by the most loaded device. Wang et al. define \(\text{MaxVio} = (\max_i \text{Load}_i - \overline{\text{Load}})/\overline{\text{Load}}\). A value of 0.72 means the hottest expert gets 1.72× the mean, so at one expert per device utilization would be \(1/1.72 = 58\%\) if that skew held every step. Their aux-loss baselines measured 0.72 (1B model) and 0.52 (3B), against 0.04 for their method [Wang et al. 2024, Tab. 2].
  3. Overflow. Overloaded experts overflow fixed buffers, which forces token dropping (below).

The auxiliary loss, derived

GShard wanted to minimize the mean square of the routed fraction \(c_e/S\), but a top-2 count is not differentiable, so it replaced one factor by the mean gate \(m_e\): \(\ell_{\text{aux}} = \frac{1}{E}\sum_e \frac{c_e}{S} m_e\) [Lepikhin et al. 2020, §2.2]. Switch and DeepSeek keep the same idea. For \(T\) tokens with top-\(k\) choices \(e_{t,1..k}\):

\[ f_i = \frac{1}{kT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{k} \mathbb{1}[e_{t,j} = i], \qquad P_i = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} p_{t,i}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha\, n_r \sum_{i=1}^{n_r} f_i P_i \]
f: fraction of assignments dispatched to expert i (not differentiable). P: mean router probability (differentiable)

DeepSeek folds \(n_r/(kT)\) into \(f_i\) (so uniform routing gives \(f_i = 1\)) and drops the explicit \(n_r\); the value is identical [DeepSeek-V2, Eq. 23-25]. The factor \(n_r\) keeps the loss at \(\alpha\) under uniform routing for any number of experts.

Where the gradient flows. \(f\) is a count and carries none. Through \(P\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}}{\partial p_{t,i}} = \frac{\alpha\, n_r\, f_i}{T} \]

The same push hits every token and is proportional to the expert's load: an overloaded expert's probability is pushed down on all tokens. The gradient continues through \(p_t = \softmax(x_t W_r)\) into \(W_r\) and into \(x_t\), that is, into every layer below. That is the "interference gradient": an objective unrelated to next-token prediction shapes the backbone.

Numbers. Take \(n_r = 4\), \(T = 4\) tokens with top-1 choices \((1,1,2,3)\), so \(f = (0.5, 0.25, 0.25, 0)\), and \(P = (0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1)\). Then \(n_r \sum f_i P_i = 4(0.20 + 0.075 + 0.05) = 1.3\), so \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = 1.3\alpha\). Perfect balance gives \(\alpha\), total collapse \(n_r \alpha = 4\alpha\). Each token's probability for expert 1 gets gradient \(4 \cdot 0.5\,\alpha/4 = 0.5\alpha\); expert 4 gets none.

Is uniform routing the minimizer? Switch says the loss "is minimized under a uniform distribution" [Fedus et al. 2021, §2.2]. That is exact when routing is decisive (\(f \approx P\)): \(n_r\sum f_i^2 \ge 1\) by Cauchy-Schwarz, with equality only for uniform \(f\). In general \(f\) and \(P\) are coupled only through the argmax, and a router can hedge. With \(n_r = 2\): 90% of tokens choose expert 1 with probabilities \((0.5, 0.5)\), 10% choose expert 2 with \((0, 1)\). Then \(f = (0.9, 0.1)\), \(P = (0.45, 0.55)\) and the loss is \(2(0.405 + 0.055)\alpha = 0.92\alpha < \alpha\). The gradient still lowers \(p_1\) until the argmax flips, so the loss is a surrogate, not an exact constraint (our derivation).

Capacity, dropped tokens, and dropless MoE

Static-shape systems (TPUs, batched matmuls on GPUs) give each expert a fixed buffer, the capacity \(C = \lceil \text{cf} \cdot T k / n_r \rceil\), where cf is the capacity factor 容量因子 (Switch's Eq. 3 is the \(k = 1\) case, tokens per batch over experts times cf [Fedus et al. 2021, Eq. 3]; GShard's top-2 capacity is \(2N/E\)). With \(T = 4096\), \(n_r = 64\), \(k = 2\), cf = 1.25, the average load is 128 and \(C = 160\). Assignments beyond capacity are dropped: the expert contributes nothing for that token, which still flows through the residual connection [Lepikhin et al. 2020, §2.2]. GShard handles all first choices before any second choice, so second choices drop first.

The factor trades padding waste against dropped tokens. With an adequate balance loss Switch typically dropped under 1% of tokens, and it did better on a speed-quality basis at low factors: Switch-Base at cf 1.0 scored \(-1.561\) negative log perplexity at 1,000 examples/s and reached the quality threshold in 62.8 hours, against \(-1.554\), 860 examples/s and 72.8 hours at cf 2.0 [Fedus et al. 2021, §2.2, Tab. 1]. Dropping still costs quality: MegaBlocks measured a validation-loss gain of 0.15 at cf 1 against 0.26 with no dropping [Gale et al. 2022, §3].

Dropless MoE fixes this in the kernel. MegaBlocks writes the expert computation as a block-sparse product whose diagonal blocks have variable numbers of rows (128×128 blocks), so nothing is dropped or padded, and trained 1.18× to 1.38× faster than the best-capacity-factor MoE [Gale et al. 2022, §6.1-6.2]. Grouped-GEMM kernels serve the same purpose today. V2 dropped at device level in training (cf 1.0 per device; about 10% of sequences were exempt so training resembles inference, which drops nothing) [DeepSeek-V2, §2.2.4]; V3 drops no tokens in training or inference [DeepSeek-V3, §2.1.2]. Capacity-based dropping also makes a token's output depend on its batch neighbors, a nuisance for serving.

Router stability: fp32 routers and the z-loss

The router exponentiates logits, and exponentials amplify rounding. ST-MoE's example: ten logits of 128 and one of 128.5; rounding 128.5 to 128 in bfloat16 moves the top probability from 0.142 to 0.091 (36%), and bfloat16 has 65,536× the roundoff error of float32 [Zoph et al. 2022, §3.4]. Two fixes, usually combined:

  • Compute the router in float32 (Switch's "selective precision"). In Switch's test bfloat16 diverged (\(-3.780\)) while the selective version matched float32 quality (\(-1.716\) vs \(-1.718\)) at bfloat16 speed (1,390 vs 1,160 examples/s for full float32) [Fedus et al. 2021, Tab. 2]. V3 keeps its MoE gating modules in BF16 or FP32 inside an FP8 framework [DeepSeek-V3, §3.3.1].
  • The router z-loss penalizes large logits: \(\mathcal{L}_z = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \big(\log\sum_j e^{z_{t,j}}\big)^2\), \(c_z = 0.001\). In ST-MoE's study the baseline was stable in 4 of 6 runs (quality \(-1.755\)); with z-loss 3 of 3 were stable and quality slightly better (\(-1.741\)); tight update clipping was also stable but ruined quality (\(-4.206\)) [Zoph et al. 2022, §3.3, Tab. 4]. DeepSeek-V2: three balance losses

Under expert parallelism, device load and communication matter more than per-expert load, so V2 adds two more losses of the same \(f \cdot P\) form [DeepSeek-V2, §2.2.3]. Partition the routed experts into \(D\) device groups \(\mathcal{E}_1, \dots, \mathcal{E}_D\), and let \(M\) be the device limit per token:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{Dev}} = \alpha_2 \sum_{i=1}^{D} f'_i P'_i,\quad f'_i = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_i|}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{E}_i} f_j,\; P'_i = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{E}_i} P_j; \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{Comm}} = \alpha_3 \sum_{i=1}^{D} f''_i P''_i,\quad f''_i = \frac{D}{MT}\sum_{t}\mathbb{1}[\text{token } t \text{ is sent to device } i] \]

The device loss balances compute across devices without over-constraining each device's experts (which hurts quality). The communication loss balances what each device receives, complementing the device limit, which bounds only what it sends. V2 used \(D = 8\), \(M = 3\), \(\alpha_1 = 0.003\), \(\alpha_2 = 0.05\), \(\alpha_3 = 0.02\).

Auxiliary-loss-free balancing (DeepSeek-V3)

Auxiliary-loss-free load balancing 无辅助损失负载均衡 takes the balance term out of the gradient and uses a controller instead [Wang et al. 2024]. Each routed expert gets a bias \(b_i\) (initially 0) added to its affinity only to decide the top-\(k\):

\[ g'_{i,t} = \begin{cases} s_{i,t}, & s_{i,t} + b_i \in \operatorname{TopK}\big(\{s_{j,t} + b_j\},\, k\big) \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \qquad g_{i,t} = \frac{g'_{i,t}}{\sum_{j} g'_{j,t}}, \qquad b_i \leftarrow b_i - \gamma\,\operatorname{sign}\!\big(c_i - \bar c\big) \]
c_i: tokens routed to expert i in the step; c̄: mean over experts. Gates use the un-biased score s. [DeepSeek-V3, Eq. 16 and §2.1.2]

After every step, measured over the whole batch, each overloaded expert's bias drops by \(\gamma\) and each underloaded expert's rises by \(\gamma\) [DeepSeek-V3, §2.1.2]. Wang et al.'s pseudocode is identical with \(e_i = \bar c - c_i\) and \(b_i \mathrel{+}= u\,\operatorname{sign}(e_i)\).

Why it works. (1) No balance term enters the loss, so no interference gradient reaches the router or backbone. (2) It is a feedback controller: \(b_i\) integrates the sign of expert \(i\)'s load error, so it settles (up to \(\pm\gamma\) dithering) where the expert's load equals the mean. (3) It is causal: the bias uses loads from previous steps, so nothing from the current sequence's future tokens enters routing [Wang et al. 2024, §3]. (4) The bias lives in score units (sigmoid scores are in \((0,1)\)), so with \(\gamma = 0.001\) a shift of 0.1 takes at least 100 consistent steps, and speed matters: \(u = 10^{-4}\) balanced slowly early, \(u = 10^{-2}\) fluctuated late, \(10^{-3}\) was best (perplexity 9.51, 9.51, 9.50). The sign rule matched or beat the proportional variant \(b_i \mathrel{+}= u\,e_i\), and additive beat multiplicative biases [Wang et al. 2024, §4.3, Tab. 3-4].

V3's recipe. \(\gamma = 0.001\) for the first 14.3T tokens and 0 for the last 500B (the biases freeze). A complementary sequence-wise loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bal}} = \alpha \sum_i f_i P_i\) with \(\alpha = 0.0001\), \(f_i\) in DeepSeek's normalization (uniform routing gives \(f_i = 1\)), and \(P_i\) the mean of the normalized scores \(s'_{i,t} = s_{i,t}/\sum_j s_{j,t}\), prevents extreme imbalance inside any single sequence. V3 also routes to at most \(M = 4\) nodes and drops no tokens [DeepSeek-V3, §2.1.2, §4.2]. In the released reference code the bias is a checkpoint parameter, added to the scores before top-\(k\) at inference too (inference/model.py, class Gate).

Which batch defines "balanced"? Micro-batch versus global-batch

Most open frameworks compute \(f_i\) inside each micro-batch and average. A micro-batch holds a handful of sequences, so the loss is nearly a sequence-level constraint: even a code-only sequence is pushed evenly across all experts, which discourages domain specialization. Qiu et al. synchronize the \(n_r\)-sized count vector \(f_i\) across parallel groups (under 3% extra latency), or accumulate it in a buffer, so balance is enforced over the global batch [Qiu et al. 2025, §3].

Key idea

Two ways to keep experts busy: a loss that rewards balance (gradient noise on the backbone, a dilemma over \(\alpha\)) or a controller that shifts selection thresholds (no gradient, but only aggregate balance). Both must be causal, and both work better when "balanced" is judged over a large, diverse batch instead of one sequence.

DeepSeekMoE: fine-grained and shared experts, and the V2 to V4 lineage

DeepSeekMoE starts from two diagnoses of conventional 8 to 16 expert MoE [Dai et al. 2024, §1]. Knowledge hybridity: the tokens sent to one expert cover very different kinds of knowledge, which one FFN must store together. Knowledge redundancy: tokens sent to different experts all need some common knowledge, so several experts learn copies. Each fix attacks one problem.

Fine-grained expert segmentation

Fine-grained expert segmentation 细粒度专家分割 splits each expert's hidden dimension into \(m\) pieces: \(N\) experts of width \(d_e\) become \(mN\) experts of width \(d_e/m\), and \(mK\) are activated instead of \(K\). Parameters and FLOPs are unchanged (\(mK \cdot d_e/m = K d_e\)); the number of expert combinations is not. With \(N = 16\), \(K = 2\) there are \(\binom{16}{2} = 120\) routing choices; splitting each expert in 4 gives \(\binom{64}{8} = 4{,}426{,}165{,}368\), \(3.7 \times 10^7\) times more [Dai et al. 2024, §3.1]. At V3's scale, \(\binom{256}{8} = 4.1 \times 10^{14}\). A token's FFN becomes a sum of 8 small pieces chosen from 256 rather than 2 large ones chosen from 16, a finer partition of what each expert stores.

It is not free: router cost grows with \(n_r\), GEMMs get smaller (lower arithmetic intensity, more all-to-all messages), and balancing gets harder. DeepSeekMoE 16B stopped at 64 routed experts because excessively small experts reduce efficiency [Dai et al. 2024, §5.1.2], and Krajewski et al. saw quality fall at extreme granularity once routing parameters exceeded the active expert parameters [Krajewski et al. 2024, §7].

Shared expert isolation

The second idea sets \(K_s\) experts aside as always-on shared experts that absorb common knowledge, so routed experts need not store it; \(K_s\) fewer routed experts are activated to keep FLOPs equal. In the 2B ablation, 1, 2 and 4 shared experts (of 64, total activated fixed) gave Pile loss 1.808, 1.806 and 1.811, so the exact ratio barely matters, and a shared-to-activated-routed ratio of 1:3 was kept when scaling up [Dai et al. 2024, §4.4]. V2 used 2 shared experts, V3 uses 1.

Expert parallelism, communication, and serving

Expert parallelism: the all-to-all

When experts do not fit on one device, shard them. Expert parallelism 专家并行 (EP) gives each device \(n_r/\text{EP}\) of the experts of every MoE layer, while attention and other layers run data-parallel [Lepikhin et al. 2020, §2.2]. One MoE layer in the forward pass:

  1. Each device routes its own tokens.
  2. Dispatch (all-to-all): every token is sent to the devices hosting its chosen experts.
  3. Each device runs its local experts on the tokens it received, one grouped GEMM per layer.
  4. Combine (all-to-all): outputs return to the tokens' home devices and are summed with the gates.

The backward pass mirrors it, so a training step has four all-to-alls per MoE layer. EP also cures the shrinking batch: each expert receives tokens from every device in the group [Shazeer et al. 2017, §3.1]. It is not tensor parallelism: no weight matrix is split, and the collective is an all-to-all, not an all-reduce. V3 trained without tensor parallelism (16-way pipeline, 64-way EP across 8 nodes, ZeRO-1) [DeepSeek-V3, §3.2]. More in page 15.

Volume per token. A token's hidden state (\(d\) elements) goes to each destination hosting at least one of its experts. V3 dispatches in FP8 (1 byte per element) and combines in BF16 (2 bytes) [DeepSeek-V3, §3.3.3], so one destination costs \(3d = 21{,}504\) bytes per layer at \(d = 7168\). A token-expert pair does three matmuls of size \(d \times d_e\), \(6\,d\,d_e = 88.1\) MFLOPs, so the ratio is \(2 d_e = 4{,}096\) FLOPs per byte. Communication hides entirely behind compute when the device's peak FLOP/s divided by its interconnect bandwidth is at most that ratio. The V4 report makes the same derivation for V4-Pro, \(2 \cdot 3072 = 6{,}144\) FLOPs/byte, so each GB/s of interconnect hides 6.1 TFLOP/s of compute [DeepSeek-V4, §3.1]. Before overlap, V3's cross-node EP had a computation-to-communication ratio of roughly 1:1, which motivated DualPipe (page 27) [DeepSeek-V3, §3.2.1].

Why limit the number of nodes per token?

Inside a node GPUs talk over NVLink (160 GB/s on DeepSeek's H800 cluster); across nodes they use InfiniBand (50 GB/s), 3.2× slower [DeepSeek-V3, §3.2.2]. With top-8 over fine-grained experts, a token's experts could sit on 8 different nodes. Node-limited routing (V2: device-limited) restricts each token to at most \(M\) nodes. V3's version: the 256 routed experts form 8 groups (one per node during training, 32 experts each); each group's score is the sum of its top \(K_r/M = 2\) (biased) affinities; keep the best \(M = 4\) groups; take the top-8 experts among them [DeepSeek-V3, §2.1.2, §4.2]. The reference code does exactly this, with n_expert_groups = 8 and n_limited_groups = 4 set in its 671B config (with the bias present, the group score is the sum of the top 2 biased scores). V2's device-limited version was simpler: the paper picks the \(M = 3\) devices holding the highest-affinity experts and takes the top-\(K\) among them [DeepSeek-V2, §2.2.2], and the released V2 code scores each device group by its single best expert (a max, not V3's sum of the top 2).

The kernels send one copy of the token over IB to the GPU with the same in-node index on each target node, and NVLink forwards it to the GPUs hosting the chosen experts. Extra experts on an already-reached node are therefore nearly free: a token can use 3.2 experts per node without extra NVLink overhead, so V3 could raise its 8 routed experts to 13 (\(4 \times 3.2\)) at the same communication cost. Combine reverses the path, accumulating on NVLink before returning over IB. All of it takes 20 of an H800's 132 SMs [DeepSeek-V3, §3.2.2].

Fine-grained top-8 routing can send a token to 8 nodes; IB traffic scales with the number of destination nodes and IB is 3.2× slower than NVLink.
Idea

Why it works
IB traffic per token is capped at \(M\) copies, and NVLink forwarding makes extra experts per reached node almost free. The router learns under the constraint.
Evidence

Cost

DeepSeek-V2 (devices), V3 (nodes). Kimi K2 uses no grouping and a smaller EP=16 [Kimi K2, §2.3-2.4], and V4 removes the limit.

Overlap, kernels, and layout

Communication is hidden by overlapping it with compute. The shared expert is a natural filler: V2 overlaps it with the EP all-to-all [DeepSeek-V2, §3.1.3]. V3's DualPipe overlaps forward and backward chunks so all-to-all and pipeline communication are hidden [DeepSeek-V3, §3.2.1] (details in page 27). Kimi K2 finds DualPipe's doubled parameter memory too costly at 1T parameters and uses interleaved 1F1B plus the smallest feasible EP (16), which also relaxes balance constraints [Kimi K2, §2.4.2]. V4 uses one fused kernel that splits experts into waves, so computing the current wave, transferring the next, and sending finished results run concurrently. Its profiling found communication time below computation time within an MoE layer, with 1.50 to 1.73× speedups over non-fused baselines (up to 1.96× for latency-sensitive cases such as RL rollouts); the CUDA implementation, MegaMoE, is open-sourced in DeepGEMM [DeepSeek-V4, §3.1]. Kernel details belong to page 17.

Serving an MoE: memory first, then batch size

Memory. All experts must be resident. V3 at one byte per parameter is about 671 GB, at least 9 GPUs of 80 GB for weights alone (671/80 = 8.4). Mixtral 8x7B computes with 12.9B parameters but holds 46.7B: 93.4 GB in bf16, more than one 80 GB GPU. FP8 and FP4 experts (V4) attack this directly.

Batch size and experts. With \(b\) tokens in flight and independent uniform routing, the expected fraction of routed experts touched in a layer is \(1 - (1 - k/n_r)^b\), and each expert receives \(b\,k/n_r\) tokens. For V3 (\(k/n_r = 1/32\)):

An expert needs about 295 tokens, so V3 needs \(295 \cdot 256/8 = 9{,}440\) tokens in flight, 32× more than a dense model. V3 reports that decode batches per expert are usually within 256 tokens, where memory access, not computation, is the bottleneck [DeepSeek-V3, §3.4.2].

DeepSeek's serving layout follows from this [DeepSeek-V3, §3.4]. Prefill: 32 GPUs, attention with TP4, sequence parallelism and DP8, and EP32 for the MoE layers, so each GPU hosts 8 routed experts plus one redundant expert. Decode: 320 GPUs, attention TP4 with SP and DP80, EP320 with one routed expert per GPU, and 64 GPUs holding redundant and shared experts. The shared expert is treated as an always-selected routed expert (9 per token). Hot experts are found from online load statistics and re-placed periodically (every 10 minutes in the prefill example), V3's answer to inference-time domain shift that batch-wise balancing cannot prevent.

Pitfall

"Activated parameters" measure compute, not memory or bandwidth. Comparing a 37B-activated MoE with a 37B dense model on cost per token is fair for FLOPs and unfair for everything that scales with resident weights, including how many GPUs you must buy before serving the first request.

Scaling behavior and design choices

What the scaling studies say

  • Clark et al. 2022 (routed models up to 200B parameters, all trained on 130B tokens): validation loss is bilinear in \(\log N\) (dense size) and a saturating \(\log E\) (experts), so adding experts helps at every size tried, with returns that shrink as \(N\) grows. They define an effective parameter count, the dense size a routed model matches (a 110M base with 32 experts behaves like a 370M dense model), and fit a cutoff size beyond which routing stops helping: 937B for the best method (S-BASE), about 85B for the others [Clark et al. 2022, §4-5, App. E].
  • Krajewski et al. 2024 add granularity and let the token count \(D\) vary. Their fit over 100+ runs (129M to 3.7B parameters, 16B to 130B tokens, 64 experts) is \(L(N,D,G) = c + \big(g/G^{\gamma} + a\big)N^{-\alpha} + b D^{-\beta}\) with \(c = 0.47\), \(g = 2.1\), \(\gamma = 0.58\), \(a = 18.1\), \(\alpha = 0.115\), \(b = 30.8\), \(\beta = 0.147\). At a compute-optimal budget of \(10^{20}\) FLOPs an MoE matches a dense model trained with 20× more compute, and the factor exceeds 40× beyond \(10^{25}\) FLOPs (an extrapolation far beyond the fitted range). Optimal granularity grows with budget (16 at 64×1B, 32 at 64×7B, 64 at 64×300B). They attribute the disagreement with Clark et al. to fixed token counts, which under-train large MoEs [Krajewski et al. 2024, §5-6, Tab. 1-2].
  • Kimi K2's sparsity law (Muon-trained MoEs, activated parameters fixed at 8 routed plus 1 shared experts): more total experts lower loss at constant FLOPs. To reach validation loss 1.5, sparsity 48 needs 1.69×, 1.39× and 1.15× fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8, 16 and 32. K2 picks 384 experts (sparsity 48), noting the price is infrastructure complexity, and halves attention heads to 64 to offset inference cost [Kimi K2, §2.3].

All three measure loss at fixed FLOPs and none charges for the memory and communication of extra experts, which is why what labs ship (sparsity 16 to 64) sits far below what FLOP-only laws would suggest. More in page 13.

V4 uses hash-routed MoE there, Qwen3 and gpt-oss use MoE everywhere, and Llama 4 Maverick alternates dense and MoE layers, which halves the MoE parameters at the same expert count.

Sparse upcycling

Sparse upcycling initializes an MoE from a trained dense checkpoint: copy every parameter, make each expert a copy of the dense FFN, and add a randomly initialized router. Komatsuzaki et al. upcycled T5 and ViT (half the MLPs became 32-expert MoE layers) and beat both dense continuation and MoE-from-scratch with about 50% of the original dense compute; a scratch MoE needed about 120% to catch up. Upcycling is the more efficient use of a limited budget, while a very large budget may favor training from scratch [Komatsuzaki et al. 2022, §3-4]. Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B was built from Qwen-1.8B (14.3B total, 2.7B active), with 4 shared plus 60 routed experts (4 routed active), and its authors report that introducing randomness at initialization significantly expedited convergence [Qwen blog]. The blog gives no mechanism, and Komatsuzaki et al. found that small noise on copied experts had little effect and random expert re-initialization was worse at limited budgets, so treat the Qwen result as unexplained.

Common misconceptions

  • "Experts specialize by topic: a math expert, a code expert." Mixtral's routing analysis found no obvious topic patterns; expert choice followed syntax and token identity. Domain-level specialization appears when balance is enforced over a large, diverse batch (Qiu et al.; V3's bias method), so it depends on training choices.
  • "An MoE with 37B activated parameters costs like a 37B model." FLOPs per token, yes. Memory, bandwidth, communication and the minimum GPU count follow the total (671B for V3).
  • "Top-k routing is differentiable." The choice is discrete. The router learns only from the gate values of the chosen experts and from balance losses. With \(k = 1\) and renormalized gates the gate is always 1 and the router gets no task gradient.
  • "The balance loss is exactly minimized at balance." Its value at perfect balance is \(\alpha\), not 0, and a hedging router can push it below \(\alpha\) (the 0.92\(\alpha\) example). It is a surrogate.
  • "Auxiliary-loss-free means V3 has no balancing loss." V3 keeps a sequence-wise loss with \(\alpha = 10^{-4}\). The bias is not learned by gradient descent, changes only which experts are selected, and balances in aggregate over a batch, not inside each sequence.
  • "Dropped tokens are lost." A dropped assignment skips that expert but the token still travels through the residual connection. Dropping still costs quality (MegaBlocks: gain 0.15 at cf 1 versus 0.26 dropless), and V3 drops nothing.
  • "Top-8 in V3 means 8 experts per token" (it is 8 routed plus 1 shared), and "limiting nodes per token is a quality trick" (it is a communication bound; quality is protected by training the router under the constraint).

Interview questions

Warm-up Why does MoE let you scale parameters without scaling FLOPs? What does it cost instead?

Per token, the FFN sublayer costs \(2 \cdot 3\,d\,d_e\,(k + n_s)\) FLOPs: it depends on the experts a token uses, not on how many exist. Adding routed experts adds parameters (capacity to store knowledge) at almost no FLOP cost, so training compute is \(\approx 6 N_{\text{act}} D\) rather than \(6ND\). DeepSeek-V3 has 671B parameters but 37B activated (5.5%), and its \(3.3 \times 10^{24}\) training FLOPs are 18× below a dense 671B model's.

The costs: (1) memory, since all experts must be resident (about 0.67 TB in FP8 for V3); (2) communication, two all-to-alls per layer in the forward pass under expert parallelism; (3) small per-expert batches, so low arithmetic intensity, especially at decode; (4) training complexity: load balancing, router instability, capacity handling; (5) worse parameter efficiency per byte than dense, and more overfitting when fine-tuning on small data.

Warm-up Compute Mixtral 8x7B's total and activated parameters from its config (\(d = 4096\), 32 layers, expert FFN width 14336, 8 experts, top-2, 32 query and 8 KV heads of dim 128, vocabulary 32,000, untied).

Expert: \(3 \cdot 4096 \cdot 14336 = 176.16\)M. Attention per layer: \(2 \cdot 4096 \cdot 4096 + 2 \cdot 4096 \cdot 1024 = 41.94\)M. Embedding plus head: \(2 \cdot 32000 \cdot 4096 = 0.26\)B. Router: about 1M in total.

Total \(= 32(8 \cdot 176.16 + 41.94)\text{M} + 0.26\text{B} = 45.10 + 1.34 + 0.26 = 46.7\)B (reported 47B). Activated (\(k = 2\)) \(= 32(2 \cdot 176.16 + 41.94)\text{M} + 0.26\text{B} = 12.9\)B (13B). Memory in bf16 is 93 GB, more than one 80 GB GPU. "8x7B" is loose: attention and embeddings are shared, so it is not 8 × 7.24B.

Warm-up What is routing collapse and how do you prevent it?

The router converges to sending most tokens to a few experts. It is self-reinforcing: favored experts train more, improve, and score higher. Consequences are dead experts (wasted parameters), overloaded experts (dropped tokens) and, under expert parallelism, a straggler device that sets the step time. Shazeer et al. measured perplexity 39.8 with a max-to-mean expert load of 17.8× without balancing, against 35.6 and 1.14× with importance and load losses.

Prevention, usually combined: near-uniform router initialization; an auxiliary balance loss (\(\alpha \approx 0.01\) in Switch); V3's bias-based aux-loss-free balancing; capacity limits or dropless kernels so overload is handled safely; balancing over large, diverse batches. Monitor the per-expert load histogram, MaxVio and router entropy.

Warm-up What is the capacity factor, and what happens to dropped tokens?

Static-shape implementations give each expert a buffer of \(C = \lceil \text{cf} \cdot T k / n_r \rceil\) slots. With \(T = 4096\), \(k = 2\), \(n_r = 64\), cf = 1.25: average load 128, capacity 160. A token whose expert is full is dropped for that assignment: the expert contributes zero and the token flows on through the residual. GShard processes all first choices before second choices, so second choices are dropped first.

The factor trades padding waste (too high) against quality loss from drops (too low). Switch typically dropped under 1% of tokens with an adequate balance loss, yet dropping still hurts (MegaBlocks: 0.15 loss gain at cf 1 vs 0.26 dropless). Alternatives: dropless kernels (block-sparse or grouped GEMM), or, as in V3, balancing good enough that nothing is dropped.

Core Derive the Switch load-balancing loss and explain where its gradient flows.

We want each expert to receive a \(1/n_r\) share. The fraction of assignments \(f_i\) is a count (not differentiable); the mean router probability \(P_i = \frac1T\sum_t p_{t,i}\) is differentiable. GShard's idea: to reduce \(\sum f_i^2\), replace one factor by \(P_i\), giving \(\sum f_i P_i\). Multiply by \(n_r\) so uniform routing gives \(n_r \cdot n_r \cdot \frac{1}{n_r^2} = 1\) regardless of \(n_r\): \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \alpha\, n_r \sum_i f_i P_i\), with \(\alpha = 0.01\).

Gradient: \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial p_{t,i} = \alpha n_r f_i / T\). Each token's probability for expert \(i\) is pushed down in proportion to that expert's load, identically for all tokens. It then flows through the softmax into \(W_r\) and into \(x_t\), i.e. into all layers below (the interference gradient). Nothing flows through \(f\). Numbers: \(f = (.5,.25,.25,0)\), \(P = (.4,.3,.2,.1)\), \(n_r = 4\) gives \(1.3\alpha\), against \(\alpha\) at uniform and \(4\alpha\) at collapse.

Core Explain DeepSeek-V3's auxiliary-loss-free balancing. Why is it better than an auxiliary loss?

Each routed expert has a bias \(b_i\) (init 0). Routing selects the top-\(k\) of \(s_{i,t} + b_i\), but the gate is \(s_{i,t}\) normalized over the chosen experts, so the bias never scales an output. After each step, over the whole batch: if expert \(i\) got more than the mean load, \(b_i \leftarrow b_i - \gamma\); if less, \(b_i \leftarrow b_i + \gamma\) (\(\gamma = 0.001\) for the first 14.3T tokens, then 0). A tiny sequence-wise loss (\(\alpha = 10^{-4}\)) guards against extreme imbalance within one sequence.

Why better: an aux loss adds a gradient that reaches the router and backbone and competes with next-token prediction; a small \(\alpha\) collapses, a large one hurts perplexity. The bias adds no gradient, so outputs and learning signal are unperturbed, and the controller is causal (it uses previous steps' loads). Evidence: Wang et al. (1B) perplexity 9.50 vs 9.56 with MaxVio 0.04 vs 0.72; V3's ablations favor it on most benchmarks (large-model MMLU is 1.1 lower). Caveats: single-lab ablations, and a batch-wise aux loss matched it (2.253 vs 2.253 at 1B), so part of the gain is the batch-wise scope.

Core Why fine-grained experts plus shared experts?

Two problems of conventional 8 to 16 expert MoE: hybridity (each expert stores very different knowledge) and redundancy (common knowledge is duplicated). Fine-grained segmentation splits each expert into \(m\) parts and activates \(m\) times more, keeping parameters and FLOPs equal: \(\binom{16}{2} = 120\) routing choices become \(\binom{64}{8} = 4.4 \times 10^9\), so each token assembles a more precise combination and experts specialize more narrowly. Shared experts are always on, absorb common knowledge, and let routed experts be specialists.

Evidence (2B, 100B tokens): DeepSeekMoE Pile loss 1.808 vs GShard 1.867 at equal parameters and activated parameters, and equal to GShard with 1.5× expert parameters; disabling the shared expert raises loss to 2.414; the shared-to-routed ratio barely matters (1.808, 1.806, 1.811 for 1, 2, 4 shared). Costs: router and communication that scale with expert count, smaller GEMMs, harder balancing. Shared experts are contested at scale (Qwen3 drops them).

Core Walk through expert parallelism communication for one MoE layer. Why limit the number of nodes per token?

Each device holds \(n_r/\text{EP}\) experts. Forward: (1) route local tokens; (2) dispatch all-to-all sends each token's hidden state to the devices hosting its experts (V3: FP8, \(d\) bytes per destination); (3) local experts run on their received tokens (grouped GEMM); (4) combine all-to-all returns outputs (BF16, \(2d\) bytes), summed with the gates. Backward doubles this: four all-to-alls per layer per step. Volume is about \(3d\) bytes per destination against \(6\,d\,d_e\) FLOPs per token-expert pair, i.e. \(2 d_e = 4096\) FLOPs/byte for V3.

Cross-node InfiniBand (50 GB/s) is 3.2× slower than NVLink (160 GB/s), and traffic grows with the number of destination nodes. V3 caps it at \(M = 4\) nodes: score nodes by the sum of their top-2 affinities, keep the best 4, take the top-8 within them. A token crosses IB once per node and NVLink fans out, so 8 experts cost at most 4 IB copies (unrestricted uniform routing would touch 5.3 nodes on average). V2 limited devices (\(M = 3\)) and added device and communication balance losses; Kimi K2 dropped the grouping and V4 the limit.

Core Serving an MoE: why is memory the constraint, and how does batch size interact with experts?

Any token can pick any expert, so all experts must be in memory: V3 is about 671 GB in FP8, at least 9 GPUs of 80 GB before any KV cache, though it computes like a 37B model.

With \(b\) tokens in flight each expert sees about \(b k / n_r\) tokens, and a fraction \(1 - (1 - k/n_r)^b\) of experts is touched. For V3 (\(k/n_r = 1/32\)): \(b = 1\) reads 8 experts, \(b = 64\) reads 87% of them. So bandwidth approaches the total parameter count while FLOPs stay at the activated count. An expert GEMM in bf16 has intensity \(n\) FLOPs/byte for \(n\) tokens, so reaching the H100 ridge (about 295) needs about 295 tokens per expert, \(b \approx 9{,}440\) for V3, 32× more than a dense model. DeepSeek decodes with EP320 (one expert per GPU), so each GPU loads one expert's weights and serves many sequences, plus redundant copies of hot experts rebalanced from online load statistics.

Core How would you implement an MoE layer's forward pass efficiently on a GPU?

Route in fp32: logits \(T \times n_r\), scores, top-\(k\) of scores plus bias, gates gathered from unbiased scores and normalized. Permute: flatten the \(T k\) (token, slot) pairs, stable-argsort by expert id, gather hidden states into a \((Tk, d)\) buffer in expert order, get segment offsets from a bincount. Run the experts as a grouped GEMM over the ragged segments (a loop of per-expert matmuls is fine when \(n_r\) is small, as in Mixtral; with 256 experts it launches too many small kernels). Un-permute: multiply by the gates and scatter-add into the output (index_add_), then add the shared expert.

A capacity layout pads to \((n_r, C, d)\) for a batched matmul but drops tokens; dropless block-sparse or grouped kernels (MegaBlocks) keep every token without padding. Under EP an all-to-all sits between permute and GEMM, and another after. Common bugs: adding the bias to the gates, renormalizing at \(k = 1\), mishandling experts with zero tokens, softmax over the wrong axis, and running each expert on all tokens and masking afterwards.

Deep-dive MoE vs dense at equal training FLOPs vs equal inference memory: which wins when?

Equal training FLOPs: MoE wins clearly. Qwen3's MoE bases match dense ones with about 1/5 of the activated parameters, DeepSeekMoE 145B matches a dense 67B at 28.5% of the FLOPs, and Krajewski's fits put a compute-optimal MoE at the quality of a dense model trained with 20× the compute at \(10^{20}\) FLOPs (an extrapolated, single-lab law).

Equal total parameters or memory: dense wins per parameter. The dense model with the same FFN parameters is an upper bound (Pile 1.806) that DeepSeekMoE nearly reaches (1.808) with 5.7× fewer FLOPs, but if memory binds (edge devices, small clusters) a dense model of that size has more active capacity. Serving: MoE wins on throughput at large batch with good interconnect and EP; at small batch on few GPUs it loses its edge. Other axes: dense is simpler to train and fine-tune. A defensible answer names the regime: MoE for frontier pretraining and high-throughput serving, dense for memory-limited or small-data settings.

Deep-dive Is the Switch auxiliary loss really minimized by uniform routing? And what changes if you compute it per micro-batch versus over the global batch?

Uniform is the minimizer only in the decisive limit (\(f \approx P\)), where \(n_r \sum f_i^2 \ge 1\) by Cauchy-Schwarz. In general \(f\) is fixed by the argmax and \(P\) is free, so hedging is possible: with \(n_r = 2\), 90% of tokens choosing expert 1 with probabilities \((.5,.5)\) and 10% choosing expert 2 with \((0,1)\) gives \(f = (.9,.1)\), \(P = (.45,.55)\) and loss \(0.92\alpha < \alpha\). The gradient still lowers \(p_1\) until the argmax flips, so it works as a surrogate.

Scope matters. Frameworks compute \(f_i\) per micro-batch and average, which with few sequences per micro-batch is close to a sequence-level constraint: a code-only sequence must still spread over all experts, hurting specialization. Qiu et al. all-reduce the \(n_r\)-sized count vector so \(f\) reflects the global batch (\(\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N_P}\sum_j n_r \sum_i \bar f_i P^j_i\), gradients only through the local \(P^j\)). At 400B tokens average perplexity fell from 7.347 to 7.198 (3.4B/0.6B model) and from 5.778 to 5.603 (15B/2.54B), with clearer domain-specialized experts, and Qwen3 adopted it. A shuffled micro-batch control recovers most of the gain, so token diversity matters. Trade-off: a lopsided micro-batch makes a straggler device, so a small micro-batch term can be kept.

Deep-dive Design question: you need a model with about 10B activated and 100B total parameters, served on 8 GPUs of 80 GB. Choose the MoE configuration and say what you would ablate.

Feasibility first: 100B parameters in FP8 is 100 GB, well inside 8 GPUs (640 GB) with room for KV cache; bf16 (200 GB) also fits, but FP8 halves decode bandwidth. Assume attention plus embeddings take about 2B, leaving 8B activated and about 98B total expert parameters, a ratio of about 12. With \(k = 8\) routed and 1 shared expert, \((n_r + 1)/9 \approx 12\) gives \(n_r \approx 107\); take 112 (14 experts per GPU under EP8). Set the expert width so \((k + n_s) d_e\) equals the dense FFN width you would use for a 10B model, and keep the first 1 to 3 layers dense.

Choices: sigmoid gates with normalization; aux-loss-free bias plus a tiny sequence-wise loss, balanced over the global batch; dropless kernels; fp32 router; no node limit (one node). Ablate at small scale, at fixed FLOPs and tokens: granularity (optimal \(G\) rises with budget), shared experts (0 vs 1), sparsity (Kimi K2's law says more total experts help), bias vs aux loss vs both, hash routing for early layers. Measure loss, downstream evals, MaxVio, dead experts, and decode throughput at your target batch.

Deep-dive What changed in DeepSeek-V4's MoE relative to V3, and why do you think each change was made?

From the V4 report (a preview): (1) \(\sqrt{\operatorname{softplus}}\) affinity instead of sigmoid, no reason given; (2) bias balancing kept, plus a sequence-wise loss (\(\gamma = 0.001\), weight \(10^{-4}\)); (3) hash-routed MoE (expert chosen by a hash of the token ID) in the first 3 MoE layers, replacing dense FFNs; (4) no limit on nodes per token, with a fused kernel pipelining dispatch, GEMMs and combine in expert "waves"; (5) anticipatory routing (indices from older parameters, enabled around loss spikes) and SwiGLU clamping (linear branch in \([-10,10]\), gate capped at 10); (6) FP4 (MXFP4) quantization-aware training of expert weights; (7) 384 routed experts, top-6 in Pro (1.6T/49B); 256, top-6 in Flash.

Interpretations, ours where the report is silent: hash routing needs no router or balancing where a learned router has the least context (DeepSeekMoE saw slow balance convergence in the first layer). The node limit became unnecessary because the fused kernel hides communication: they found communication below compute time within a layer, and hiding requires \(C/B \le 2 d_e = 6144\) FLOPs/byte for Pro. The stability changes target the reported cause of spikes, outliers in MoE layers exacerbated by routing. FP4 targets experts because they dominate memory. Say plainly that V4 is a preview and no ablations for these items appear in the sections we read.

Cheat sheet

  • Layer: \(y = \sum_{s} \mathrm{FFN}_s(x) + \sum_{i \in \mathrm{TopK}} g_i\,\mathrm{FFN}_i(x)\). FLOPs \(\propto (k + n_s)\,d_e\), parameters \(\propto n_r + n_s\). Training compute \(\approx 6 N_{\text{act}} D\).
  • Sizes: expert \(= 3 d d_e\) (V3: 44.04M). V3: 671B total, 37B active (5.5%), experts 97.8% of parameters. Mixtral: 46.7B / 12.9B. Activated FFN width \(= (k+n_s)d_e\) = the dense FFN width (V3: \(9 \cdot 2048 = 18{,}432\)).
  • Gates: softmax then renormalize = top-\(k\) then softmax. V3: sigmoid, normalize, times 2.5 in code. At \(k = 1\) keep the raw probability, or the router gets no gradient.
  • Switch loss: \(\alpha n_r \sum f_i P_i\); \(f\) is a count (no gradient), \(P\) the mean probability; \(\partial/\partial p_{t,i} = \alpha n_r f_i/T\); equals \(\alpha\) at uniform, \(n_r\alpha\) at collapse; \(\alpha = 0.01\).
  • Capacity: \(C = \lceil \text{cf}\,T k/n_r \rceil\); overflow skips the expert (residual only); dropless = block-sparse or grouped GEMM; V3 drops nothing.
  • Router stability: compute in fp32; z-loss \(= \text{mean}(\log\sum e^{z})^2\), \(c_z = 0.001\).
  • Aux-loss-free: bias only for selection, \(b_i \leftarrow b_i - \gamma\,\mathrm{sign}(c_i - \bar c)\); V3 \(\gamma = 0.001\), frozen for the last 500B tokens, plus a sequence-wise loss \(10^{-4}\). MaxVio \(= (\max - \text{mean})/\text{mean}\) of expert load.
  • Balance scope: micro-batch balance is nearly sequence-level and hurts specialization; synchronize \(f_i\) across the global batch (Qiu et al.; Qwen3).
  • DeepSeekMoE: split experts \(m\)-fold, activate \(m\)-fold; \(\binom{16}{2} = 120\) vs \(\binom{64}{8} = 4.4 \times 10^9\); shared experts always on. V2: 2 + 160, top-6. V3: 1 + 256, top-8, \(d_e = 2048\).
  • Expert parallelism: dispatch (FP8) + combine (BF16) all-to-all. V3: at most 4 of 8 nodes per token (node score = sum of top-2 affinities), IB 50 vs NVLink 160 GB/s, hidden when \(C/B \le 2 d_e = 4096\) FLOPs/byte.
  • Serving: all experts resident (V3 about 0.67 TB in FP8). Tokens per expert \(= bk/n_r\); about 295 per expert to be compute-bound in bf16, so \(b \approx 9{,}440\) for V3. Redundant experts for hot spots.
  • V4 (preview, 2026): \(\sqrt{\text{softplus}}\) affinity, hash routing in first 3 MoE layers, no node limit, fused EP kernel, anticipatory routing, SwiGLU clamp \(\pm 10\), FP4 experts. Flash 1 + 256 (top-6, 2048); Pro 1 + 384 (top-6, 3072).
  • Scaling: Kimi K2 sparsity 48 needs 1.69× fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8 for equal loss; optimal granularity grows with compute (Krajewski).
