Long context: extension & evaluation

P0 ~60 min Prereqs: 05 RoPE, 06 KV cache, 07 KV-efficient attention, 09 Beyond full attention

Models are pretrained on 4K to 8K tokens and shipped with 128K to 1M. This page covers how the gap is closed (rescaling RoPE frequencies, staged continued pretraining, long-document data) and how to check whether the model uses what it claims to accept: perplexity, needle tests, RULER, HELMET and their limits.

Why long context is a separate, late stage

A long context 长上下文 model accepts a context window 上下文窗口 of 128K to 1M tokens, but is almost never trained at that length from the first step. Three forces push the same way.

Compute. With causal attention and FlashAttention (nothing of size \(T \times T\) is stored), prefill of \(T\) tokens costs the weight matmuls, linear in \(T\), plus attention, quadratic in \(T\). Each query at position \(t\) does \(4 n_h d_h t\) FLOPs per layer (scores and weighted values), and summing over \(t\) gives \(2 n_h d_h T^2\):

\[ F_{\text{prefill}}(T) \;=\; \underbrace{2\,N_{\text{mm}}\,T}_{\text{weights}} \;+\; \underbrace{2\,L\,n_h d_h\,T^2}_{\text{attention}}, \qquad T^\star = \frac{N_{\text{mm}}}{L\,n_h d_h} \]
\(N_{\text{mm}}\) = parameters that take part in matmuls; \(T^\star\) is where attention FLOPs equal weight FLOPs

For Llama-3-8B, \(N_{\text{mm}} = 7.50\)B (page 06) and \(L\,n_h d_h = 32 \cdot 4096 = 2^{17}\), so \(T^\star = 7.504\times10^{9}/131{,}072 \approx 57\text{K}\). FLOPs count a multiply-add as 2, and the causal mask is counted as half a square.

Training pays the same quadratic term on every step; Llama 3's report says it directly: "We do not train on long sequences earlier because the compute in self-attention layers grows quadratically in the sequence length" [Llama 3, §3.4.2].

Data. Long documents are rare and skewed toward a few domains. In SlimPajama about 30% of documents are naturally longer than 4K tokens, and books and GitHub code are the longest sources [Fu et al. 2024, §3]. Counting only documents of at least 64K tokens, ProLong's authors find 98.8B tokens in concatenated code repositories, 33.2B in books, 15.3B in CommonCrawl, 5.2B in arXiv and almost none in Wikipedia, StackExchange or C4 [Gao et al. 2024, Tab. 3]. A from-scratch run at 128K would either repeat this small pool or fill sequences with unrelated concatenated documents.

Quality. At a fixed token budget, shorter training contexts give better standard-benchmark scores in a controlled study of 1B-parameter models on 100B tokens (contexts from 512 to 16K). The authors' SkyLadder schedule, which grows the window from 8 tokens to the target, gains up to 3.7% on standard benchmarks and saves up to 22% of training time at 32K [Zhu et al. 2025, Abstract, Fig. 1, §4.7]. Caution: small models on academic corpora, not shown at frontier scale.

The standard recipe follows: pretrain short (4K to 8K) for almost all tokens, then continue pretraining 继续预训练 at longer lengths for a small fraction of the budget (Llama 3: about 800B of 15.6T tokens, 5%; DeepSeek-V3: 119K of 2,788K GPU-hours, 4.3% [DeepSeek-V3, Tab. 1]), then fine-tune. Xiong et al. measured the saving directly: switching from 4K to 32K sequences at 80% of training cost \(2.270\times10^{22}\) FLOPs against \(3.783\times10^{22}\) for 32K from the start, 40% less, with similar long-task quality [Xiong et al. 2023, Tab. 10]. The one frontier exception is DeepSeek-V4, which grows the sequence length inside pretraining (4K, 16K, 64K, 1M) and needs sparse and compressed attention to afford it (recipes below).

From your jet tagger

A jet has tens to a few hundred constituents, so the \(T^2\) term never mattered and the set was small enough to attend over fully. Here \(T\) spans four orders of magnitude and the cost model changes with it: below about 57K tokens an 8B model is weight-bound, above it attention dominates. Also, your tagger had no positions, so a longer jet changed nothing about how any pair of particles scored. In a RoPE model the score of a pair depends on their offset, so lengths never seen in training are an out-of-distribution problem by construction.

Why running a short-context RoPE model longer fails

Extrapolation 外推 means running at lengths beyond training. Interpolation 插值 means squeezing the longer range into the range the model has seen. Page 05 derives why extrapolation fails for RoPE (angles the network never saw, and attention dilution); we use its notation (\(\theta_i = b^{-2i/d_h}\), wavelength \(\lambda_i = 2\pi/\theta_i\), row vectors, \(R_t R_j^\top = R_{t-j}\)) and add only what the rescaling methods need.

1. Some pairs never finish a turn in training. A pair with \(\lambda_i > L\) covers less than one rotation over the training context, so during training it is close to a smooth position-dependent channel rather than a periodic one. The YaRN authors argue such pairs can carry absolute position, while pairs with \(\lambda_i \ll L\) carry only relative order [Peng et al. 2023, §3.2]. At \(d_h = 128\), \(b = 10^4\), \(L = 4096\), that is 18 of 64 pairs (pairs 46 to 63). The slowest pair reaches 0.47 rad at 4,096 tokens, 3.78 rad at 32K and 15.1 rad at 128K (page 05), so most of its extended range is phase the network never saw.

2. Offsets near the training length are barely trained even inside it. Relative offset \(i\) occurs \(f(i) = \sum_{s \in \text{corpus}} \max(|s| - i, 0)\) times, which falls off quickly when most documents are short. With a 2,048-token training length on SlimPajama, offsets of 1,536 or more make up under 5% of position indices, and the STRING authors report that open models' effective context is usually under half their training length: Llama 3.1's needle retrieval degrades once position indices pass about 90K, and most failures sit in the first third of the document [An et al. 2024, §2-3]. Being trained at a length does not mean being trained well at it.

3. The softmax gets flatter. With \(N\) roughly equal distractors a fixed logit margin \(\Delta\) buys weight \(e^{\Delta}/(e^{\Delta} + N - 1)\) (page 05). A model calibrates its sharpness to the lengths it saw. YaRN's temperature addresses this directly (next section).

Rescaling methods target the first and third failures. The second, and the model's need to learn to use distant tokens, is why every serious recipe also trains.

Position Interpolation: shrink every angle

PI [Chen et al. 2023, Eq. 4] replaces position \(m\) by \(mL/L' = m/s\) inside RoPE. Because \(\theta_i (m/s) = (\theta_i/s)\, m\), this is \(f_i = s\) for all pairs:

\[ \theta'_i = \frac{\theta_i}{s}, \qquad \text{so } \lambda'_i = s\,\lambda_i \text{ for every pair.} \]
The maximum angle any pair reaches at \(L'\) equals what it reached at \(L\) in training

Notation check: the YaRN paper's §2.3 writes PI as \(g(m) = s\cdot m\) while defining \(s = L'/L\); its Appendix A.1 and Chen et al. have \(mL/L'\), used here.

Why it works. The score as a function of offset, \(a(u) = \operatorname{Re}\sum_j h_j e^{\mathrm{i} u\theta_j}\), is a sum of sinusoids: smooth between integer offsets, unconstrained outside \([0, L]\). Interpolating stays between offsets the model already handles, and Chen et al. bound the error there by \(\tfrac{d}{32\ln b}\max_j|h_j|\), at least about 600 times below the extrapolation bound [§2.3] (page 05). Empirically, 1,000 steps take LLaMA 7B to 65B to 32K, with passkey retrieval reaching the full target window after 200 steps [Tab. 3-4].

Its cost. Neighboring tokens' angles now differ by \(\theta_i/s\) instead of \(\theta_i\). The fastest pair, 1 rad per token (57.3°), turns 0.125 rad (7.2°) per token at \(s = 8\), so the distinctions between nearby tokens that fast pairs provide are squeezed 8×. Chen et al. see 0.01 to 0.05 perplexity loss inside the original window; at 8K their zero-shot benchmarks stay within about 2 points of the original except BoolQ (76.1 to 73.2, and 64.7 at 32K), which they attribute to its need for close attention to word order [Tab. 5]. The YaRN paper notes that earlier PI fine-tunes reached only about \(s = 8\) before outputs degraded [§3.1].

NTK-aware scaling: change the base, not the positions

The idea, from an open-source forum post later written up in the YaRN paper, is to spread the interpolation over pairs: leave fast pairs alone and slow slow pairs more. The simplest such family is a base change. Require the fastest pair (\(i = 0\)) unchanged and the slowest pair (\(i = d/2 - 1\), exponent \(2i/d = (d-2)/d\)) slowed by exactly \(s\):

\[ b'^{-(d-2)/d} = \frac{b^{-(d-2)/d}}{s} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \boxed{\,b' = b\, s^{\,d/(d-2)}\,}, \qquad f_i = \Big(\frac{b'}{b}\Big)^{2i/d} = s^{\,2i/(d-2)}. \]

This is Eq. 22 of the YaRN paper [App. A.2]. For \(d = 128\), \(s = 8\): \(b' = 8^{128/126}\,b = 8.268\,b\), so \(10^4 \to 82{,}685\), and \(f_i = 8^{i/63}\), a geometric ladder from 1 to 8.

Its flaw. Only the slowest pair is truly interpolated. Pair \(i\) has a trained angle range \(L\theta_i\) but at \(L'\) sees \(L'\theta_i / f_i = (s/f_i)\,L\theta_i\), so any pair with \(f_i < s\) is extrapolated by \(s/f_i\). That is harmless for fast pairs, whose phase wraps many times, and harmful for pairs whose wavelength is near \(L\): pair 45 (\(\lambda = 4{,}080\)) has \(f = 8^{45/63} = 4.42\) and sees 1.81× its trained range. The YaRN paper reports that fine-tuning with NTK-aware scaling ends up worse than PI, and that \(s\) has to be set above the true ratio because some pairs are extrapolated to out-of-bound values [App. A.2].

Dynamic scaling recomputes the scale from the current length \(l\): \(s = \max(1, l/L)\) at each forward pass, so short inputs are untouched and long ones degrade gracefully instead of falling off a cliff at \(L'\); with NTK-aware scaling this is "Dynamic NTK" [§3.4]. The catch is the KV cache: the rotation of every earlier key changes whenever \(s\) changes, so the cache must hold unrotated keys (or be recomputed), at odds with the "cache rotated keys" habit of page 05.

YaRN: treat pairs by wavelength, and fix the temperature

YaRN's two observations are about which pairs to touch. Pairs with many turns inside \(L\) (\(\lambda \ll L\)) resolve local order and are needed at full resolution: do not interpolate them. Pairs with \(\lambda \geq L\) never complete a turn: interpolate them by the full \(s\), so no extrapolation remains. Pairs in between get a blend. With \(r_i = L/\lambda_i\), the number of turns pair \(i\) makes in the original context [Peng et al. 2023, Eq. 10-13]:

\[ \gamma_i = \operatorname{clip}\!\Big(\frac{r_i - \alpha}{\beta - \alpha},\, 0,\, 1\Big), \qquad \theta'_i = (1-\gamma_i)\,\frac{\theta_i}{s} + \gamma_i\,\theta_i . \]
\(r_i \le \alpha\): fully interpolated. \(r_i \ge \beta\): untouched. Suggested for the LLaMA family: \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 32\)

The Hugging Face parameters beta_fast \(= \beta = 32\) and beta_slow \(= \alpha = 1\) are named for the rotation counts they bound, which is why the mapping looks reversed. Set \(\alpha = 1, \beta = 32\) with \(L = 4096\): pairs with wavelength under \(L/32 = 128\) tokens keep their frequency, pairs above 4,096 tokens are divided by \(s\).

The temperature. YaRN also multiplies the logits: \(\softmax\big(q^\top k / (t\sqrt{d_h})\big)\) with

\[ \sqrt{1/t} = 0.1\ln s + 1 . \]

The authors fit this constant by minimizing perplexity against \(s\) for LLaMA 7B to 65B without fine-tuning, and found it also works "fairly well" for Llama 2 [§3.3, Eq. 15]. It is implemented for free by scaling both \(q\) and \(k\) by \(\sqrt{1/t}\), for example by scaling the cos and sin tables, so the logits are multiplied by \(1/t = (0.1\ln s + 1)^2\).

Why a temperature at all (our derivation, not the paper's). Under the dilution model, the needle's weight is about \(e^{\Delta}/N\). Going from \(N\) to \(sN\) keys and wanting the same weight requires the margin to grow by \(\ln s\): \(\Delta' = \Delta + \ln s\). A logit multiplier \(m\) gives \(m\Delta = \Delta + \ln s\), so \(m = 1 + \ln s/\Delta\). YaRN's fit is \(m = (1 + 0.1\ln s)^2 \approx 1 + 0.2\ln s\), which corresponds to an effective margin \(\Delta \approx 5\) nats. It is a heuristic: the constant was fitted, not derived, and other models may want another value (Llama 4's logarithmic query scaling follows the same logic, page 05).

A trap: two ramps are both called YaRN

The paper's \(\gamma\) is linear in \(r_i = L/\lambda_i\), that is linear in frequency. The reference implementation (Hugging Face rope_type="yarn", and DeepSeek-V3's precompute_freqs_cis) instead finds the two boundary pair indices and ramps linearly in the index, which is linear in log-wavelength [DeepSeek-V3 model.py]:

\[ \text{low} = \Big\lfloor \tfrac{d\,\ln\!\big(L/(\beta\,2\pi)\big)}{2\ln b} \Big\rfloor, \quad \text{high} = \Big\lceil \tfrac{d\,\ln\!\big(L/(\alpha\,2\pi)\big)}{2\ln b} \Big\rceil, \quad \gamma_i = 1 - \operatorname{clip}\!\Big(\tfrac{i - \text{low}}{\text{high} - \text{low}}, 0, 1\Big). \]

The endpoints agree (same set of untouched and fully interpolated pairs, up to rounding). The middle does not. At \(d = 128\), \(b = 10^4\), \(s = 8\), \(L = 4096\), pair 32 is slowed by 3.56 under the paper's ramp and 1.68 under the code's, and at pair 40 the two disagree by 6.45 against 3.06, a factor 2.1. Neither is "wrong", but a model fine-tuned with one and served with the other is silently different. The drill implements both (ramp="wavelength" and ramp="index"). Llama 3.1's llama3 rule (below) uses the paper's form.

import math, torch

def theta(d, base):                                   # (d/2,) float64: 1, ..., base^(-(d-2)/d)
    return base ** (-torch.arange(0, d, 2, dtype=torch.float64) / d)

def slowdown(method, d=128, base=1e4, s=8.0, L=4096, alpha=1.0, beta=32.0):
    """f_i = theta_i / theta'_i per pair (1 = untouched, s = fully interpolated)."""
    n = d // 2
    if method == "pi":
        return torch.full((n,), s, dtype=torch.float64)
    if method == "ntk":                               # b' = b * s^(d/(d-2))  =>  f_i = s^(i/(n-1))
        return theta(d, base) / theta(d, base * s ** (d / (d - 2)))
    if method == "yarn":                              # the paper's ramp: linear in r = L / wavelength
        r = L / (2 * math.pi / theta(d, base))        # turns each pair makes inside the original context
        g = ((r - alpha) / (beta - alpha)).clamp(0, 1)    # 1 = keep, 0 = interpolate
        return 1.0 / ((1 - g) / s + g)                # theta'_i = (1-g) theta_i/s + g theta_i
    raise ValueError(method)

Llama 3.1's llama3 rule

Llama 3 raised the RoPE base to 500,000 at the start of pretraining, citing Xiong et al. [Llama 3, §3.2]. The Llama 3.1 release additionally ships a rope_scaling entry: rope_type="llama3", factor 8, low_freq_factor 1, high_freq_factor 4, original_max_position_embeddings 8192, with rope_theta 500,000 and max_position_embeddings 131,072 [config, mirror]. The Hugging Face implementation is short:

# transformers/modeling_rope_utils.py, _compute_llama3_parameters (abridged)
low_freq_wavelen  = old_context_len / low_freq_factor      # 8192 / 1 = 8192
high_freq_wavelen = old_context_len / high_freq_factor     # 8192 / 4 = 2048
wavelen = 2 * math.pi / inv_freq
inv_freq_llama = torch.where(wavelen > low_freq_wavelen, inv_freq / factor, inv_freq)

smoothed = (1 - smooth) * inv_freq_llama / factor + smooth * inv_freq_llama

inv_freq_llama = torch.where(is_medium, smoothed, inv_freq_llama)   # attention_factor stays 1.0

Read it as YaRN's paper ramp with \(\alpha\) = low_freq_factor = 1, \(\beta\) = high_freq_factor = 4, \(L\) = 8,192, \(s\) = factor = 8, and no temperature: \(r_i = L/\lambda_i\), \(\gamma = (r_i - 1)/3\), \(\theta' = (1-\gamma)\theta/8 + \gamma\theta\). (In medium-band pairs inv_freq_llama equals inv_freq, so the code's blend is exactly that; the drill's tests assert it.) At \(d_h = 128\), \(b = 5\times10^5\): the wavelength thresholds are 2,048 and 8,192, so pairs 0 to 28 (29 pairs) are untouched, pairs 29 to 34 (6 pairs, slowed by 1.21, 1.55, 2.03, 2.69, 3.68, 5.26) are blended, and pairs 35 to 63 (29 pairs) are divided by 8. The slowest wavelength goes from 2.56M to 20.5M tokens. YaRN's \(\beta = 32\) would leave only 19 pairs untouched at this base and \(L\), so Meta chose a much smaller \(\beta\).

Pitfall: factor is not \(L'/L\)

Llama 3.1 has factor 8 on an 8,192 original length, which would nominally reach 65,536, yet it advertises 131,072; Llama 3.2 1B ships 32 and Llama 4 Scout 16 (page 05). The base was already 500,000, so treat the factor as a tuned slow-down for the slow pairs. Meta does not document how this entry was chosen, or whether the checkpoints were trained with it.

Raising the base during training (ABF), and how far to raise it

Xiong et al.'s "adjusted base frequency" raises \(b\) (10,000 to 500,000 for Llama 2) and trains through it, with results already in page 05. The open question is how far, and two recent data points say the analytic rules undershoot. ProLong ablates the base at 64K for Llama-3-8B (base 500K): the long-context average is 29.1 at 0.5M, 48.7 at 4M and 54.6 at 8M (needle-style recall 25.8, 81.3, 96.0), while a Dynamic-NTK-style rule suggests about 4M. At 512K the rule suggests 64M, and 128M and 256M did slightly better (57.7 and 57.8 against 57.6) [Gao et al. 2024, App. B.1, Tab. 18-19]. ProLong ships 8M for the 64K stage and 128M for the 512K stage; Qwen2.5-1M raises the base at every stage (10K to 1M, 1M at 64K, 5M at 128K, 10M at 256K) [Qwen2.5-1M, §3]. The two also compose: UltraLong starts from Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (base 500K) and reaches 1M to 4M with YaRN scales 128 to 512 at \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 4\), beating an NTK-aware base at long lengths (RULER average below 1M: 80.17 against 76.62) [Xu et al. 2025, Tab. 3].

LongRoPE: search the factors

If the ideal \(f_i\) is unknown, search for it. LongRoPE [Ding et al. 2024, §3] makes two departures from the formulas above. (1) The factors \(\lambda_i\) (their name for \(f_i\)) are free parameters searched in \([1, 1.25\,s]\) in steps of 0.01 under a monotonicity constraint, by an evolutionary search seeded with the PI, NTK and YaRN factors and scored by perplexity on long samples. (2) The first \(\hat n\) positions (up to 256) are not rescaled, echoing the observation that initial tokens receive large attention (attention sinks). A progressive schedule reaches 2,048K tokens: fine-tune to 256K, then run a second search on the fine-tuned model to 2,048K with no further fine-tuning, a 512× extension. Rescaling hurts short inputs, so they re-search factors for 4K and 8K and switch factor sets by input length.

LongRoPE2 [Shang et al. 2025] hypothesizes that higher RoPE dimensions are undertrained, so the "critical dimension" beyond which pairs stay out of distribution sits earlier than the analytic value (30 rather than 35 for LLaMA3-8B). Its search is scored on "needle" tokens only, and mixed-context-window training uses the original RoPE on short sequences and the rescaled RoPE on long ones. It extends LLaMA3-8B to 128K with 10B tokens (Meta used about 800B) while retaining over 98.5% of short-context performance, with RULER 82.03 at 128K against 73.40 (LongRoPE), 73.19 (NTK) and 49.39 (YaRN). Read with care: the comparison is in the authors' own setup, YaRN improves to 71.46 with their corrected critical dimension (Tab. 5), and switching factor sets forces a KV-cache recomputation [App. B].

How named models did it

The pieces above combine into a recurring skeleton: short pretraining, a RoPE change, one or more continued-pretraining stages on long data, a check after each stage, and long-aware fine-tuning. The details differ, and so do the tokens spent.

Llama 3: six stages, gated on two checks

Llama 3 405B pretrained on 15.6T tokens at 8K (4K sequences early, 8K after 252M tokens) with RoPE base 500,000. Then "we increased context length gradually in six stages, starting from the original 8K context window and ending in the final 128K context window", on about 800B tokens, moving to the next stage only after the model "has successfully adapted": (1) short-context evaluations "recovered completely" and (2) the model "perfectly solves needle in a haystack tasks up to that length" [Llama 3, §3.4.2]. The report gives neither the intermediate lengths nor the per-stage token counts. Two implementation facts matter:

  • Document masks. Llama 3 masks attention between documents packed into one sequence, and found this "limited impact during standard pre-training" but "important in continued pre-training on very long sequences" [§3.2]. At 128K, a sequence holds dozens of unrelated documents, and letting every token attend to all of them adds noise and cross-document "dependencies" that do not exist.
  • Context parallelism. The 128K stage ran TP 8, CP 16, PP 16, DP 8 on 16,384 GPUs at 38% MFU, against 43% at 8K on 8,192 GPUs [Tab. 4]. See systems.

Fine-tuning needed care too: "naively applying our existing SFT recipe with only short-context data resulted in significant regressions in long-context capabilities from pre-training". The fix was synthetic long data at 0.1% of the SFT mix (question answering over long documents, hierarchical summarization, reasoning over code repositories, bucketed from 16K to 128K), with DPO on short context only [§4.3.4]. Reported results: multi-needle retrieval 98.8 (8B), 97.5 (70B), 98.1 (405B), and InfiniteBench En.MC 65.1, 78.2, 83.4 [Tab. 2, 21].

DeepSeek-V3: YaRN on the RoPE key

After 14.8T tokens at 4K, V3 applies YaRN and runs two phases of 1,000 steps each, 4K to 32K to 128K. The hyperparameters are identical in both phases: \(s = 40\), \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 32\), \(\sqrt{t} = 0.1\ln s + 1\) as printed in the report. Phase 1 uses sequence length 32K and batch size 1,920, phase 2 uses 128K and 480, and the learning rate is 7.3e-6 for both, equal to the final pretraining learning rate [DeepSeek-V3, §4.3]. If the batch size counts sequences, as in V3's pretraining, each step is \(1{,}920 \times 32{,}768 = 480 \times 131{,}072 = 62.9\)M tokens, so the extension is about 126B tokens, 0.85% of pretraining, and cost 119K of 2,788K H800 GPU-hours.

Why "applied exclusively to the decoupled shared key \(k^R\)". In MLA (page 07) the content path \(q^C, k^C\) is position-free so that weight absorption works, and RoPE lives only in the 64-dimensional \(q^R\) (per head) and \(k^R\) (shared). Nothing positional is left in the 128 content dimensions, so the only frequency table to rescale is the one that rotates those 64 dimensions. The reference code shows the details [inference/model.py]:

  • The table is built for dim = qk_rope_head_dim = 64 (32 pairs at base 10,000; slowest wavelength \(2\pi\cdot10^{4\cdot62/64} = 47{,}117\) tokens) and rotates both \(q^R\) and \(k^R\): a relative rotation needs both sides on the same frequencies, though the report's wording is loose.
  • It uses the code ramp. With \(L = 4096\), \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 32\): \(\text{low} = \lfloor 10.47 \rfloor = 10\) and \(\text{high} = \lceil 22.51 \rceil = 23\), so pairs 0 to 10 (11) are untouched, pairs 11 to 22 (12) blended and pairs 23 to 31 (9) divided by 40, taking the slowest wavelength to 1.88M tokens.
  • The temperature is not folded into cos and sin. The code multiplies the softmax scale of the whole 192-dimensional logit by \(\text{mscale}^2\): \(\text{mscale} = 0.1\ln 40 + 1 = 1.3689\), so the scale becomes \(192^{-1/2} \times 1.8739 = 0.1352\). Scaling only the rotated slice would rescale \(q^R\cdot k^R\) but not \(q^C\cdot k^C\).
  • It is static: the rescaled table and scale are chosen at construction when max_seq_len > original_seq_len, so a 100-token prompt sees the extended model too. The config carries factor 40, original_max_position_embeddings 4096, beta_fast 32, beta_slow 1 and max_position_embeddings 163,840 (\(= 4096\times40\)) [config].

V3 reports strong needle retrieval to 128K after SFT (its Fig. 8) and LongBench v2 accuracy 48.7 against GPT-4o 48.1 and Claude-3.5-Sonnet 41.0 [Tab. 6]. Its pretraining implements document packing "for data integrity but do[es] not incorporate cross-sample attention masking during training" (§4.1), the opposite choice from Llama 3.

Qwen2.5-1M and Qwen3: base schedule plus inference-time extrapolation

Qwen2.5-1M uses five stages: 4,096, then 32,768 (base raised from 10,000 to 1,000,000 with ABF), then 65,536 (base 1M), 131,072 (5M) and 262,144 (10M). In the last three stages 75% of sequences are at the current maximum length and 25% shorter. Because natural text "prioritize[s] local coherence", the data adds synthetic long-range tasks: fill-in-the-middle, keyword- and position-based paragraph retrieval, and paragraph reordering. Data: how much, what mix, how to pack

How many long tokens?

Budgets span four orders of magnitude, because the goals differ. Fu et al. continue LLaMA-2 7B at 80K on per-source-upsampled SlimPajama and track needle accuracy as tokens grow: 37.8% at 100M, 59.0% at 300M, 81.1% at 500M, 85.3% at 1B, 88.0% at 5B and 84.0% at 10B, where the model seems to overfit its 80K range (GPT-4 at 128K: 87.1%). Their conclusion is that "500 million to 5 billion tokens are enough" for retrieval anywhere in 128K, because the capability is mostly acquired in pretraining [Fu et al. 2024, Fig. 1, 3]. Xiong et al. used 400B tokens, Llama 3 about 800B, ProLong 40B, UltraLong 1B: passkey-style retrieval is cheap to unlock, while robust use on realistic tasks with short-context quality preserved is what the larger budgets buy. ProLong beats Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on most long-context tasks with 5% as many long-context tokens, but it evaluates after SFT on downstream tasks, not needles [Gao et al. 2024, §1, §6].

The mixture

Upsample long documents per domain, do not reweight domains. Fu et al. keep SlimPajama's domain proportions (67% CommonCrawl, 15% C4, 4.5% each GitHub, Wikipedia and books, 2.5% arXiv, 2% StackExchange) and raise the share of documents longer than 4K inside each domain from about 30% to about 70%. Alternatives change the domain mix as a side effect, because long documents concentrate in books and code: upsampling books alone cuts book loss by 0.175 at 0 to 4K tokens but raises loss on C4 (+0.010), CommonCrawl (+0.016), StackExchange (+0.021) and GitHub (+0.029), while per-source upsampling has no domain worse by more than 0.008 at 0 to 4K and six of seven better at 4K to 128K [Tab. 5].

Keep a large share of high-quality short data. ProLong's ablations, with Llama-3-8B and 5B tokens at 64K: 60% long and 40% short is best on average; more long data helps at first and then hurts, short-task performance falls monotonically with the long fraction, and after SFT all-long training is a poor initialization. The long part is best as a 1:1 mix of code repositories and books (books help in-context learning, summarization and re-ranking; code helps needle-style recall), and the short part matters: their curated "ShortMix" beats SlimPajama, FineWeb-Edu and DCLM on both long and short scores, notably GSM8K (46.6 against 39.4 to 41.9) [§3, Tab. 4-6]. Head to head at 5B tokens, the ProLong mix beats the Fu et al. mix (long-task average 54.6 against 51.8 after SFT), though that comparison is by ProLong's authors.

Data quality may matter more than length. Xiong et al. report that "even with most of the long texts removed, the model can still obtain most of the performance gain over LLAMA 2" (though on QMSum that ablation falls below the baseline mix, 36.6 against 44.9), and that upsampling existing long text had no consistent advantage: the improvements "mostly come from the quality of the data itself, instead of the length distribution difference" [Tab. 7-8, §4.2]. Three studies, three emphases (domain-balanced length upsampling, short/long balance, quality) agree that a naive "only long documents" corpus is the wrong answer.

Synthetic long-range data is contested. Qwen2.5-1M adds synthetic retrieval and reordering tasks during pretraining, and Llama 3 mixes 0.1% synthetic long data into SFT. ProLong finds synthetic long instruction data does not help and even 1% hurts, and UltraLong also fine-tunes on short data only. ProLong's hypotheses: earlier work under-trained the long stage so synthetic data acted as extra long training, and Llama 3's short SFT set is much larger and can afford dilution [§5, Tab. 8]. Treat it as open, tied to how much long pretraining came first.

Train longer than you evaluate. Switching ProLong from 64K to 512K training, at the same extra 4B tokens, improved all four categories reported at a 64K evaluation length (recall 95.0 to 98.5, RAG 56.4 to 56.9, re-ranking 28.0 to 32.9, in-context learning 78.8 to 79.2) [Tab. 7]; Qwen2.5-1M's last stage did the same at 128K (Figure 2). ProLong's toy argument: a document of \(nd\) tokens contains \((n-1)(d-1)\) more distance-\(d\) dependencies than \(n\) documents of \(d\) tokens.

Short-context regressions and how to prevent them

Damage from extension depends on the method and the training. PI on LLaMA-7B stays within about 2 points of the original on every listed benchmark except BoolQ at 8K (BoolQ 76.1 to 73.2, and 64.7 at 32K). YaRN at \(s = 16\) then \(32\) on Llama 2 7B: MMLU 43.8, 42.5, 41.7 [Tab. 3]. In LongRoPE2's setup on Phi3-mini (original GSM8K 78.54), plain YaRN falls to 57.39, NTK to 63.99, LongRoPE to 67.17, and LongRoPE2 holds 73.62 [Tab. 4]. What works, roughly in order of how often it appears:

  1. Mix in high-quality short data during the long stage (ProLong's 40%; Llama 3's second gate, "short-context evaluations have recovered completely").
  2. Keep the short-input model unchanged when possible: dynamic scaling below \(L\), LongRoPE's per-length factor sets, or LongRoPE2's original RoPE on short windows during training (cost: two RoPE configurations to serve, and a KV recompute when the length crosses the switch).
  3. Small stages, checked each time (Llama 3's six stages; Qwen2.5-1M's RULER table).
  4. Start from an instruct model or restore instruction data afterwards: ProLong initializes its final model from Llama-3-8B-Instruct, which helped short-context scores.
  5. Mind SFT. Llama 3 saw long-context regressions from short-only SFT, ProLong and UltraLong did not: the answer depends on how strong the long pretraining was.

Static YaRN as in V3 changes behavior at every length, so its short-context numbers need to be reported alongside the long ones; page 27 covers V3's full evaluation.

Systems for long sequences

The distributed-training details are on page 15 and the kernels on page 16. What a model builder needs to know here:

Context parallelism 上下文并行 shards the sequence across GPUs. Llama 3 splits each sequence into \(2\times\text{CP}\) chunks and gives rank \(i\) chunks \(i\) and \(2\text{CP}-1-i\), which balances the causal mask's uneven work. It then all-gathers K and V and computes attention for the local Q chunk. The all-gather latency sits on the critical path, but the paper gives two reasons that it is acceptable: it supports arbitrary masks such as document masks with little effort, and with GQA the gathered K and V tensors are much smaller than Q, so \(O(S^2)\) attention dwarfs an \(O(S)\) all-gather [Llama 3, §3.3.2].

Ring Attention 环形注意力 [Liu et al. 2023] instead passes KV blocks around a ring: each device holds one query block and receives key-value blocks from its neighbor while computing on the current one. With block size \(c\) tokens and hidden size \(d\), each step does \(4dc^2\) FLOPs and moves \(4cd\) bytes, so the transfer hides behind compute when \(c \ge F/B\) (compute rate over link bandwidth). Per-device activation memory is \(6bch\) bytes, independent of total length, so context grows with device count. The paper's Table 2 puts the minimum sequence per device (\(6c\)) at 6.2K tokens for A100s on NVLink but 149.5K on InfiniBand, so the trick needs fast links; Table 3 reports 4,096K tokens for a 7B model on 32 A100s against 128K for the best baseline (32×). Both schemes are exact; they differ in mask flexibility (all-gather) versus never materializing full K and V on one device (ring).

Training throughput. Llama 3's MFU drops from 43% at 8K to 38% at 128K even with CP, and Xiong et al. measured about 17% speed loss going from 4K to 16K sequences for a 70B model [§2.1]: FlashAttention keeps memory flat, but the quadratic FLOPs remain.

Activation memory. FlashAttention stores no \(T\times T\) tensor, but the remaining activations still grow linearly with \(T\): one bf16 hidden-state tensor at \(T = 131{,}072\), \(d = 4096\) is \(131072\cdot4096\cdot2\) B \(= 1\) GiB per sequence, so the 32 layer inputs that activation checkpointing keeps already total 32 GiB per sequence for Llama-3-8B, before any recomputed intermediate. Long-sequence training therefore recomputes each layer in the backward pass (one extra forward per layer) and shards the sequence with context parallelism, which divides these per-device costs by the CP degree.

Chunked prefill 分块预填充 processes a long prompt in chunks, each attending to the cache built so far (the end-aligned mask of page 07), which bounds peak activation memory and lets decode requests interleave with a long prefill. Qwen2.5-1M's serving stack combines it with sparse attention for the 3× to 7× prefill speedup at 1M. The KV cache alone is large: Qwen2.5-7B has 28 layers, 4 KV heads and head dimension 128, so \(28\cdot2\cdot4\cdot128\cdot2 = 57{,}344\) B per token, 54 GiB at 1,010,000 tokens, and the model card asks for at least 120 GB of VRAM across GPUs [model card]. Cache-reduction levers are in page 07 and page 17.

Evaluation: what each test measures

A long-context claim has three separable parts: the model can access a fact placed far away, can use it (compose, aggregate, reason), and has not lost short-context quality or speed. Each benchmark below covers some of these and hides others.

Perplexity: necessary, not sufficient

Loss on long documents is cheap and smooth, and it should fall with context length: Xiong et al. fit validation loss as a power law in context length up to 32,768 tokens, and their per-position curves show whether loss "explodes" beyond the training length (it does for plain Llama 2 past 4,096, while their 70B ABF model trained at 16K holds its loss to 32K) [Xiong et al., Fig. 1, App. C]. But averaged loss is dominated by tokens that need only local context. Fu et al. compare the original data mixture with per-source length upsampling: validation losses at 1K, 16K, 32K, 64K and 128K differ by at most 0.01 (1.80, 1.56, 1.42, 1.33, 1.37 against 1.80, 1.56, 1.41, 1.32, 1.36), yet the original mixture "performs badly on precise retrieval" [Fig. 4]. Use loss as a regression alarm and a per-position sanity check, never as the headline.

Needle in a haystack: a floor, not a ceiling

Needle in a haystack (NIAH) 大海捞针 inserts one short fact at depth \(d\) into filler text of length \(T\) and asks for it, giving a \(d \times T\) grid (the drill builds this grid). It tests one thing, exact retrieval of a near-unique string. Llama 3 reports 100% retrieval at every depth and length, DeepSeek-V3 strong scores at every length to 128K (its Fig. 8), the Qwen2.5-1M models accurate passkey retrieval to 1M tokens (minor errors only in the 7B), and UltraLong-8B 100% at every length and depth up to 4M tokens. Such a result is now table stakes. Why it means little on its own:

  • The needle is a low-entropy signal. Success can reduce to matching a rare string, and HELMET finds most models score either near 100% or near 0% on it, leaving nothing to rank; its Spearman correlation with InfiniteBench QA (35 instruction-tuned models) is 0.63, against 0.81 for RULER's multi-key NIAH and 0.88 for HotpotQA in a RAG setup [Yen et al. 2025, §3.1, Fig. 4].
  • Passing it does not transfer even to nearby retrieval. Llama 3.1 70B scores 100.00 on InfiniteBench passkey retrieval at 128K but 2.22 on key-value retrieval, a task that is also plain lookup (the STRING paper's Table 3; a training-free position remapping lifts the second to 76.07) [An et al. 2024].
  • Literal overlap does the work. NoLiMa measures the ROUGE-1 precision between question and needle context at 0.905 for vanilla NIAH and 0.069 for its own design, where questions and needles share almost no words and linking them needs a world-knowledge hop [Modarressi et al. 2025, Tab. 1].

RULER and the effective context length

RULER [Hsieh et al. 2024] generates 13 synthetic tasks in four categories at controllable lengths: retrieval (single-key, multi-key with distractor needles, multi-value and multi-query variants), multi-hop tracing (variable tracking: follow \(X_2 = X_1\), \(X_3 = X_2\), ... to list every name bound to a value), aggregation (common and frequent words extraction, a proxy for summarization) and question answering (SQuAD and HotpotQA with distractor paragraphs). It runs 500 examples per task at 4K, 8K, 16K, 32K, 64K and 128K with greedy decoding. Its headline definition is the effective context length 有效上下文长度: the largest tested length at which the average score still exceeds a fixed pass mark, Llama-2-7B's 85.6% at 4K (the drill's effective_length).

The paper's error analysis of Yi-34B (200K) names failure modes worth testing for in any architecture: brittleness to needle type (UUID values fail beyond 128K while word-number pairs pass), failure to ignore distractor needles (about 40 points lost at 256K in the hardest version), incomplete retrieval when many values must be returned, and copying from context or falling back on parametric knowledge [§5]. Its stated limits: no control over needle depth, and no proof that the synthetic tasks track real ones. Newer models clear the bar (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct-1M scores 92.2 at 128K [Tab. 4], Gemma 4 31B 96.4 [Tab. 9]), so RULER at 128K now serves mainly as a stage gate and for smaller models.

Lost in the middle: position matters

Liu et al. put the one relevant document at each position among 10, 20 or 30 retrieved documents. Accuracy forms a U: for GPT-3.5-Turbo with 20 documents, 75.8% when the answer document is first, 53.8% when it is tenth and 63.2% when it is last, and the middle value is below the 56.1% it scores with no documents at all. Extended-context variants (16K, 100K) perform the same as their short versions when the input fits in both, and reader accuracy saturates far before retriever recall (more than 20 documents add only about 1.5% for GPT-3.5-Turbo and 1% for Claude-1.3) [Liu et al. 2023, §2-6]. Magnitudes differ across model generations but the lesson holds: report performance by position, not just by length (RULER does not; NIAH grids do), and for RAG rerank and truncate rather than append.

Realistic multi-task suites

HELMET [Yen et al. 2025] covers seven application-style categories (synthetic recall, retrieval-augmented generation, many-shot in-context learning, passage re-ranking, generation with citations, long-document QA, summarization) at 8K to 128K, with model-based metrics for open-ended outputs and few-shot prompts so base models can be scored. Over 59 models, categories correlate only moderately (in-context learning against the others: Spearman 0.34 to 0.61 at 128K), closed models lead open ones by 30 to 40 points on re-ranking and citations with the gap widening at length, and RAG tasks are the best cheap proxy for development [§3, Fig. 5-6].

LongBench v2 [Bai et al. 2024] has 503 multiple-choice questions over contexts of 8K to 2M words (median 54K) in six categories: single- and multi-document QA, in-context learning, dialogue history, code repositories, structured data. Questions came from 97 annotators and were kept only if the three screening LLMs did not all answer correctly and a human reviewer could not answer within 3 minutes with search. Human experts reach 53.7% in 15 minutes, the best direct-answer model 50.1%, and o1-preview, which reasons longer, 57.7%: these tasks reward inference-time compute, not just retrieval. With 503 items the confidence intervals are wide, and the task mix differs across length buckets.

What frontier reports use in 2026

As of September 2026, frontier reports lean on OpenAI's MRCR (multi-round coreference resolution, an in-context retrieval task with several near-identical needles) and CorpusQA at 1M tokens (DeepSeek-V4, above), and on RULER at 32K and 128K plus LOFT text retrieval, GraphWalks and MTOB translation from a half or full grammar book (Gemma 4, Tab. 9). A January 2026 preprint, EverMemBench-S, targets the literal-needle weakness with collision-tested distractors and a corpus ladder up to 326M tokens, and separates evidence access from answer use: the best dense retriever falls from 0.93 to 0.68 SR@10 between 64K and 326M tokens, and strict multi-source recall from 0.80 to 0.30 [Lin et al. 2026, Tab. 3]. One benchmark, one preprint: a data point, not a ranking.

A long-context evaluation suite for an architecture paper
Problem
A new architecture (sparse, hybrid, compressed, linear) can win on perplexity and needles while losing on tasks that use context, or win on quality while its cost advantage goes unmeasured.
Idea
Report six things: (1) per-position loss at 1×, 2× and 4× the training length; (2) a depth × length grid with distractors and non-literal needles (RULER multi-key, multi-value, tracking, aggregation; NoLiMa-style); (3) realistic tasks (HELMET categories, LongBench v2) after the same SFT for every model; (4) a short-context regression suite; (5) cost at each length (prefill and decode throughput, KV or state bytes); (6) claimed against effective length with an explicit pass mark.
Why it works
It separates access from use (2 against 3), quality from cost (5) and long from short (4), so one saturated number cannot carry the paper.
Evidence
Loss and needle numbers disagree with downstream behavior (Fu et al.; HELMET's correlations), and the recipe moves scores as much as many architecture changes (ProLong's data-mix and RoPE-base ablations), so baselines must share data, token budget and RoPE treatment.
Cost
Several long-context runs per model, some with model-based metrics.
Used by
Increasingly by long-context reports: Llama 3 (needle, ZeroSCROLLS, InfiniteBench), Gemma 4 (RULER, LOFT, GraphWalks, MTOB), DeepSeek-V4 (MRCR, CorpusQA, plus efficiency at 1M).

For a paper on an efficient architecture: put fixed-size-state models (linear attention, SSMs) on the recall-heavy tasks where they are expected to be weakest (page 09), report quality against cost, keep the training recipe identical across compared models, give confidence intervals, and check contamination on public benchmarks.

Failure modes and open problems

  • Effective length falls short of the window. RULER (Figure 3), NoLiMa (Llama 4 Scout at 10M claimed: 21.6 at 32K) and STRING's position-frequency argument (above) say the same thing: tokens near the end of the trained range are seen too rarely. Open: fix it in training (sequences longer than the evaluation length, per ProLong) rather than by inference-time remapping such as STRING, which lifts Llama 3.1 70B from 66.6 to 81.7 on RULER at 128K without training.
  • Attention sinks. A softmax must sum to one, so when no key matches, mass goes somewhere. Xiao et al. show that Llama-2-13B on a 65K-token PG19 book with a 1,024-token window (initial tokens evicted) has perplexity 5,158, but keeping four initial tokens ("sinks") plus 1,020 recent ones gives 5.40; replacing the four with linebreak tokens gives 5.60, so it is position, not content. Pretraining with a dedicated learnable sink token makes one sink enough (18.01 with it plus 1,023 recent tokens in their 160M models; the model trained without one needs four initial tokens for 18.01). It is not context extension: StreamEval accuracy falls to zero once the query-answer distance exceeds the cache size [Xiao et al. 2024, §3, App. C]. Modern models bake sinks in: DeepSeek-V4 adds a learnable per-head sink logit to the softmax denominator in its CSA and HCA core attention (their Eq. 27), so a head can attend to "nothing" (gpt-oss does the same, page 31). LongRoPE's unscaled prefix \(\hat n\) exploits the same effect.
  • Short-context regression, and its dependence on how SFT is done (above). Recency bias is the related position effect (the U-shaped curve above; RoPE layers concentrate on recent tokens while NoPE layers retrieve, page 05).
  • Retrieval versus reasoning. Passing retrieval does not mean answering questions that need synthesis: LongBench v2 needs reasoning tokens to beat humans, Qwen3's thinking mode scores below its non-thinking mode on average RULER at every model size (Tab. 23: 235B-A22B 92.2 against 95.0), and in DeepSeek's own comparison table every model scores lower on CorpusQA 1M than on MRCR 1M (V4-Pro-Max 62.0 against 83.5, Opus 4.6 71.7 against 92.9; the metrics differ, so this is suggestive only).
  • Length generalization beyond training. Gemma 3 models "generalize to 128K, but rapidly degrade as we continue to scale" [§5.3]: rescaling buys a multiple of the trained length, not unbounded length.
  • Architecture as the fix. The systems in page 09 attack the cost curve of the first section directly: sliding-window plus global layers (Gemma 3: KV overhead 60% to under 15% at 32K with little perplexity change, §5.2), RoPE/NoPE hybrids (RULER retrieval at 256K: 74.8 against 57.1 in Cohere's study, page 05), sparse selection, compression (DeepSeek-V4's 10% KV at 1M), linear and SSM hybrids. Whether these match dense attention on use at 1M, not just access, is the open question the evaluation section is for.
  • Beyond one context. At corpus scale (EverMemBench-S, 326M tokens) retrieval quality degrades with size and interference, and the gap widens between finding one relevant document and finding all of them.

Common misconceptions

  • "The scale factor is target length over trained length." Only in the textbook. Llama 3.1's factor is 8 on an 8,192 original length yet the model advertises 131,072; Llama 3.2 1B ships 32; DeepSeek-V3's 40 does match \(163{,}840/4{,}096\). Treat it as a tuned slow-down, and read the config for what the ramp does.
  • "NTK-aware scaling is interpolation." It interpolates only the slowest pair. Every other pair with \(f_i < s\) sees an angle range \(s/f_i\) times its trained one (1.81× for pair 45 at \(s = 8\)). YaRN's \(\alpha\)-clamp exists to remove exactly this.
  • "YaRN is NTK-aware plus a temperature." It is wavelength-dependent interpolation (NTK-by-parts) plus a temperature, and two ramps circulate: the paper's is linear in \(L/\lambda\), the reference code's is linear in pair index. A model tuned with one and served with the other differs silently.
  • "A positional-encoding trick extends the context." Cheap rescaling gets passkey-level retrieval (PI: 200 steps for passkey at 32K). Robust use on realistic tasks needs billions of tokens of long data (ProLong: 40B; Llama 3: about 800B), and evaluating after SFT.
  • "100% on needle-in-a-haystack means the model uses 128K." Llama 3.1 70B has 100.00 on passkey and 2.22 on key-value retrieval; almost every model on RULER is effective at half its claim or less; NoLiMa cuts most models below half of their baseline at 32K.
  • "Falling perplexity with length shows the context is used." Averaged loss is dominated by local tokens; two recipes with the same losses had very different retrieval.
  • "Train on long documents, and more of them is better." All-long mixes hurt after SFT and on short tasks (ProLong: best at 60% long); upsampling only books raises loss in other domains (Fu et al.); quality may matter more than length (Xiong et al.).
  • "Sliding windows plus sinks give unlimited context." They give stable perplexity on unbounded streams, not access to evicted content: accuracy falls to zero beyond the cache.

Interview questions

Warm-up Why not just pretrain at 128K from the first step?

Cost: attention FLOPs grow with \(T^2\) while weight FLOPs grow with \(T\). For Llama-3-8B, prefill at 128K costs 46× that at 8K for 16× the tokens, and attention is 70% of it (12.5% at 8K). The crossover is \(T^\star = N_{\text{mm}}/(L n_h d_h) \approx 57\text{K}\). KV cache and activations also grow linearly, forcing context parallelism. Data: long documents are scarce and skewed to books and code (about 98.8B tokens of 64K+ code-repo documents against 5.2B of arXiv in ProLong's counts), so a from-scratch 128K run either repeats a small pool or packs unrelated text. Quality: at a fixed token budget, shorter training contexts scored higher on standard benchmarks in a 1B study (SkyLadder).

So everyone trains short first and spends a small fraction on the long stage (Llama 3: about 800B of 15.6T tokens, 5%; DeepSeek-V3: 4.3% of GPU-hours). Xiong et al. measured 40% FLOP savings for the same long-task quality. The exception is DeepSeek-V4, which grows the sequence length inside pretraining (4K, 16K, 64K, 1M) and uses sparse and compressed attention to make that affordable.

Warm-up Calculate: Llama-3-8B's prefill FLOPs and KV cache at 8K and at 128K.

\(F(T) = 2N_{\text{mm}}T + 2Ln_hd_hT^2\) with \(N_{\text{mm}} = 7.504\)B and \(L n_h d_h = 2^{17}\). At \(T = 8192\): weights \(1.229\times10^{14}\), attention \(2^{18}\cdot 8192^2 = 1.759\times10^{13}\), total \(1.405\times10^{14}\) (0.28 s at 50% of 989.4 TFLOP/s). At \(T = 131072\): weights \(1.967\times10^{15}\), attention \(4.504\times10^{15}\), total \(6.471\times10^{15}\) (13.1 s). Ratio 46×.

KV: \(2\cdot 32\cdot 8\cdot 128\cdot 2 = 131{,}072\) B per token, so 1 GiB at 8K and 16 GiB at 128K per sequence. A batch-1 decode step reads weights (16.06 GB) plus the cache: 4.79 + 0.32 = 5.1 ms at 8K, 4.79 + 5.13 = 9.9 ms at 128K, about 196 and 101 tokens per second at 3.35 TB/s. For a 128K-context batch of 4 the cache is 64 GiB, more than the weights, which is what page 07's designs attack.

Warm-up What is YaRN's attention temperature, and what are its values for \(s = 4, 8, 40\)?

YaRN multiplies the pre-softmax logits by \(1/t\), with \(\sqrt{1/t} = 0.1\ln s + 1\): a longer context means more keys and a flatter softmax, and the fitted constant sharpens it back. Implemented by scaling both \(q\) and \(k\) by \(\sqrt{1/t}\) (for instance in the cos and sin tables) so it costs nothing. Values: \(s = 4\): amplitude 1.1386, logits ×1.2965; \(s = 8\): 1.2079, ×1.4591; \(s = 40\): 1.3689, ×1.8739. The constant was fitted on LLaMA 7B to 65B by minimizing perplexity without fine-tuning. A heuristic reading: keeping a needle's weight constant as \(N \to sN\) needs margin \(+\ln s\), so \(m = 1 + \ln s/\Delta\); YaRN's fit matches \(\Delta \approx 5\) nats. DeepSeek-V3 applies \(1.8739\) to the softmax scale of the entire logit rather than to cos and sin.

Core Why can't a RoPE model trained on 8K tokens just run at 32K?

Three reasons, separable. (1) Unseen phases. With \(b = 5\times10^5\), \(d_h = 128\), 29 of the 64 pairs (35 to 63) have wavelength above 8,192, so they never complete a turn in training (the slowest covers 0.02 rad, about 1.15°). At 32K it covers 0.08 rad: the network is asked about phases outside its training range, and the score \(a(u) = \operatorname{Re}\sum_j h_j e^{\mathrm{i}u\theta_j}\) is unconstrained there (Chen et al. show fitted curves within \([-1,1]\) on \([0,L]\) that exceed 8,000 outside). (2) Undertrained offsets. Offset \(i\) is seen \(\sum_s \max(|s|-i, 0)\) times, so offsets near 8K are rare even in training; STRING reports that open models' effective length typically does not exceed half their training length. (3) Dilution. The softmax over 4× more keys is flatter for the same logit margins.

Fixes address (1) and (3): rescale frequencies (PI, NTK/ABF, YaRN) so extended positions map to trained phases, add a temperature, and then train on long data, which is the only fix for (2) and for learning to use distant tokens.

Core Compare Position Interpolation, NTK-aware scaling and YaRN.

All three set \(\theta'_i = \theta_i/f_i\) and differ in \(f_i\). PI (位置插值): \(f_i = s\) for all pairs, so no pair exceeds its trained angle range, but the fastest pairs are slowed 8× at \(s = 8\) and neighboring tokens crowd (7.2° per token instead of 57.3° for pair 0). It needs about 1,000 steps, and earlier PI fine-tunes reached only about \(s = 8\) before outputs degraded. NTK-aware: change the base so pair 0 is unchanged and the last pair is slowed by exactly \(s\): \(b' = b\,s^{d/(d-2)}\), \(f_i = s^{2i/(d-2)}\), a geometric ladder (\(10^4 \to 82{,}685\) at \(d = 128\), \(s = 8\)). Fast pairs are preserved, but only the slowest is truly interpolated, and mid-wavelength pairs (pair 45: 1.81×) are extrapolated, so it works zero-shot at small \(s\) and fine-tunes worse than PI. YaRN: classify pairs by turns in the trained context \(r_i = L/\lambda_i\): \(r \le \alpha = 1\) fully interpolated, \(r \ge \beta = 32\) untouched, linear blend between, plus the logit temperature \((0.1\ln s + 1)^2\). No extrapolation remains, fast pairs are intact, and it needed 400 steps for 64K (256 A100-hours for a 7B). At \(d = 128\), \(b = 10^4\), \(L = 4096\), \(s = 8\): 21 pairs untouched, 25 blended, 18 divided by 8.

Caveats: \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), the temperature and the base are fitted heuristics; there are two ramp conventions (paper linear in \(r\); code linear in index); and none replaces long-data training.

Core Why does DeepSeek apply YaRN only to the decoupled RoPE key?

Because that is the only place position exists. MLA's content path (\(q^C\), \(k^C\), from the 512-dim latent) is position-free so weight absorption works; RoPE is confined to \(q^R\) (per head) and the shared \(k^R\), 64 dimensions (page 07). The only frequency table in the whole attention is the one rotating those 64 dimensions, so that is what YaRN rescales: \(s = 40\), \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 32\). Two details: \(q^R\) uses the same modified table (a relative rotation needs both sides), and in the reference code the temperature is applied as \(\text{mscale}^2 = 1.8739\) on the softmax scale of the whole 192-dimensional logit, not just the rotated slice. In the code the ramp is over 32 pairs at base 10,000: pairs 0 to 10 untouched, 11 to 22 blended, 23 to 31 divided by 40 (slowest wavelength 47K to 1.88M tokens). It is applied statically at construction time, so short inputs also see the extended model.

Core How much long-context data do you need, and how should you mix it?

Amount depends on the goal. Needle-level retrieval unlocks with 0.5B to 5B tokens (Fu et al.: needle accuracy 59.0% at 300M, 81.1% at 500M, 88.0% at 5B for LLaMA-2 7B post-trained on 80K; at 10B it "seems to overfit" and drops to 84.0%). Robust downstream use, evaluated after SFT, took 40B for ProLong-8B and about 800B (5% of pretraining) for Llama 3 405B; UltraLong got to 1M to 4M with 1B tokens by starting from an already-128K model. Mix: (a) upsample long documents within each domain and keep the domain proportions (Fu: about 30% to 70% long per domain; upsampling only books helps books and hurts C4, CommonCrawl, StackExchange, GitHub); (b) keep about 40% high-quality short data (ProLong: 60/40 best; short-task scores fall monotonically with the long share; the choice of short mix matters, GSM8K 46.6 against 39.4 to 41.9 for the alternatives); (c) long sources 1:1 books and code repositories (code for recall, books for in-context learning and summarization); (d) train longer than you evaluate (512K training improved 64K results); (e) use document masks at long lengths (Llama 3, ProLong), noting that UltraLong's separator-only approach also worked. Evidence conflicts on synthetic long data (Llama 3 and Qwen use it; ProLong says it hurts), and Xiong et al. argue that quality outweighs length.

Core A model scores 100% on needle-in-a-haystack at 128K. What does that tell you, and what would you test next?

It tells you exact retrieval of one near-unique string works at every tested depth at that length, with no evidence of a hard positional failure. It does not tell you the model can ignore distractors, retrieve several facts, follow a chain, aggregate, or reason: Llama 3.1 70B has 100.00 on passkey and 2.22 on key-value retrieval at 128K; HELMET finds NIAH's correlation with a realistic long-QA benchmark is 0.63 (RULER multi-key 0.81, RAG QA 0.88); RULER finds most models effective at half their claim. Next: (1) RULER's multi-key, multi-value, multi-query, variable tracking and aggregation to get an effective length; (2) a non-literal needle test (NoLiMa) since literal overlap is the crutch; (3) position-controlled results, since lost-in-the-middle is a depth effect; (4) realistic tasks (HELMET categories, LongBench v2) after the same SFT; (5) short-context regression; (6) cost. Also check the prompt format and tokenizer used for length, and repeat over needles and haystacks: single grids are noisy.

Core Explain Llama 3.1's rope_scaling of type llama3. Is it YaRN?

Config: base 500,000, factor 8, low_freq_factor 1, high_freq_factor 4, original_max_position_embeddings 8192. Wavelength thresholds: \(8192/1 = 8192\) and \(8192/4 = 2048\). Pairs with wavelength below 2,048 (0 to 28) keep their frequency, pairs above 8,192 (35 to 63) are divided by 8, and pairs between (29 to 34) blend with \(w = (8192/\lambda_i - 1)/(4-1)\): \(\theta' = (1-w)\theta/8 + w\theta\). That is exactly YaRN's paper ramp with \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 4\), \(s = 8\), \(L = 8192\), but with no attention temperature (attention_factor is 1.0). It differs from YaRN in Hugging Face and DeepSeek-V3 in two ways: \(\beta\) is 4 rather than 32 and the ramp is linear in \(L/\lambda\), not in pair index. The slowest wavelength moves from 2.56M to 20.5M tokens. factor is not \(L'/L\): 8 × 8192 = 65,536 but the model advertises 131,072, because the base was already raised to 500K. The Llama 3 report describes the base change and staged training, not this entry.

Core How does long-context continued pretraining affect short-context benchmarks, and how do you prevent regressions?

Position rescaling squeezes the angle range that short inputs use, and long-data training shifts the data distribution, so short benchmarks can fall. Measured: PI on LLaMA-7B, within about 2 points of the original at 8K except BoolQ (76.1 to 73.2), with BoolQ down to 64.7 at 32K; YaRN on Llama 2 7B, MMLU 43.8 to 42.5 (\(s = 16\)) to 41.7 (\(s = 32\)); on Phi3-mini, plain YaRN takes GSM8K from 78.5 to 57.4 while LongRoPE2 keeps 73.6. Prevention: mix about 40% high-quality short data (ProLong) and evaluate short benchmarks as a gate (Llama 3 required "recovered completely" before each stage advance); keep short inputs on the original RoPE (LongRoPE2 mixed-window training, LongRoPE per-length factors, dynamic scaling below \(L\), at the price of two RoPE configurations and a KV recompute at the switch); move in small stages; start from an instruct model or restore instruction data; and design SFT carefully (short-only SFT regressed Llama 3's long-context ability but not ProLong's). Also report short-context numbers next to long ones for static schemes such as V3's YaRN.

Deep-dive Design an evaluation suite for a paper claiming a better long-context architecture.

Controls first: identical data, token budget, training lengths, RoPE treatment and SFT for every compared model; the ProLong ablations show data mix and RoPE base move long-context scores by tens of points. Then, in order: (1) training-time diagnostics, per-position loss at 1×, 2×, 4× the training length, and loss on tokens that need long context; (2) controlled synthetic grid by depth × length with distractors and non-literal needles: RULER (multi-key, multi-value, variable tracking, aggregation), a NoLiMa-style set, and position-controlled retrieval; report effective length at a stated pass mark; (3) realistic tasks after the same SFT: HELMET categories (RAG, re-ranking, citation, long QA, summarization, ICL), LongBench v2, and at 1M a corpus-level QA and an MRCR-style task; (4) short-context regression: MMLU, GSM8K, code; (5) cost at each length: prefill and decode throughput, KV or state bytes, plotted with quality; (6) statistics: enough examples for confidence intervals (LongBench v2's 503 items are few), seeds if training is cheap, contamination checks. For an architecture with fixed-size state or sparse access include recall-heavy tasks where it is expected to be weakest. State claimed versus effective length, and beyond-training-length generalization.

Deep-dive Design: you must train a new 7B-class model for 1M-token context on 15T tokens. Lay out the schedule and say what you would check.

First quantify dense attention at 1M: per-token FLOPs are \(2N_{\text{mm}} + 2Ln_hd_hT\); for an 8B like Llama-3-8B, \(1.5\times10^{10} + 2.75\times10^{11}\), about 19× the cost of the weight term alone, and 128 KiB × \(2^{20}\) = 128 GiB of KV per sequence. So either accept a very short long stage or change the attention: local:global layers with short windows (Gemma 3: KV overhead 60% to under 15% at 32K), sparse selection, or compression (V4: 10% of V3.2's KV at 1M). Schedule: 4K to 8K for most of the tokens; then either staged lengths (V4: 4K, 16K, 64K, 1M, with dense attention early and sparse from 64K; Qwen2.5-1M: 32K, 64K, 128K, 256K) or a gradual window (SkyLadder); spend roughly 1% to 5% of tokens on long stages (V3: 0.85%, Llama 3: 5%). RoPE: raise the base per stage (Qwen2.5-1M: 1M, 1M, 5M, 10M; ProLong: 8M, then 128M) rather than trust a formula. Data: 60/40 long/short with per-domain length upsampling, books plus code repositories, high-quality short mix, document masks, train at a length above your evaluation length. Parallelism: context parallelism for the long stages. Gates after each stage: short-context recovery, then RULER/HELMET-subset at the trained length, then position-controlled retrieval; fix regressions before the next stage. SFT: short data first, then small amounts of long data, verifying whether synthetic long data helps or hurts in your setting.

Deep-dive Why is a model's effective context length shorter than its window, and what can you do about it?

STRING's account: with relative positions, offset \(i\) occurs \(f(i) = \sum_s \max(|s|-i, 0)\) times, so \(f(i) = L - i\) even for full-length sequences, and much lower for real length distributions. For a 2,048-token training length on SlimPajama, offsets of 1,536 and more make up under 5% of position indices. Controlled runs (TinyLlama 1.3B, 1T tokens) show effective length tracking the frequency of the corresponding offsets, and open models fail mostly when the needle is in the first third of the document (Llama 3.1: beyond about 90K). NoLiMa adds an attention-side account: without lexical overlap, retrieval degrades with length even at fixed offset. Remedies: train on sequences longer than the target (ProLong: 512K training helps at 64K; Qwen2.5-1M's 256K stage lifts 128K RULER from 83.8 to 87.6); upsample long documents; use tail-offset remapping at inference (STRING: Llama 3.1 70B RULER 66.6 to 81.7 at 128K; DCA in Qwen); architectures with local RoPE layers plus NoPE global layers, or sparse and compressed attention, so far offsets are handled by a few layers or by selection; and search RoPE factors with needle-focused objectives (LongRoPE2). Diagnose it with per-depth results.

Deep-dive Compare Ring Attention with all-gather context parallelism. Why did Llama 3 pick all-gather?

Both shard the sequence and compute exact attention. Ring: each device keeps its query block and rotates K/V blocks around a ring, overlapping the transfer with blockwise attention. Overlap requires block size \(c \ge F/B\) (FLOPs per second over link bytes per second), per-device activation memory is \(6bch\) bytes, independent of total length, and the minimum useful sequence per device is \(6c\): 6.2K tokens for A100s on NVLink, 149.5K for A100s on InfiniBand. All-gather: gather K and V, then compute local Q against them; the gather latency is exposed. Llama 3 split each sequence into \(2\times\)CP chunks (rank \(i\) gets chunks \(i\) and \(2\text{CP}-1-i\)) for causal load balance and chose all-gather for two reasons: arbitrary masks such as document masks are easy, and GQA makes K and V small compared with Q, while attention costs \(O(S^2)\) against \(O(S)\) for the gather, so the exposed latency is negligible. The trade is peak memory (full K and V on each device) against simplicity and mask support. With 8 KV heads and 128K tokens, K and V for one layer are 128K × 8 × 128 × 2 tensors of 2 bytes, 512 MiB per layer, small next to the compute.

Cheat sheet

  • Frequency view: \(\theta'_i = \theta_i/f_i\), \(f_i \in [1, s]\), \(s = L'/L\). PI: \(f_i = s\). NTK-aware: \(b' = b\,s^{d/(d-2)}\), \(f_i = s^{2i/(d-2)}\). YaRN: \(r_i = L/\lambda_i\), \(\gamma = \operatorname{clip}((r-\alpha)/(\beta-\alpha), 0, 1)\), \(\theta' = (1-\gamma)\theta/s + \gamma\theta\), \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 32\); temperature \(\sqrt{1/t} = 0.1\ln s + 1\) (1.139, 1.208, 1.369 for \(s = 4, 8, 40\); logits ×1.297, 1.459, 1.874).
  • Worked case (\(d_h = 128\), \(b = 10^4\), \(L = 4096\), \(s = 8\)): slowest wavelength 54,410 to 435,281 tokens; PI slows the fastest pair from 6.3 to 50.3 tokens per turn, the others leave it. YaRN: 21 pairs untouched, 25 blended, 18 divided by 8. NTK-aware \(b' = 82{,}685\); pair 45 extrapolated 1.81×.
  • Two YaRN ramps: paper linear in \(L/\lambda\); Hugging Face and DeepSeek-V3 code linear in pair index. \(\alpha\) is beta_slow, \(\beta\) is beta_fast.
  • Llama 3.1: base 500K; llama3: factor 8, low 1, high 4, \(L = 8192\): pairs 0 to 28 untouched, 29 to 34 blended, 35 to 63 divided by 8; the paper ramp with \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 4\), no temperature. factor is not \(L'/L\).
  • DeepSeek-V3: YaRN \(s = 40\), \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 32\) on the 64 RoPE dims, \(\text{mscale}^2 = 1.874\) on the whole logit, static; 2 × 1,000 steps (32K at batch 1,920, 128K at 480); 119K of 2,788K GPU-hours.
  • Recipes: Llama 3: 8K to 128K, 6 stages, ~800B tokens, gate = short-context recovery + needle, document masks. Qwen2.5-1M: 4K to 256K, base 10K, 1M, 1M, 5M, 10M, DCA + YaRN to 1M. DeepSeek-V4: 4K, 16K, 64K, 1M inside pretraining, dense first 1T tokens, sparse from 64K.
  • Costs, Llama-3-8B: prefill \(2N_{\text{mm}}T + 2^{18}T^2\): \(1.405\times10^{14}\) at 8K, \(6.471\times10^{15}\) at 128K (46×); attention overtakes weights at 57K; KV 1 GiB to 16 GiB; batch-1 decode 196 to 101 tok/s.
  • Data: 0.5B to 5B tokens unlock needles (Fu); 40B (ProLong) to 800B (Llama 3) for robust use. Upsample long per domain; 60% long (books:code 1:1) / 40% short; train longer than you evaluate.
  • Eval: perplexity is a regression alarm; NIAH saturates (Llama 3.1 70B: passkey 100, KV retrieval 2.2); RULER pass mark 85.6, most models effective at half their claim or less; NoLiMa: 11 of 13 models below half at 32K; HELMET: 7 categories; LongBench v2: human 53.7, best direct-answer model 50.1, o1-preview 57.7.
  • Sinks and position: a window cache without the first tokens explodes (PPL 5,158), four sink tokens restore 5.40, but that is not context extension; lost in the middle: 75.8 / 53.8 / 63.2 (GPT-3.5, closed book 56.1).
