Pretraining data

P0 ~60 min Prereqs: 01 Big picture, 02 Tokenization

A language model is a compressed copy of its training distribution, so the pipeline that turns a web crawl into tokens matters as much as the architecture. This page follows that pipeline stage by stage (text extraction, filtering, deduplication, model-based quality scores), then covers how mixture weights are chosen, why the last few percent of training use different data, what to do when unique tokens run out, how contamination is handled, and how labs decide any of this with experiments they can afford.

  • Evidence hygiene: contamination detection, proxy-model experiments, and why perplexity alone misleads.

The pipeline and why it is ordered this way

A web pipeline orders its filters by cost: cheap, per-document, high-recall steps first, then steps that need global state (deduplication), then steps that need a model. Every threshold is justified by an ablation (see How labs make data decisions). This stage is what people mean by data cleaning 数据清洗.

URL filtering and language identification

A URL blocklist drops adult content before parsing [FineWeb, §3.3]; Llama 3 also removes PII-heavy and unsafe domains [§3.1.1]. Language identification 语言识别 is a fastText classifier over character n-grams. FineWeb keeps English at score at least 0.65, which passes about 40% of documents [FineWeb2, §4.1]; C4's langdetect at 0.99 also drops noisy English. One threshold does not fit all languages: FineWeb2 found Arabic and Russian prefer cutoffs above 0.8 while Swahili is best near 0.3, and uses a per-language cutoff \(\max\{0.3, \min\{0.9, \text{median} - \text{std}\}\}\) [§4.2].

Deduplication

Deduplication 去重 matters for three reasons, all measured by Lee et al. [Lee et al. 2021]. Memorization: a model trained on C4 emitted memorized 50-token sequences in 1.9% of unprompted generated tokens, and 0.19% after near-duplicate removal, about 10× less. Efficiency: datasets shrank by up to 19% at the same or better accuracy. Clean evaluation: 4.6% of C4's validation examples had a near-duplicate in training, and one 61-word sentence occurred 61,036 times. Most duplicates are templated pages differing by a city name or a price, so exact matching misses them. Not everyone sees a downstream benefit: Muennighoff et al. found none in a data-constrained setting [§7].

Exact deduplication

  • Document: hash the normalized text, keep one copy per hash. Catches mirrors and scraped copies.
  • Line or paragraph: Llama 3 removes any line seen more than 6 times in a bucket of 30M documents, killing menus and cookie notices [§3.1.1]. A Bloom filter (probabilistic set, no false negatives) scales this to 10TB and beyond.
  • Substring: build a suffix array (all suffix start positions of the concatenated corpus, sorted). A sequence occurring twice makes two adjacent suffixes with a long common prefix: for banana the sorted suffixes are a, ana, anana, banana, na, nana, and ana and anana share 3 characters. One scan finds every repeat of at least \(k\) tokens. Lee et al. use \(k=50\) at 8 bytes per token (1T tokens need 8TB); on C4 it marks 7.18% of tokens as repeated [§4.1].

DCLM compared the options at 1B-1x: Bloom filter +2.1 CORE over no dedup, suffix array +1.9, exact +1.3, MinHash +0.9, gains that do not add and sit near run-to-run noise [App. L, Tab. 17].

Near-duplicates: MinHash and LSH

Treat each document as a set of shingles (overlapping word \(n\)-grams: \(n=5\) in FineWeb and Lee et al., 13 in Gopher). Similarity is the Jaccard index \(J(A,B) = |A \cap B| / |A \cup B|\). We compress each set to a short signature whose agreement rate estimates \(J\).

MinHash 最小哈希. For a random permutation \(\pi\) of all shingles let \(h_\pi(A) = \min_{x \in A} \pi(x)\). The element of \(A \cup B\) that \(\pi\) ranks first is uniform over the union, and \(h_\pi(A) = h_\pi(B)\) exactly when it lies in \(A \cap B\). Hence

\[ \Pr\big[h_\pi(A) = h_\pi(B)\big] = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} = J(A,B) \]
One minhash agrees with probability J

Use \(k\) independent hashes (in practice \(h_i(x) = (a_i x + b_i) \bmod p\)). The fraction of agreeing positions is an unbiased estimate of \(J\) with standard error \(\sqrt{J(1-J)/k}\): at \(J=0.8\), 0.038 for \(k=112\) and 0.004 for \(k=9{,}000\).

Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) 局部敏感哈希. Comparing all pairs is \(O(N^2)\), hopeless for \(10^{10}\) documents. Split the \(k\) signature rows into \(b\) bands of \(r\) rows (\(k = b\,r\)), hash each band to a bucket, and call two documents a candidate pair if they share a bucket in any band. One row agrees with probability \(J\); a whole band with \(J^r\); at least one of \(b\) bands with

\[ P_{\text{candidate}}(J) = 1 - \left(1 - J^{r}\right)^{b} \]
The LSH S-curve. Steepest near J* ≈ (1/b)^(1/r)

At \(J^* = (1/b)^{1/r}\) we have \(J^{*r} = 1/b\), so \(P = 1-(1-1/b)^b \approx 1 - 1/e = 0.63\): the middle of the transition, and how you read a threshold off \((b, r)\).

Choosing bands and rows. Fix the hash budget \(k\) first (compute and memory are linear in it). Then minimize a weighted sum of the false-positive area \(\int_0^{t} P(s)\,ds\) and the false-negative area \(\int_t^{1} (1-P(s))\,ds\) around the target threshold \(t\). For \(k=128\) and equal weights this gives 25 × 5 for \(t=0.5\) and 9 × 13 for \(t=0.8\) (computed with the drill's reference solution). The weights say what an error costs: a missed duplicate leaves one redundant document, a false positive deletes a distinct one, so pipelines lean toward precision. Candidate pairs are then graph edges: take connected components (union-find) and keep one document per component. This is transitive, so A~B and B~C collapse all three. Storage is the practical limit: \(10^{10}\) documents × 112 values × 8 bytes is 8.96TB.

import hashlib, random
import numpy as np

P = (1 << 61) - 1  # Mersenne prime: universal hash h_i(x) = (a_i x + b_i) mod P

def minhash(shingle_set, k=112, seed=0):
    rng = random.Random(seed)                      # same seed for every document
    a = [rng.randrange(1, P) for _ in range(k)]
    b = [rng.randrange(P) for _ in range(k)]
    xs = [int.from_bytes(hashlib.blake2b(s.encode(), digest_size=8).digest(), "big") % P
          for s in shingle_set]                    # stable 61-bit id per shingle (not hash())
    return np.array([min((ai * x + bi) % P for x in xs) for ai, bi in zip(a, b)],
                    dtype=np.uint64)               # (k,): signature[i] = min_x h_i(x)

def lsh_pairs(sigs, bands=14, rows=8):             # sigs: (N, bands * rows)
    pairs = set()
    for j in range(bands):
        buckets = {}
        for i, row in enumerate(sigs[:, j * rows:(j + 1) * rows]):
            buckets.setdefault(row.tobytes(), []).append(i)   # equal band content = same bucket
        for ids in buckets.values():
            pairs.update((x, y) for x in ids for y in ids if x < y)
    return pairs                                   # then union-find, keep one per component

Global versus per-snapshot deduplication

The obvious plan is to deduplicate across the whole corpus. FineWeb tried it [§3.4, Fig. 3 to 5]: MinHash over all 96 snapshots (newest first) removed up to 90% of the oldest ones and left 4T tokens, and a model on a 350B-token sample barely improved on no dedup and stayed far below RefinedWeb. On the 2013-48 snapshot the globally kept data (about 31B tokens, 10%) was worse than the removed data (460B tokens; 171B after per-snapshot dedup): more ads, keyword lists and bad formatting. Per-snapshot dedup kept 20T tokens and matched RefinedWeb.

FineWeb's hypothesis: dedup pays off by removing huge duplicate clusters, while collapsing clusters under about 100 copies backfires, because what survives is content that appeared exactly once, unique because nobody else wanted it. Duplicate count is a weak quality signal, not only a nuisance.

This is unsettled. Everyone agrees that huge duplicate clusters must go and that duplicate counts are worth keeping as metadata.

GPT-3's soft threshold. NumPy's pareto(a) draws \(X\) with \(\Pr[X > x] = (1+x)^{-a}\), so a document with score \(s\) is kept with probability \(\Pr[X > 1-s] = (2-s)^{-9}\): 1.0 at \(s=1\), 0.42 at 0.9, 0.19 at 0.8, 0.026 at 0.5. Steep but non-zero, so "some documents that were out of distribution" still get in [GPT-3, App. A].

FineWeb-Edu. An LLM is too slow to score 15T tokens, so it labels a sample and a small regressor imitates it on frozen embeddings (410k pages to train, 50k to validate, F1 82% at the "score at least 3" decision; 6,000 H100 hours over FineWeb). Distilling an LLM's judgment into a cheap scorer is now the standard pattern. On 350B-token runs of a 1.71B model, FineWeb-Edu lifted MMLU from 33% to 37% and ARC from 46% to 57%, and reached 33.6% MMLU with 38B tokens where the next-best dataset needed about 300B; threshold 3 beat 2 and 4 [FineWeb, §4].

DCLM ran the most controlled comparison. At 1B-1x a fastText classifier scored CORE 30.2 against 27.5 for heuristics alone, ahead of perplexity filtering (29.0), top-k logits (29.2), AskLLM (28.6), a BGE-embedding classifier (27.2), SemDedup (27.1) and PageRank (26.1). The positives mattered: at 7B-1x instruction data plus ELI5 reached CORE 41.0 against 37.5 for GPT-3-style positives (Wikipedia, OpenWebText2, books) and 35.7 for Wikipedia alone, and the top 10% beat the top 15% (39.8) and top 20% (38.7). The 7B model trained on 2.6T tokens reached 64% MMLU, near Llama 3 8B (66%) with 6.6× less compute [Tab. 4, 5].

What the filters cost

  • Topic sculpting. A classifier changes the topic mix, not just the quality. FineWeb-Edu favors education, history and politics pages and downsamples business, entertainment and travel; its perplexity is worse on broad web (C4, Twitter, Reddit) and better on Wikipedia, academic text and code [FineWeb, §4].
  • Unique-token starvation. Keeping the top 10% suits a 1T to 2.6T horizon, but a 15T run then repeats it many times. Nemotron-CC counts about 1.0T unique tokens in DCLM and 1.1T in FineWeb-Edu-2, and responds by (i) ensembling three classifiers, whose high-quality sets overlap by only 10.1%, (ii) skipping the heuristic filters on documents the classifier trusts (they removed 18.1% of high-quality tokens), and (iii) rephrasing (below) [§2, Tab. 8]. Olmo 3 drops the flat cutoff: discard the bottom of the score distribution and repeat the top up to 7× [§3.4.4].
  • Benchmark alignment. DCLM's positives are instruction-formatted, so the corpus resembles benchmark prompts. Nemotron-CC therefore sets its tiers by short annealing runs rather than trusting raw scores.
  • Interaction with mixing. Adding curated sources helped weakly filtered Common Crawl subsets by +1.4 to +2.2 CORE but hurt DCLM-Baseline by 1.2 (1B-1x, Tab. 6). Strong filtering already captures what they offered.
From your jet tagger

If a tagger's score is correlated with jet mass, cutting on it sculpts the mass spectrum, which is why decorrelation methods exist. A quality classifier trained on textbook-style positives sculpts topic and style the same way. The analog of checking the mass distribution before and after a cut is to compare topic histograms before and after filtering, and to evaluate on the domains the filter disfavors.

Mixtures: choosing domain weights

A corpus is a set of domains \(i\) (web, code, math, books, ...) with \(n_i\) available tokens each. The mixture 数据配比 is the vector of sampling weights \(w_i\), summing to 1. A weight is not a share of the corpus: with \(D\) training tokens, domain \(i\) is seen \(w_i D / n_i\) times. Small high-quality domains get repeated; huge mediocre ones are barely touched. Ways to set the weights

Scaling-law ablations (Llama 3). Meta downsampled over-represented web categories such as arts and entertainment, trained small models on candidate mixes to predict large-model performance, picked one and verified it at larger scale. The final mix is about 50% general knowledge, 25% math and reasoning, 17% code and 8% multilingual [§3.1.2]: 7.5T, 3.75T, 2.55T and 1.2T of 15T tokens. It also shifted during training toward more non-English data and upsampled math (§3.4.1).

DoReMi: a proxy with group DRO. DoReMi [Xie et al. 2023] seeks weights that make a small model good on every domain without looking at downstream tasks. Train a reference model on baseline weights, then a same-size proxy whose domain weights \(\alpha\) are updated online to maximize the proxy's excess loss over the reference:

\[ \min_\theta \max_{\alpha \in \Delta^k} \; \sum_{i=1}^{k} \alpha_i \Bigg[ \frac{1}{\sum_{x \in D_i}|x|} \sum_{x \in D_i} \big( \ell_\theta(x) - \ell_{\text{ref}}(x) \big) \Bigg] \]

Per-token excess loss is clipped at zero and \(\alpha \leftarrow \alpha \odot \exp(\eta \lambda)\) is renormalized and smoothed; the average of \(\alpha\) over training is returned (Alg. 1). The reference loss marks how learnable a domain is: where the proxy lags the reference there is headroom, while hopelessly high-entropy or trivially easy domains get less. On the Pile (22 domains) a 280M proxy set weights for an 8B model: +6.5 points of one-shot accuracy, the baseline's accuracy in 75k steps instead of 200k (2.6× faster), lower perplexity on all 22 domains, at 8% extra FLOPs. Pile-CC went from 0.112 to 0.606 and ArXiv from 0.105 to 0.004. Caveat: a 1B proxy gave different weights, so the result depends on the reference model and domain definitions [§3, §4, §6].

RegMix: regression on many tiny runs. RegMix [Liu et al. 2024] samples 512 mixtures from a Dirichlet around the natural distribution, trains a 1M-parameter model on 1B tokens for each, fits gradient-boosted trees from weights to target loss (validation loss on the Pile's web subset), and searches the fit for the best mix. The bet is rank invariance: mixtures rank the same at small and large scale. It held: Spearman correlation between predicted and actual rank of unseen mixtures was 97.1 for 1B models on 25B tokens. The 512 runs cost about 2% of one 1B run, and the whole method about 10% of DoReMi's FLOPs (3.5e18 versus 3.7e19). Average score: 47.3 for RegMix, 46.8 for DoReMi, 45.1 for the Pile's hand-picked weights. Web-text validation loss correlated with downstream accuracy far better than Wikipedia loss did, and domain interactions were often counterintuitive, which is the argument for fitting a model instead of reasoning by hand [§4, §5].

Constrained swarms and instance-level mixing. Olmo 3 runs the RegMix recipe with 30M-parameter models on 3B tokens, a swarm about 5× the domain count, and a search that caps repetition at about 4 to 7× at the 6T budget; "conditional mixing" freezes the previous optimum as one virtual domain so a late source does not force a new swarm [§3.4.4]. Qwen3 labels over 30T tokens (educational value, field, domain, safety) and optimizes the mix at the instance level with proxy ablations [§3.1]. Data mixing laws fit explicit functional forms instead of trees [Ye et al. 2024].

Code, math and language shares

Every recent report upweights reasoning domains. Llama 3 gives math 25% and code 17%, with classifiers that find math and code pages inside the crawl [§3.1.1]; DeepSeek-V3 raised the math and code ratio over V2 [§4.1]. Code is nearly free for language performance: Muennighoff et al. swapped up to 50% of natural-language tokens for Python with no drop on natural-language tasks [§7]. Llama 3 set its 8% multilingual share "experimentally". At the long tail there is little web: of FineWeb2's 1,868 language-script pairs, 70% have more than half their documents from Bible- or Wikipedia-related domains [§5].

Annealing and mid-training

Most learning-rate schedules end with a decay to a small value or to zero (page 14). Recent reports use that decay as a second lever: in the last few percent of training they switch to a smaller, cleaner, more task-like data mix. Meta calls this annealing 退火; OLMo and GLM call the stage mid-training 中期训练, Qwen3 a "reasoning stage". It is now standard, and interviewers ask why it works.

The evidence

Llama 3 linearly annealed the 405B model's learning rate to zero over its final 40M tokens at 128K context on upsampled very high-quality data, and averaged checkpoints [§3.4.3]. Annealing the 8B model on small amounts of code and math data improved GSM8K by 24.0% and MATH by 6.4% (validation sets, with the GSM8K and MATH training sets in the annealing data), but the effect at 405B was negligible (§3.1.3).

OLMo 2 spends 5% to 10% of FLOPs on stage 2. For 7B, three 50B-token anneals with different data orders are averaged ("souping") [§2.3]. The effect (Tab. 9): MMLU 59.8 to 63.7, ARC-C 72.6 to 79.8, DROP 40.7 to 60.8, GSM8K 24.1 to 67.5. Its ablation (50B-token anneals from a 4T checkpoint, Tab. 11) separates decay from data:

Others follow the pattern. Qwen3's reasoning stage is about 5T tokens after 30T+, with more STEM, code, reasoning and synthetic data and accelerated decay [§3.2]. Olmo 3 builds a 100B-token mix from a 2T pool (math, code, QA, instruction data, reasoning traces) and tests a candidate mix's post-trainability with SFT [§2, §3.5]. The boundary with post-training (page 19) is blurring on the data side.

Annealing as an experiment

Annealing also evaluates small datasets: 5B new tokens cannot move the loss at pretraining proportions. Meta's recipe: take an 8B checkpoint that is 50% trained, decay the learning rate linearly to zero over 40B tokens with 30% candidate data and 70% default mix, and compare benchmarks [Llama 3, §3.1.3]. Nemotron-CC scored each of its 20 quality buckets this way (70%-trained 8B, 50B tokens, 34% bucket), found the top bucket far better than the rest, and used it to define five tiers [§2.2, App. C]. The limit: the measurement is conditional on the late-stage regime, and benchmark-like data looks better than it is.

Repetition, synthetic data and running out of tokens

Aggressive filtering, deduplication and long runs push toward the same wall: the supply of unique high-quality tokens. You can repeat what you have or manufacture more. Both have measured limits.

How many epochs are nearly free

Muennighoff et al. trained more than 400 models (up to 9B parameters and 900B tokens, up to 1,500 epochs) on C4 and other corpora [Muennighoff et al. 2023]. With total tokens \(D\) drawn from \(U\) unique tokens, let \(R_D = D/U - 1\) be the number of repeats. Each repeat is worth a geometrically shrinking fraction of a fresh token, giving an effective data size (§3.1):

\[ D' = U + U\,R_D^{*}\left(1 - e^{-R_D / R_D^{*}}\right) \]
Repeated tokens decay in value; R*_D ≈ 15 is fitted

For small \(R_D\), \(D' \approx D\): repetition is free. As \(R_D \to \infty\), \(D' \to U(1 + R_D^*)\), so no number of epochs is worth more than about 16 fresh passes; \(R^*_D \approx 15\) is the "half-life". The headline result: an 8.7B model trained for 4 epochs on 44B unique tokens had only 0.5% higher validation loss than one trained on 178B unique tokens at the same compute, and downstream accuracy did not differ significantly up to about 4 epochs. Excess parameters lose value faster than repeated data, so when data-constrained, scale epochs faster than parameters: at \(9.3 \times 10^{21}\) FLOPs with 25B unique tokens the optimal model with 27% fewer parameters beat the Chinchilla-allocated one [§5, §6].

The same paper adds two moves: fill with code (up to 50% of natural-language tokens replaced by Python, no deterioration, §7) and filter selectively (perplexity filtering helped, deduplication did not).

Synthetic data: rephrasing and textbooks

Synthetic data 合成数据 in pretraining comes in three kinds: rephrasing real documents, generating textbook-style text from scratch, and task-shaped data (QA, reasoning traces) used mostly in mid-training.

WRAP. Maini et al. have a frozen Mistral-7B-Instruct rephrase C4 documents in several styles (easy, Wikipedia-like, terse, question-answer), at most 300 tokens at a time because longer inputs lost information, and train on a 1:1 real:rephrased mix [WRAP, §3]. For 1.3B models, real C4 plus rephrases (85B real tokens) averaged 49.4 on eight zero-shot tasks versus 47.4 for the same real tokens alone, with about 3× faster learning. Their caveats: synthetic data cannot impart new knowledge, only speed up learning of what the source contained; synthetic-only training hurt on some Pile domains; a small rephraser (Qwen-1.8B, Mistral-7B) sufficed while fine-tuned T5-base was much worse.

Nemotron-CC and Kimi K2 at scale. Nemotron-CC rephrased low-quality documents into Wikipedia style to remove noise, and high-quality ones four more ways (question-answer pairs, distilled passages, extracted knowledge, knowledge lists) to make fresh unique tokens, using Mistral NeMo 12B: 1.9T synthetic of 6.3T total [§2.3]. Kimi K2 rephrases knowledge text in varied styles chunk by chunk, each conditioned on the previous output, with a fidelity check against the source. On SimpleQA with an early checkpoint, raw text for 10 epochs scored 23.76, one rephrasing for 10 epochs 27.39, and ten rephrasings seen once 28.94; in production each corpus is rephrased at most twice [§2.2, Tab. 1].

Cost check. Generating 1.9T tokens with a 12B model costs at least \(2 \cdot 12 \times 10^9 \cdot 1.9 \times 10^{12} \approx 4.6 \times 10^{22}\) FLOPs (generated tokens only). Training an 8B model on 15T tokens is \(6 \cdot 8.03 \times 10^9 \cdot 15 \times 10^{12} \approx 7.2 \times 10^{23}\). So rephrasing at this scale is at least about 6% of the run it feeds.

Textbooks from scratch: phi-1. Gunasekar et al. trained a 1.3B code model on about 6B tokens of web code chosen by a classifier (GPT-4 rated about 100k files for "educational value"; a random forest on embeddings scored the rest), under 1B tokens of GPT-3.5 "textbooks" and a 180M-token exercise set [§2]. Filtering alone lifted a 350M model from 12.19% to 17.68% HumanEval and textbooks to 20.12%; phi-1 reached 50.6% HumanEval and 55.5% MBPP after about 8 passes over 7B tokens.

Model collapse

Model collapse 模型崩溃 is the degradation from training recursively on a model's own samples: the distribution's tails vanish first [Shumailov et al. 2023]. Gerstgrasser et al. separate two regimes. If each generation replaces the data with synthetic samples, test error grows (in linear regression, \(\sigma^2 d\, n/(T-d-1)\)). If synthetic data accumulates next to the original real data, error stays bounded, at most \(\sigma^2 d/(T-d-1) \cdot \pi^2/6\), and language-model experiments up to 125M parameters agree [§2.1, Thm. 2]. So never replace real data with synthetic. Labs also guard against synthetic text arriving through the crawl: DeepSeek-V4 filters "batched auto-generated and templated content" [§4.1], and DCLM drops all Common Crawl from 2023 on [App. E].

Contamination

Data contamination 数据污染 means benchmark test items, or close variants, are in the training data, so a score measures memorization. The web holds benchmark files, answer keys, exam sites and discussions of both, and synthetic data from a model that saw a benchmark can carry it along. Evaluation-side treatment (dated benchmarks, held-out suites) is on page 18; this is the data side.

Detecting overlap

The standard tool is n-gram overlap between training documents and evaluation examples. Implementations differ in \(n\), in what a match triggers, and in what counts as a "contaminated example":

  • GPT-3. Find 13-gram matches, remove the colliding 13-gram plus a 200-character window, discard leftovers under 200 characters, drop documents split into more than 10 pieces, and ignore 13-grams found in more than 10 documents (boilerplate). A bug made the filter miss many overlaps and retraining was unaffordable, so OpenAI compared scores on the whole benchmark and on a "clean" subset afterwards [GPT-3, §4, App. C].
  • Llama 3. An example is contaminated if at least a fraction \(T_D\) of its tokens lie in 8-grams found in the pretraining corpus, with \(T_D\) chosen per benchmark to maximize the estimated gain (clean-subset versus whole-set score). HellaSwag: 85% flagged, gain about 14 to 15 points at all three sizes; PIQA 55%, about 8 points; Natural Questions 52%, about 1 point; GSM8K 41%, roughly 0 to 1. For MMLU, MMLU-Pro, HumanEval and MBPP the overlap is so high that no gain can be estimated [§5.1.4, Tab. 15].
  • DCLM. Flag a page if it contains an MMLU or HellaSwag question and at least one answer option. Only 0.007% of DCLM-Baseline was flagged (Dolma 1.7: 0.001%, FineWeb-Edu: 0.009%), and training without the overlaps did not lower scores (MMLU 51.8 to 52.7 at 7B-2x) [§4.6, App. O].
  • Olmo 3. The decon tool samples n-grams at a stride and expands hits to neighbors before dropping a document. It is applied to mid-training and long-context data because memorization is strongest near the end of training [§3.5.3].
  • Beyond n-grams (phi-1). A 13-gram search found only 4 overlaps between HumanEval and phi-1's synthetic exercises, all false positives. Embedding plus AST similarity found many close problems; after pruning over 40% of the exercise set, phi-1 still scored 45.1% on HumanEval against StarCoder-Prompted's 41.5% [§5].

Limits, and what labs do

Paraphrase and translation escape n-gram matching (phi-1), and short items (MMLU, HumanEval) produce false positives, so thresholds are tuned by hand. Effect size varies: catastrophic for HellaSwag, minor for a Natural Questions question without its answer, and large models absorb less. The clean-subset comparison is biased too, since the clean subset may be easier or harder (GPT-3 says so). Removal is not free: it shifts the distribution. And the field disagrees: DCLM and Nemotron-CC release without decontaminating, while Olmo 3 and Gemma 4 decontaminate deliberately [Gemma 4, §2.4].

A defensible practice: (1) n-gram decontaminate against every benchmark in your suite before training, at 8 to 13 tokens plus whole-question matching; (2) check late-stage and synthetic data first; (3) keep a private held-out set that never touches the web; (4) report a clean-subset comparison beside headline numbers.

From your jet tagger

Train-test leakage in a tagger is the same event: a jet or Monte Carlo file appearing in both splits under different reconstructions. The fix is to split on the event identifier before any featurization. Web text has no event identifier, and near-duplicate detection (n-gram overlap, MinHash) is how you rebuild one, which is why the same machinery serves both deduplication and decontamination.

How labs make data decisions

Nobody trains a 15T-token model per candidate dataset, so every decision above comes from a smaller experiment.

Proxy models and how far they transfer

DCLM compared rankings of ten curation methods at smaller scales with the 7B-1x ranking: correlations 0.838 for 400M-1x, 0.956 for 1B-1x and 0.982 for 3B-1x [§3.2]. DataDecide made the question systematic with 1,050 models (25 corpora × 14 sizes from 4M to 1B × 3 seeds, 100 tokens per parameter) [DataDecide, §2, §3]:

  • Ranking corpora at a single small size (150M) picks the right winner in about 80% of pairwise comparisons at 1B, and eight scaling-law extrapolation baselines did not do better.
  • Run-to-run noise is real: seed standard deviation at 1B can reach 2 accuracy points, so use multiple seeds.
  • A continuous metric (probability of the correct answer) beats accuracy at small scale: with it MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, MBPP and HumanEval were more than 80% predictable at 1B from 0.01% of the target compute. Tasks differ: HellaSwag needs more compute and SocialIQA is hard at every scale.

Early-signal benchmarks and compute-matched ablations

A benchmark helps only if it clears noise at your model size. FineWeb chose tasks by three rules: low variance across random samples of the same data, near-monotonic improvement during training, and above-chance scores at the ablation scale [§3.1]; DCLM's CORE (22 tasks, centered at chance) followed the same logic. MMLU is the cautionary case: across fastText variants at 7B-1x it sits between about 24 and 29 (chance is 25) [DCLM, Tab. 5]. Ablations must be compute-matched (only the data differs), with two seeds per variant as in FineWeb. Two traps: some effects appear only at scale (FineWeb ran its dedup studies at 350B tokens because 28B was too small to show them [App. E.2]; Gopher says its ablations underestimate dedup's benefit [App. A.3.2]), and equal tokens is the wrong constraint for a long run, since an aggressive filter can win at equal tokens yet leave too few unique tokens for 15T.

Why perplexity alone misleads

  1. It depends on the reference distribution. In RegMix, validation loss on web text tracked downstream accuracy well and loss on Wikipedia did not [§5].
  2. It is not comparable across training distributions. Muennighoff et al. stopped using loss to compare data strategies for that reason.
  3. Better data can have worse perplexity. FineWeb-Edu is worse than FineWeb on broad web sources yet better on MMLU (above).
  4. Low perplexity is not quality. Repetitive text is easy to predict, and DCLM's perplexity filter (CORE 29.0) trailed its fastText filter (30.2).

The remedy is a small panel: in-distribution loss on the domain you care about, an early-signal task suite, and checks on the domains the change may hurt. A protocol that follows: train compute-matched proxies of each variant at a size where benchmarks clear the noise floor, with at least two seeds; confirm the finalists at a second scale; test late-stage data by annealing; and remember that effects needing scale (deduplication, repetition, unique-token supply) can be invisible or misleading in small runs, that data recipes can cross over with scale, and that contamination can make a change look better than it is.

Common misconceptions

  • "Filter harder and quality always goes up." Filtering only removes tokens. Aggressive filters win at 1T to 2.6T tokens, but leave about 1T unique tokens, which starves a 15T run (Nemotron-CC). The right cutoff depends on the training horizon.
  • "Deduplicate as globally as possible." FineWeb's global MinHash left 4T tokens that trained worse than per-snapshot dedup's 20T. Duplicate counts carry information (FineWeb2 upsamples by them). Remove huge clusters and think twice about small ones.
  • "A quality classifier measures quality." It measures similarity to its positives and moves the topic mix (FineWeb-Edu favors education and history). Compare topic histograms before and after, and evaluate on the domains it disfavors.
  • "The mixture is a property of the corpus." Weights are in tokens, so they depend on the tokenizer; they change with compute budget and domain definitions; and they are not corpus shares, since domain \(i\) is seen \(w_i D/n_i\) times.
  • "Annealing is just a lower learning rate." In OLMo 2, decay alone gave +4.4 OLMES and no math gain; the curated data added +19.5 on the math dev set. It is two interventions, and the data half can teach a benchmark's format.
  • "Repeating data is harmful" or "worthless." Neither. About 4 epochs behaves almost like fresh data (0.5% loss difference at 8.7B), the half-life is about 15 epochs, and returns then decay toward a ceiling of \(U(1+R^*_D)\).
  • "Synthetic data means collapse" or "is free knowledge." Collapse is a replace-real-data failure; accumulating synthetic beside real data keeps error bounded. Rephrasing speeds up learning of what the source contains (WRAP, K2) but adds no facts and can add errors.
  • "N-gram decontamination solves contamination." It misses paraphrases and translations and struggles on short items; for MMLU and HumanEval, Llama 3's 8-gram overlap was too high to estimate any effect. It lowers risk; it does not certify a benchmark.

Interview questions

Warm-up What is the difference between WARC and WET files, and why do most modern pipelines re-extract text from HTML?

WARC stores the raw crawl (HTTP responses with full HTML). WET stores plain text from Common Crawl's generic extractor, which keeps menus, footers and cookie banners and flattens code and math. Labs re-extract from WARC with trafilatura, resiliparse, Justext or a custom parser.

At DCLM's 1B-1x scale CORE was 24.1 (resiliparse) and 24.5 (trafilatura) versus 20.7 for WET, and resiliparse is 8× faster. The cost is CPU over petabytes of HTML, and the extractor also sets yield: Justext gave 28.6% more high-quality tokens than trafilatura in Nemotron-CC.

Warm-up Name five Gopher quality rules with thresholds. What does each catch?

(1) 50 to 100,000 words: stubs and dumps. (2) Mean word length 3 to 10: URL and symbol soup. (3) "#" or ellipsis-to-word ratio above 0.1: hashtag spam. (4) More than 90% of lines bulleted, or more than 30% ending in an ellipsis: menus and search-result pages. (5) At least 80% of words containing a letter: numeric tables. (6) At least 2 of {the, be, to, of, and, that, have, with}: "English" pages with no English. Repetition rules (duplicate lines above 30%, top 2-gram above 20% of characters) catch templated pages.

DeepMind chose transparent statistics over a learned classifier to avoid biasing against dialects. The rules work in combination: a navigation page passes the length rule and fails the letter and stop-word rules.

Warm-up Why deduplicate pretraining data? Give numbers.

Lee et al. on C4: memorized 50-token sequences appeared in 1.9% of unprompted generated tokens versus 0.19% after near-duplicate removal (about 10× less); datasets shrank by up to 19% at the same or better accuracy; and 4.6% of C4's validation examples had a near-duplicate in training, one 61-word sentence occurring 61,036 times. Duplicates are mostly templated pages, so exact hashing is not enough.

Caveats: Muennighoff et al. saw no downstream gain from dedup in a data-constrained setting, and global over-deduplication hurt in FineWeb (see the deep-dive).

Core Design a pipeline to turn Common Crawl into a high-quality pretraining corpus.

Order stages by cost. (1) WARC download. (2) URL and domain filters. (3) HTML to text with a main-content extractor. (4) fastText language ID, per-language thresholds. (5) Heuristics (Gopher and repetition rules, C4 rules minus its punctuation rule, which alone drops 30% of tokens), thresholds tuned from histograms of good and bad subsets. (6) Dedup: exact line or URL, then MinHash (FineWeb: 14 × 8 on 5-grams); decide global versus per-snapshot and keep cluster sizes. (7) PII and safety. (8) A quality model (fastText on instruction-like positives, or a distilled LLM rubric) as cutoff or upsampling weight. (9) Separate code, math and per-language pipelines. (10) Decontaminate. (11) Mix, tokenize, shard.

Measure each step with compute-matched proxies (FineWeb: 1.7B models on 28B tokens for filters, 350B for dedup, two seeds) and track yield: FineWeb 36T, 20T after per-snapshot dedup, 15T final; DCLM 240T to 3.8T. For long horizons track unique tokens, and budget the expensive steps (FineWeb-Edu's classifier: 6,000 H100 hours).

Core Explain MinHash LSH and how you would choose bands and rows.

Represent documents as sets of word \(n\)-gram shingles. For a random permutation, the minimum over \(A\) equals the minimum over \(B\) exactly when the first element of \(A \cup B\) lies in \(A \cap B\), probability \(J\). A signature of \(k\) minhashes estimates \(J\) with standard error \(\sqrt{J(1-J)/k}\).

Split \(k = b\,r\) rows into \(b\) bands of \(r\). A band matches with probability \(J^r\), so a pair becomes a candidate with probability \(1-(1-J^r)^b\), an S-curve steepest near \((1/b)^{1/r}\). Fix \(k\) from the cost budget, then pick the factorization minimizing weighted false-positive area \(\int_0^t P\) and false-negative area \(\int_t^1 (1-P)\), weighting precision more since a false positive deletes a distinct document. FineWeb's 14 × 8 gives \(P = 0.053, 0.565, 0.772, 0.924\) at \(J = 0.5, 0.7, 0.75, 0.8\). Then take connected components and keep one document each.

Core Calculate: with 128 minhashes, compare 16 bands × 8 rows and 32 bands × 4 rows at \(J = 0.5\) and \(J = 0.8\). Which do you use for a 0.8 duplicate threshold?

16 × 8: at \(J=0.5\), \(0.5^8 = 0.0039\), \(P = 1-(0.9961)^{16} = 0.061\); at \(J=0.8\), \(0.8^8 = 0.168\), \(P = 1-(0.832)^{16} = 0.947\). Threshold \((1/16)^{1/8} = 0.71\).

32 × 4: at \(J=0.5\), \(0.5^4 = 0.0625\), \(P = 1-(0.9375)^{32} = 0.873\); at \(J=0.8\), \(0.8^4 = 0.41\), \(P \approx 1.000\). Threshold \((1/32)^{1/4} = 0.42\).

Use 16 × 8: it catches 95% of \(J=0.8\) pairs and passes only 6% at \(J=0.5\). The 32 × 4 setting passes 87% of \(J=0.5\) pairs and would delete many distinct documents without exact verification. For more recall use fewer rows: 21 × 6 gives 0.998 at \(J=0.8\) but 0.28 at \(J=0.5\).

Core How do you decide the mixture proportions of web, code, math and books?

Weights set epochs: domain \(i\) is seen \(w_i D/n_i\) times, so small high-quality sources get repeated (Wikipedia: 1.5 epochs in Gopher, 3.4 in GPT-3) while web is seen less than once. Cap repetition using data-constrained results (about 4 epochs is nearly free).

Then measure on proxies: a hand sweep (Gopher, seven combinations); scaling-law prediction from small runs (Llama 3: 50% general, 25% math and reasoning, 17% code, 8% multilingual); DoReMi (group-DRO proxy of 280M for an 8B model: +6.5 points, 2.6× faster, 8% extra compute); RegMix (regression on 512 tiny runs, Spearman 97.1 at 1B, about 10% of DoReMi's compute). Verify the winner at larger scale.

Caveats: the answer depends on the metric optimized (RegMix used web-text loss), the domain partition, the tokenizer and the budget; mixing other sources into strongly filtered DCLM-Baseline cost 1.2 CORE at 1B-1x; and mixes change during training (Llama 3 upsampled math and non-English later).

Core What is annealing (mid-training), and why does it help?

In the last stretch the learning rate decays to zero and the mix shifts to a smaller, higher-quality, more task-like set. Llama 3 annealed its final 40M tokens at 128K context; OLMo 2 spends 5% to 10% of FLOPs on a 50B-token stage; Qwen3's stage is about 5T tokens of STEM, code, reasoning and synthetic data.

The evidence separates two effects. OLMo 2: decay alone gives +4.4 OLMES and no math gain; curated data adds +1.7 OLMES and +19.5 on a math dev set; end to end, 7B goes from 59.8 to 63.7 MMLU and 24.1 to 67.5 GSM8K. Why is not fully settled: at low learning rate the model can fit fine structure, and late data is not diluted by trillions of web tokens. Caveats: gains can be format learning (Llama's annealing data included GSM8K and MATH training splits; the effect vanished at 405B), and benchmark-shaped data invites contamination. Annealing also evaluates datasets: decay a half-trained model on 30% to 34% candidate data (Llama 3, Nemotron-CC).

Core Compare FineWeb-Edu and DCLM quality filtering. What are the risks?

FineWeb-Edu: Llama-3-70B-Instruct scores 460k pages 0 to 5 for educational value, a linear head on frozen embeddings imitates it, and score at least 3 is kept (1.3T of 15T tokens); at 1.71B and 350B tokens MMLU rose from 33 to 37 and ARC from 46 to 57. DCLM: a fastText classifier with OpenHermes-2.5 and ELI5 positives, keeping the top 10% of scores; the best of seven scorers at 1B (CORE 30.2 versus 29.0 for perplexity filtering), and its positives beat GPT-3-style ones at 7B (41.0 versus 37.5).

Risks: topic sculpting (FineWeb-Edu shifts toward education and history, with worse broad-web perplexity); benchmark alignment (instruction-style positives; Nemotron-CC sets tiers by annealing, not raw scores); and unique-token starvation (about 1T unique tokens in DCLM), which Nemotron-CC counters with a three-classifier ensemble, relaxed heuristics on trusted documents and rephrasing. Olmo 3 replaces the cutoff with quality-aware upsampling.

Core How do you detect and prevent benchmark contamination?

Detection is n-gram overlap between training data and every evaluation set: GPT-3 used 13-grams; Llama 3 flags an example when a set fraction of its tokens lie in 8-grams found in the corpus, choosing the fraction per benchmark to maximize the clean-versus-full score gap (HellaSwag 85% flagged, gain about 14 to 15 points; MMLU and HumanEval overlap too high to estimate). Olmo 3's decon samples n-grams at a stride and expands hits. phi-1's authors found a 13-gram search flagged only 4 false positives, yet embedding and AST similarity pruned over 40% of their exercises.

Prevention: decontaminate before training, checking mid-training and synthetic data first (memorization is strongest late); keep a private held-out set; report clean-subset comparisons; use dated or regenerated benchmarks. Limits: paraphrase and translation evade n-grams, short items give false positives, removal shifts the distribution, and labs disagree on its value (DCLM found removing MMLU overlaps did not lower its score, 51.8 to 52.7).

Deep-dive You have 2T unique high-quality tokens but budget for 8T. What do you do?

Quantify first. 8T is 4 epochs. With the Muennighoff et al. fit (\(R^*_D \approx 15\)), \(R_D = 3\) and \(D' = 2 + 2 \cdot 15 \cdot (1 - e^{-0.2}) = 7.44\)T, 93% of fresh data (measured gap at 8.7B: 0.5% loss). Plain repetition is a strong baseline; 8 epochs (82%) is where it starts to hurt.

Then improve it without more real data. (1) Repeat selectively: upsample the best fraction several times, the rest once (Olmo 3, capped at 7×). (2) Add unique tokens: relax filters on trusted documents, more medium-quality web, code (up to 50% of tokens without hurting text tasks), math, other languages. (3) Rephrase good data into two or three styles (K2: at most twice per corpus; Nemotron-CC's rephrasing cost at least about 6% of the run's FLOPs). (4) Put the best tokens late, in annealing. (5) Reconsider model size: data-constrained scaling favors fewer parameters and more epochs than Chinchilla allocation. Validate at equal unique-token supply with held-out real data. An illustrative plan (mine, not from a paper): 2T top-tier at 2 epochs, 2T rephrased at 1 epoch, 2T medium-quality web at 1 epoch.

Deep-dive FineWeb found global MinHash deduplication worse than per-snapshot deduplication. Why, and what would you do?

Facts: global MinHash over 96 snapshots left 4T tokens (up to 90% removed from the oldest) and barely beat no dedup, far below RefinedWeb. On 2013-48 the 31B tokens that survived were worse than the 460B removed (171B after per-snapshot dedup): more ads and keyword lists. Per-snapshot dedup left 20T and matched RefinedWeb.

FineWeb's hypothesis: the gain comes from removing enormous clusters, while collapsing clusters under about 100 copies removes popular content and leaves singletons that are unique because they are low-value. Counter-evidence: DCLM-Baseline would lose 85% of documents to global MinHash, and Nemotron-CC, Llama 3 and Olmo 3 deduplicate globally, so the result depends on downstream filters and horizon.

I would remove the giant clusters, keep cluster sizes as metadata, upsample by a quality proxy as FineWeb2's rehydration does (weights 1 to 10), and test at the target horizon, where unique-token supply may change the answer.

Deep-dive Design the experiment program for a data decision. What scale, which benchmarks, and why is perplexity not enough?

Scale: the smallest size at which your benchmarks clear noise. FineWeb used 1.71B models on 28B tokens for filters and 350B for dedup. DataDecide: ranking at 150M picks the 1B winner in about 80% of pairs, scaling-law extrapolation did no better, and seed standard deviation at 1B can reach 2 points, so use at least two seeds. DCLM's ranking correlation with 7B-1x was 0.84, 0.96 and 0.98 for 400M, 1B and 3B.

Benchmarks: low variance across data subsets, monotonic learning curves, above chance at your scale (FineWeb's criteria; DCLM's CORE); MMLU sits at 24 to 29 (chance 25) at 7B-1x. Continuous proxies (probability of the correct answer) made MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, MBPP and HumanEval over 80% predictable at 1B from 0.01% of target compute.

Perplexity fails because it depends on the reference distribution (RegMix: web loss tracked downstream accuracy, Wikipedia loss did not), is not comparable across training distributions, and misleads in both directions (FineWeb-Edu has worse broad-web perplexity yet better MMLU; low perplexity favors repetitive text). Use a panel: in-distribution loss, early-signal tasks, checks on domains the change may hurt; anneal to test late data; confirm finalists at a second scale.

Deep-dive A teammate proposes rephrasing the whole web with a 70B model to fix the token shortage. What do you check?

Cost: generation is about \(2N\) FLOPs per output token, so 1T tokens from a 70B model is \(2 \cdot 70 \times 10^9 \cdot 10^{12} = 1.4 \times 10^{23}\) FLOPs, 19% of the \(7.2 \times 10^{23}\) for an 8B model on 15T tokens, before input tokens. A big rephraser is not needed: WRAP found Qwen-1.8B and Mistral-7B sufficient (fine-tuned T5-base was clearly worse) and Nemotron-CC used a 12B model.

Benefit: rephrasing speeds up learning of what the source contains (WRAP about 3×; K2 SimpleQA 23.76 to 28.94) but adds no facts and can add errors, so add a fidelity check (K2). Keep real data in the mix (1:1 in WRAP) and accumulate rather than replace. Use several styles and cap rephrasings per source (K2: two). Decontaminate the synthetic data, since the teacher may have seen benchmarks (my inference). Test at equal unique real-token supply and confirm the gain survives annealing.

Cheat sheet

  • Pipeline order: URL filter, HTML extraction (not WET), language ID, heuristics, dedup, PII and safety, quality model, mixture. Cheap first, expensive last.
  • Gopher: 50 to 100k words; mean word length 3 to 10; # or ellipsis ratio at most 0.1; bullet lines at most 90%; ellipsis lines at most 30%; 80% of words with a letter; 2 of 8 stop words. C4: terminal punctuation (drops about 30% of tokens), at least 3 sentences, no "{" or "Lorem ipsum".
  • Token funnel: FineWeb 36T, then 20T (per-snapshot MinHash), then 15T, then 1.3T (Edu). Global MinHash: 4T and worse. DCLM: 240T to 3.8T. Counts depend on the tokenizer.
  • MinHash: \(\Pr[h(A)=h(B)] = J\); signature error \(\sqrt{J(1-J)/k}\). LSH: \(P = 1-(1-J^r)^b\), steepest at \((1/b)^{1/r}\). FineWeb 14 × 8 on 5-grams: 0.053, 0.565, 0.772, 0.924 at \(J\) = 0.5, 0.7, 0.75, 0.8. Lee et al.: 450 × 20.
  • Dedup evidence: memorization about 10× lower (1.9% to 0.19%); C4 has 3.04% near-duplicate documents and 7.18% of tokens in repeated 50-token substrings; suffix array costs 8 bytes per token.
  • Quality filters: GPT-3 keep probability \((2-s)^{-9}\); FineWeb-Edu score at least 3 (MMLU 33 to 37, ARC 46 to 57); DCLM fastText top 10% (7B on 2.6T tokens: 64% MMLU); Nemotron-CC 6.3T tokens.
  • Epochs seen = \(w_i D/n_i\). Llama 3 mix: 50% general, 25% math, 17% code, 8% multilingual. DoReMi: 280M proxy, +6.5 points, 2.6× faster, 8% compute. RegMix: 512 × 1M models, Spearman 97.1.
  • Annealing: LR to 0 on a higher-quality mix. OLMo 2 7B: MMLU 59.8 to 63.7, GSM8K 24.1 to 67.5; decay alone +4.4 OLMES. Llama 3 evaluates datasets by annealing on 30% candidate data.
  • Repetition: \(D' = U + U R^* (1 - e^{-R/R^*})\), \(R^* \approx 15\); 4 epochs about 93% of fresh (2T unique, 8T seen gives 7.44T); ceiling \(U(1+R^*)\). Code up to 50% is free for text tasks.
  • Synthetic: WRAP about 3× faster, no new knowledge; K2 SimpleQA 23.76 to 28.94; Nemotron-CC 1.9T synthetic. Accumulate real plus synthetic, never replace (error at most \(\sigma^2 d/(T-d-1)\,\pi^2/6\)).
  • Contamination: 13-gram (GPT-3), 8-gram (Llama 3: HellaSwag 85% flagged), question plus option (DCLM), embedding and AST (phi-1). Decontaminate mid-training first; keep a private held-out set.
  • Data decisions: proxies transfer (DCLM r = 0.84 to 0.98; DataDecide about 80% of pairs), use two seeds, benchmarks above noise (MMLU near chance at 7B-1x), perplexity alone misleads.
