Scaling laws

P0 ~60 min Prereqs: 01 Big picture, 04 Transformer block

Before a lab spends tens of millions of dollars of compute on one training run, it needs to know how large the model should be, how many tokens it should see, which batch size and learning rate to use, and what loss to expect. Scaling laws 缩放定律 answer these questions from small runs. This page teaches the power-law form and its irreducible floor, the exact compute-optimal allocation, why Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreed, why real models are deliberately over-trained, and how the laws are fitted, stress-tested and used to judge architecture changes.

What interviewers probe
  • The calculation on the spot: "You have \(10^{24}\) FLOPs: what model size and how many tokens?" Expect to use \(C \approx 6ND\) and about 20 tokens per parameter in under a minute, then say when and why you would deviate.
  • Kaplan versus Chinchilla: why the two studies recommended different allocations (\(N \propto C^{0.73}\) versus \(C^{0.5}\)), which confounds are documented, and what the episode says about trusting exponents.
  • The derivation: minimize \(L = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}\) subject to \(C = 6ND\), including the closed form, the optimal loss, and what changes when inference cost enters.
  • Over-training: why Llama 3 8B saw about 15T tokens (roughly 1,870 tokens per parameter), the break-even inference volume, and why tokens per parameter is a misleading number for mixture-of-experts models.
  • Critique and use: what range you can extrapolate over, where the uncertainty comes from, whether lower loss guarantees better benchmarks, and how you would use scaling curves to decide whether a new architecture is worth scaling.

After this page you can

  • Write \(L(N)\), \(L(D)\) and \(L(C)\) as power laws with an irreducible term, say what each holds fixed, and explain why a straight-line fit without the offset understates the exponent.
  • Derive \(N_{\text{opt}}\), \(D_{\text{opt}}\) and the optimal loss from \(L = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}\) with \(C = 6ND\), and compute the allocation for \(10^{22}\), \(10^{24}\) and \(3.8 \times 10^{25}\) FLOPs under three different fitted constants.
  • Explain the Kaplan versus Chinchilla discrepancy with the evidence from Hoffmann et al., Porian et al. and Pearce and Song, and name what a scaling-law study must control.
  • Derive the inference-aware optimality condition, compute a break-even serving volume for an over-trained model, and read tokens per parameter correctly for dense and MoE models.
  • Use the data-constrained formula for repeated tokens, and apply DeepSeek's batch-size and learning-rate laws; explain critical batch size.
  • Fit a law from small runs (IsoFLOP parabolas, log-log fits), state its uncertainty and extrapolation range, and describe the two-step method that predicts benchmark accuracy from compute.

Loss as a power law

Train a family of decoder-only transformers that differ only in size, and plot each model's final held-out loss against its parameter count on log-log axes. Over many orders of magnitude the points fall close to a straight line, a power law 幂律. The same holds against dataset size and against training compute. This regularity is what makes it rational to run a \(10^{24}\)-FLOP job on the strength of experiments a thousand times smaller. Kaplan et al. state the three basic laws, each valid when the other two resources are not the bottleneck [Kaplan et al. 2020, §1.2]:

LawFormExponentWhat is held fixed
\(L(N)\)\((N_c/N)^{\alpha_N}\), \(N_c \approx 8.8 \times 10^{13}\)\(\alpha_N \approx 0.076\)models trained to convergence on a large dataset
\(L(D)\)\((D_c/D)^{\alpha_D}\), \(D_c \approx 5.4 \times 10^{13}\) tokens\(\alpha_D \approx 0.095\)a model large enough not to be capacity-limited, early stopping
\(L(C_{\min})\)\((C_c^{\min}/C_{\min})^{\alpha_C^{\min}}\), \(C_c^{\min} \approx 3.1 \times 10^{8}\) PF-days\(\alpha_C^{\min} \approx 0.050\)optimally sized model, enough data, a batch size small enough to use compute efficiently

Here \(N\) counts non-embedding parameters, one PF-day is \(10^{15} \times 86{,}400 = 8.64 \times 10^{19}\) FLOPs, and the scales \(N_c, D_c, C_c\) depend on tokenizer and data, so they have no fundamental meaning. The exponents describe the shape: doubling \(N\) multiplies the loss by \(2^{-0.076} = 0.949\) (page 01).

The irreducible term, and why it matters for fitting

Kaplan's laws have no offset (an irreducible loss 不可约损失), so they must fail somewhere: natural text has non-zero entropy. Hoffmann et al. add the offset explicitly and give each term a meaning. The loss decomposes into the entropy of text under the data and tokenizer, the approximation error of an \(N\)-parameter model, and the optimization and finite-sample error of a single pass over \(D\) tokens [Hoffmann et al. 2022, §3.3 and App. D.2]:

\[ L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}} \]
E: irreducible loss. A/Nα: too few parameters. B/Dβ: too few tokens (and finite steps).

Hoffmann et al. report \(E = 1.69\), \(A = 406.4\), \(B = 410.7\), \(\alpha = 0.34\), \(\beta = 0.28\). A 2024 replication that reconstructed the data from the paper's figure refit the same form and found \(E = 1.8172\), \(A = 482.01\), \(B = 2085.43\), \(\alpha = 0.3478\), \(\beta = 0.3658\) [Besiroglu et al. 2024, Eq. 3-4]. Both are used below, because the choice changes the answer (next sections).

The offset explains a puzzle: Kaplan's \(\alpha_N = 0.076\) is about 4.5 times smaller than Chinchilla's \(\alpha = 0.34\). Much of that gap is form, not disagreement: one fits a straight line to \(L\) and the other to \(L - E\). Take the Epoch law with unlimited data, \(L(N) = 1.8172 + 482.01\,N^{-0.3478}\). Its local log-log slope is \(-\alpha\,(A N^{-\alpha})/L\), which is \(-0.17\) at \(N = 10^7\), \(-0.106\) at \(10^8\), \(-0.057\) at \(10^9\), \(-0.028\) at \(10^{10}\). The curve bends as the reducible part shrinks against \(E\). A straight-line fit of \(\ln L\) on \(\ln N\) over \(10^7\) to \(10^9\) returns an exponent of 0.108, and over \(10^9\) to \(10^{11}\) it returns 0.030 (computed from the law), while fitting \(L - E\) returns exactly 0.3478. Pearce and Song list the missing offset in Kaplan's compute-loss form as one reason the reported compute exponents differ (0.057 versus 0.155 to 0.178) [Pearce and Song 2024, §5]. The practical rule: fit an offset, or subtract an estimated \(E\), and reason about the reducible loss.

Definitions to fix before computing

  • \(N\): total or non-embedding? Kaplan excludes embeddings, Hoffmann counts everything; for Llama-3-8B that is 6.98B versus 8.03B (page 04), and the choice affects the fitted exponent (below).
  • \(C \approx 6ND\) (page 04): Hoffmann et al.'s full FLOP count is within 0.99 to 1.10 of it for 73M to 6.8B parameters [Hoffmann et al. 2022, App. F, Tab. A4].
  • \(L\): nats per token on a stated tokenizer (use bits per byte across tokenizers, page 01). In one-epoch training, loss on fresh batches is an unbiased estimate of held-out loss.
From your jet tagger

Your tagger's error against training-set size has the same shape, with a floor from class overlap (the Bayes error). The difference: you train many epochs on a fixed sample, so you live in the data-limited law \(L(D)\) with early stopping. Pretraining is close to one pass over fresh tokens, so data is a budget to spend, not a set to overfit (data-constrained scaling covers what happens when it runs out).

Key idea

A scaling law interpolates the loss surface of one data distribution, tokenizer, architecture family and training protocol. Exponents are measurements, not constants of nature, and much of this page is about what happens when the protocol changes.

Kaplan et al. 2020: grow the model, not the data

Kaplan et al. trained decoder-only transformers on WebText2 (about 22B tokens, GPT-2 vocabulary, context 1,024) and found that loss depends strongly on scale and weakly on shape (depth versus width, number of heads). Overfitting depends on one combination of size and data: to stay within 0.02 nats of the converged loss (their seed-to-seed variation), \(D \gtrsim 5 \times 10^{3}\,N^{0.74}\), so an 8-fold larger model needs only \(8^{0.74} = 4.7\) times more data [Kaplan et al. 2020, §4.2, Eq. 4.4]. And larger models are more sample-efficient: they reach a given loss in fewer steps and tokens.

The compute-optimal recommendation

Given a fit of loss against model size and (batch-adjusted) training steps, and the critical-batch-size law (below), the optimal allocation of a compute budget follows. The paper's consistency check is that the three exponents combine as \(\alpha_C^{\min} = 1/(1/\alpha_S + 1/\alpha_B + 1/\alpha_N)\); with \(\alpha_S = 0.76\), \(\alpha_B = 0.21\), \(\alpha_N = 0.076\) that is \(1/(1.316 + 4.762 + 13.158) = 0.052\), predicting \(N \propto C^{0.052/0.076} = C^{0.68}\) with these rounded inputs. The paper's own unrounded computation gives \(\alpha_C^{\min} \approx 0.054\) and \(N \propto C^{0.71}\), against the measured \(0.73\) [Kaplan et al. 2020, §6.2, Eq. 6.4-6.5]. The fitted compute-efficient frontier (Appendix A, Table 6) is:

\[ N_{\text{opt}} \approx 1.3 \times 10^{9}\, C_{\min}^{0.73},\qquad D_{\text{opt}} \approx 2 \times 10^{10}\, C_{\min}^{0.27},\qquad B \propto C^{0.24},\qquad S_{\min} \propto C^{0.03} \]
C in PF-days (8.64e19 FLOPs); N in non-embedding parameters; D in tokens for one epoch

Per tenfold increase in compute, that is \(10^{0.73} = 5.4\) times more parameters but only \(10^{0.27} = 1.9\) times more tokens. Most of the extra compute goes into model size, a little into batch size, almost none into more optimizer steps (\(C^{0.03}\)). Compute-efficient training stops "significantly before convergence". Following this recipe, GPT-3 (175B parameters) and its successors trained on about 300B tokens (Gopher 280B on 300B, Megatron-Turing NLG 530B on 270B) [Hoffmann et al. 2022, Tab. 1], and Porian et al. note the law was influential in sizing GPT-3 and several later models [Porian et al. 2024, §1].

Two details. \(C_{\min}\) is the compute at a batch size far below the critical batch size; training at the critical batch size costs \(C = 2 C_{\min}\) [Kaplan et al. 2020, §5.1]. With that convention the formula gives \(1.3 \times 10^{9}\,(10^{21}/2 / 8.64 \times 10^{19})^{0.73} = 4.68\)B parameters at \(10^{21}\) FLOPs, the number Hoffmann et al. quote for Kaplan's prescription. And Kaplan et al. warned that their \(L(C_{\min})\) law falls below the \(L(D)\) bound near \(10^{4}\) PF-days (\(8.6 \times 10^{23}\) FLOPs, about Chinchilla's compute), so "our scaling laws must break down before this point" [Kaplan et al. 2020, §6.3].

Hoffmann et al. trained more than 400 models from 70M to over 16B parameters on 5B to 500B tokens and asked the question behind compute-optimal 计算最优 training three ways: given a FLOP budget, which model size and token count minimize loss? Two details carry the result. Each run's cosine schedule decays 10× over exactly that run's token budget (overshooting by more than 25% visibly hurts), so the points Approach 1 selects all lie in the last 15% of training and Approach 2 needs one run per (size, budget) pair [Hoffmann et al. 2022, §3.1, App. D.1]. And all three approaches agree that the compute-optimal model grows in step with the data. The paper tested this: Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) uses Gopher's compute (280B, 300B tokens) with a 4× smaller model and 4.7× more data, and reaches 67.5% average MMLU accuracy, a more than 7% improvement over Gopher [Hoffmann et al. 2022, Abstract].

Derivation from the parametric form

Approach 3 gives a closed form, and interviewers ask for it. We minimize \(L(N,D) = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B D^{-\beta}\) subject to \(6ND = C\). Substitute \(D = C/(6N)\):

\[ L(N) = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B \left(\frac{6N}{C}\right)^{\beta}, \qquad \frac{dL}{dN} = -\alpha A N^{-\alpha-1} + \beta B \left(\frac{6}{C}\right)^{\beta} N^{\beta-1} = 0 . \]

Multiplying by \(N\) and using \(D = C/(6N)\) turns the first-order condition into a balance of marginal returns:

\[ \frac{\alpha A}{N^{\alpha}} = \frac{\beta B}{D^{\beta}} . \]

Solving \(N^{\alpha+\beta} = (\alpha A / \beta B)\,(C/6)^{\beta}\) gives the allocation (Hoffmann et al. Eq. 4):

\[ N_{\text{opt}} = G \left(\frac{C}{6}\right)^{a},\quad D_{\text{opt}} = \frac{C}{6 N_{\text{opt}}} = G^{-1} \left(\frac{C}{6}\right)^{b},\quad G = \left(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}},\quad a = \frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta},\ b = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta} \]
a + b = 1. E does not enter. \(L(N,D)\) is a sum of exponentials in \(\ln N\) and \(\ln D\), hence convex, so this stationary point is the minimum.

Three consequences. Optimal loss: the first-order condition says \(B D^{-\beta} = (\alpha/\beta)\,A N^{-\alpha}\), so \(L^* = E + (1 + \alpha/\beta)\,A N_{\text{opt}}^{-\alpha} = E + K\,C^{-\gamma}\) with \(\gamma = \alpha\beta/(\alpha+\beta)\). For the Epoch constants \(\gamma = 0.178\), so each doubling of compute multiplies the reducible loss by \(2^{-0.178} = 0.884\). Tokens per parameter: \(D/N = G^{-2}\,(C/6)^{b-a}\) is constant in \(C\) only if \(\alpha = \beta\). "20 tokens per parameter" is where the fitted curves happen to cross, not a law: with the printed Hoffmann constants \(b - a = 0.097\) and the ratio drifts upward with compute (next table). Sanity check: the drill in exercises/drills/scaling.py tests this closed form against brute-force minimization of \(L\) along the IsoFLOP curve, and the two agree to \(2 \times 10^{-4}\).

def compute_optimal(C, A, B, alpha, beta):
    G = (alpha * A / (beta * B)) ** (1 / (alpha + beta))
    a = beta / (alpha + beta)
    N = G * (C / 6) ** a              # parameters
    return N, C / (6 * N)             # tokens; enforces 6 N D = C

The 2024 replication and what it found

Besiroglu et al. (Epoch AI) reconstructed 240 runs from the paper's Figure 4 (loss read from the colour scale to about 0.01; five outliers with very few tokens per parameter excluded) and refit Approach 3. The printed constants fit the reconstructed data poorly, the reported interval on \(a\) (0.454 to 0.455) would need about 600,000 experiments, and their fit (\(a = 0.5126\), standard error 0.018) agrees with Approaches 1 and 2 [Besiroglu et al. 2024, §3, Tab. 1]. Two causes: the published values are rounded (rounding \(\beta\) from 0.2849 to 0.28 biases the data term by about 13% at \(10^{11}\) tokens), and the Hoffmann et al. authors confirmed that their L-BFGS optimizer stopped early because the Huber loss was averaged over points instead of summed, which also made the bootstrap intervals narrow. The refit is "consistent with a ratio of tokens to parameters between 4 and 40" at \(10^{26}\) FLOPs and beyond, so honest error bars on the allocation are wide.

Problem
Kaplan's protocol produced oversized, under-trained models. Which \((N, D)\) minimizes loss for a fixed \(C\)?
Idea
Match each run's schedule to its token budget, then find the optimum three ways (envelope, IsoFLOP parabolas, parametric surface).
Why it works
At the optimum, the marginal loss reduction per e-fold of parameters equals that per e-fold of tokens: \(\alpha A N^{-\alpha} = \beta B D^{-\beta}\). Since \(\alpha \approx \beta\), both grow at nearly the same rate.
Evidence
Chinchilla 70B beats Gopher 280B at equal compute [Hoffmann et al. 2022, Abstract]; Porian et al. reproduce exponent 0.5 with tuned hyperparameters [Porian et al. 2024, Fig. 1].
Cost
Optimizes training loss for training FLOPs only. Ignores inference, data limits, and the fact that the frontier is curved (Hoffmann App. E notes even smaller models may be optimal at large budgets).
Used by
Chinchilla itself; the 70B-class LLaMA models sit at 20 and 30 tokens per parameter [Gadre et al. 2024, Tab. 8]; and it is the baseline that deployed models deliberately deviate from.

Why Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreed

The exponents 0.73 and 0.5 differ enormously once extrapolated (Figure 2). Hoffmann et al. offered one explanation, and two 2024 studies tested it and found more. They do not fully agree with each other, and the honest summary is a list of protocol confounds rather than a single cause.

  • Hoffmann et al. (2022): schedules and scale. Kaplan used a fixed number of training tokens and one cosine schedule (about 130B tokens) for every run, so the intermediate losses used for shorter budgets are overestimates of what a schedule matched to that budget reaches. That underestimates the value of training on less data and pushes toward larger models. Kaplan's runs were also smaller, many of them under 100M parameters, and the FLOP-loss frontier is curved, so fits from small models do not extrapolate (Hoffmann et al. train up to 16B) [Hoffmann et al. 2022, §2, App. E].
  • Porian et al. (2024): reproduce, then remove confounds one at a time. They reproduced Kaplan's exponent on two datasets and changed one factor per step (table below). Counting the FLOPs of the output layer, which Kaplan's non-embedding count omitted, moves \(a\) by more than 0.1; a warmup that scales with model size instead of a fixed long warmup (which forced small models to train longer than optimal just to escape it) moves it another 0.1; tuning learning rate, batch size and AdamW \(\beta_2\) per model size finishes the job. Matching the schedule to the token budget, Hoffmann's hypothesis, has "little effect" [Porian et al. 2024, §3, Tab. 1].
  • Pearce and Song (2024): parameter counting at small scale. Kaplan's \(N\) and \(C\) exclude embeddings, and their models had 768 to 1.5B non-embedding parameters, where the embedding share is large. Total and non-embedding parameters are related by \(N_T = N_{\setminus E} + \omega N_{\setminus E}^{1/3}\), which makes the optimal non-embedding size only locally a power law. Simulating Chinchilla's fitted loss surface under Kaplan's conditions gives local exponents of 0.78 (Epoch constants) and 0.74 (printed constants) against Kaplan's 0.73, while the same surface in total parameters gives 0.51 [Pearce and Song 2024, §3, Eq. 21-22]. Their small experiments (0.8M to 4.6M parameters) show exponent 0.49 in total parameters and 0.74 in non-embedding parameters.
  • DeepSeek LLM (2024): data quality moves the exponent. On three datasets, \(a\) was 0.450 (early in-house data), 0.524 (current, cleaner in-house data) and 0.578 (OpenWebText2, the cleanest); higher-quality data favors more parameters [DeepSeek LLM, Tab. 4].

Beyond compute-optimal: training for inference

Chinchilla minimizes loss for a training budget. A deployed model is then run on far more tokens than it was trained on, and that cost is not in the objective. A forward pass costs about \(2N\) FLOPs per token against \(6N\) for training, so a model with \(N\) parameters trained on \(D_{\text{tr}}\) tokens and later serving \(D_{\text{inf}}\) tokens costs \(6 N D_{\text{tr}} + 2 N D_{\text{inf}}\) in total. Sardana and Frankle fix the quality (a target loss \(\ell\)) and minimize this total [Sardana and Frankle 2023, §2, Eq. 3].

The optimality condition

Write the Lagrangian for minimizing \(6 N D + 2 N D_{\text{inf}}\) subject to \(E + A N^{-\alpha} + B D^{-\beta} = \ell\). The two stationarity conditions are \(6D + 2D_{\text{inf}} = -\lambda\,\alpha A N^{-\alpha-1}\) and \(6N = -\lambda\,\beta B D^{-\beta-1}\) (their App. A). Dividing one by the other and multiplying by \(N/D\), with \(D = D_{\text{tr}}\) (we do this step, not the paper):

\[ \Big(1 + \frac{D_{\text{inf}}}{3 D_{\text{tr}}}\Big)\,\frac{\beta B}{D_{\text{tr}}^{\beta}} = \frac{\alpha A}{N^{\alpha}} \]
With Dinf = 0 this is Chinchilla's balance of marginal returns. Serving pushes weight onto the parameter term, so N falls and Dtr rises.

Check with the Epoch constants and a target loss of 1.960 (the optimum at \(10^{24}\) FLOPs), \(D_{\text{inf}} = 10^{13}\): the cheapest model has \(N = 48.3\)B and \(D_{\text{tr}} = 4.31\)T. The left side is \(1 + 10^{13}/(3 \cdot 4.31 \times 10^{12}) = 1.77\) times \(\beta B D^{-\beta} = 0.0182\); the right side is \(\alpha A N^{-\alpha} = 0.0323\). They agree. There is no closed form for \(N\) as a function of \(\ell\) and \(D_{\text{inf}}\): Sardana and Frankle prove there is no general analytic solution and use Newton root-finding (App. A, Theorem A.1); the drill implements a one-dimensional search.

Reported cases: a 70B-Chinchilla-quality model serving \(10^{13}\) tokens is cheaper as a 41.6B model trained on 7.92T tokens (12% fewer total FLOPs), and a 30B-quality model serving \(10^{13}\) tokens as a 13.6B model on 2.84 times the data (28% fewer) [Sardana and Frankle 2023, §2, App. B Tab. 2]. Our solver (the drill's inference_aware_optimum), run with their constants, returns 41.6B parameters and 7.92T tokens for the first. FLOPs also understate the case for small models: training runs at 40 to 60% of peak FLOP/s while token-by-token decoding can run near 1% (page 17). In their dollar-based version, for 2T served tokens a Chinchilla-70B needs only 1.3% more FLOPs than the equal-quality compute-optimal model but costs 36% more than the equal-quality cost-optimal one [Sardana and Frankle 2023, §6].

Problem
The compute-optimal model is too expensive to serve at the volumes a successful product sees, and training-optimal is not cost-optimal.
Idea
Fix the target loss and minimize training plus serving FLOPs (or dollars). Train a smaller model on many more tokens.
Why it works
Loss is nearly flat in the size-versus-tokens trade near the optimum (Figure 1), while serving cost is linear in \(N\). Extra tokens buy back the loss lost by shrinking, at a small training premium.
Evidence
12% to 28% total FLOP savings in the model of [Sardana and Frankle 2023, Tab. 2]; no saturation up to 10,000 tokens per parameter in their 47 runs; predictable scaling to \(M = 640\) [Gadre et al. 2024].

Used by
The Llama 3 family, and the MoE models in the table above, measured against activated parameters.

Data limits and hyperparameter laws

Data-constrained scaling 数据受限缩放: how much is a repeated token worth?

Chinchilla assumes every token is fresh. High-quality text is finite (page 12): extrapolating Chinchilla to a 530B-parameter model needs 11T tokens, over 30 TB of text and far more than exists in most languages. Muennighoff et al. trained more than 400 models (10M to 9B parameters, up to 900B tokens, up to 1,500 epochs) and generalized the parametric law to repeated data [Muennighoff et al. 2023]. With \(U\) unique tokens, \(D\) total tokens processed and \(R = D/U - 1\) repetitions, repeated tokens decay in value exponentially:

\[ D' = U + U R^{*}_{D}\left(1 - e^{-R/R^{*}_{D}}\right), \qquad L = E + \frac{A}{N'^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D'^{\beta}} \]
Fitted R*D ≈ 15.4 (about 16 epochs). N′ has the same form with R*N ≈ 5.3, so excess parameters decay faster than repeated data.

For \(R \ll R^*\) the bracket is \(\approx R\) and \(D' \approx D\): early repeats are almost as good as fresh data. For \(R \gg R^*\) it saturates and \(D' \to U(1 + R^*_D) = 16.4\,U\), so no number of epochs is worth more than about 16 times the unique corpus. Limits. The law assumes extra epochs can plateau but never hurt, yet their 44-epoch runs saw loss rise mid-training. That extrapolation is theirs, from small models: a hypothesis as of September 2026.

Repeating data (data-constrained scaling)
Problem
The compute-optimal token count exceeds the unique high-quality text available.
Idea
Repeat the corpus for several epochs and scale epochs faster than parameters, valuing repeated tokens with an exponentially decaying effective-data term.
Why it works
Each pass over a token extracts part of its information, so each repeat is worth a constant fraction less than the previous one (their exponential-decay model); excess parameters likewise re-learn the same features and add little.
Evidence
Four epochs cost 0.5% in validation loss at 8.7B parameters; the recommended smaller model beat the Chinchilla-sized one at \(9.3 \times 10^{21}\) FLOPs [Muennighoff et al. 2023].
Cost
Fitted on at most 9B parameters and one corpus; assumes extra epochs never hurt (44-epoch runs did); newer evidence says the useful epoch count shrinks with scale.
Used by
Multi-epoch pretraining in data-scarce settings, for example the 4.25-epoch Galactica models cited in the paper.

Hyperparameter scaling laws and critical batch size

A scaling study is only as good as the tuning at each size (Porian et al. list untuned optimizers among three confounds). At frontier scale you cannot sweep, so labs fit laws for the batch size and learning rate themselves. DeepSeek LLM ran grids of batch size and learning rate at compute budgets from \(10^{17}\) to \(2 \times 10^{19}\), took the settings within 0.25% of the best generalization error as near-optimal, and fitted power laws in compute \(C = M D\), where \(M\) is non-embedding FLOPs per token including attention, \(M = 72\,L\,d^{2} + 12\,L\,d\,l_{\text{seq}}\) [DeepSeek LLM, §3.1, Eq. 1-2]:

\[ \eta_{\text{opt}} = 0.3118\,C^{-0.1250}, \qquad B_{\text{opt}} = 0.2920\,C^{0.3271} \]
Learning rate falls and batch size (in tokens) grows with compute. Their allocation fit in the same units: Mopt = 0.1715 C0.5243, Dopt = 5.8316 C0.4757.

Check it against the models it produced (our arithmetic; \(C = M \times 2\text{T}\) tokens). DeepSeek LLM 7B (\(L = 30\), \(d = 4096\), \(l_{\text{seq}} = 4096\)): \(M = 4.23 \times 10^{10}\), \(C = 8.5 \times 10^{22}\), giving \(B_{\text{opt}} = 9.2\)M tokens and \(\eta_{\text{opt}} = 4.2 \times 10^{-4}\), against Table 2's 2,304 sequences of 4,096 tokens (9.4M) and \(4.2 \times 10^{-4}\). The 67B model (\(L = 95\), \(d = 8192\)): \(C = 9.9 \times 10^{23}\), \(B_{\text{opt}} = 20.7\)M and \(\eta_{\text{opt}} = 3.1 \times 10^{-4}\), against 4,608 sequences (18.9M) and \(3.2 \times 10^{-4}\) [DeepSeek LLM, Tab. 2]: within 10% on batch size and 3% on learning rate. The paper's caveats: the near-optimal region is a broad band, so the order of magnitude matters more than the digits, and the optimum shifts slightly with the model-versus-data split at equal compute, which \(C\) alone does not capture. Porian et al. fit similar laws in model size and got a batch-size exponent within 0.05 of DeepSeek's and a learning-rate exponent 0.11 different (a factor 2 to 3, possibly from different weight decay) [Porian et al. 2024, §3.5].

Critical batch size 临界批量大小 is the batch size beyond which extra parallelism gives diminishing returns: below it, doubling the batch halves the steps needed to reach a target loss. Kaplan et al. found it depends on the loss reached, not directly on model size: \(B_{\text{crit}}(L) = B_{*}/L^{1/\alpha_B}\) with \(B_{*} \approx 2 \times 10^{8}\) tokens and \(\alpha_B \approx 0.21\), so it doubles for each 13% decrease in loss and is 1 to 2 million tokens for their largest models [Kaplan et al. 2020, §1.2, §5.1, Fig. 10]. Zhang et al. (85M to 1.2B parameters on C4) separated model and data: with data fixed, the critical batch size barely changes with model size (fitted \(N^{0.087}\)), while it grows with the number of tokens (fitted \(D^{0.47}\), for a definition with 20% overhead) [Zhang et al. 2024, §3.3]. Compute-optimal scaling grows both together, which hid the distinction. The reason it tracks loss: as the loss approaches its minimum, the gradient signal shrinks against per-example noise, so the gradient noise scale, and with it the useful batch size, grows [McCandlish et al. 2018]. Practical consequence: longer, over-trained runs can use larger batches without losing efficiency. Real recipes follow this: Llama 3 405B stepped its batch from 4M to 8M to 16M tokens during training [Llama 3, §3.4.1], and DeepSeek-V4 ramps to 75.5M (Flash) and 94.4M (Pro) tokens [DeepSeek-V4, §4.2.2].

Two related tools (page 14). Under the standard parameterization the best learning rate drifts down as models widen; μP 最大更新参数化 reparameterizes the network so that it is approximately width-independent, so you tune a small proxy and transfer [Yang et al. 2022]. And the schedule affects scaling-law cost: Chinchilla needs one cosine run per token budget, DeepSeek LLM's multi-step schedule (peak, 31.6% after 80% of tokens, 10% after 90%) lets later runs reuse the first stage, and Hägele et al. show a constant rate with a short cooldown matches cosine and, by their estimate, would have cut a Chinchilla-style suite from \(5.59 \times 10^{23}\) to \(2.36 \times 10^{23}\) FLOPs [Hägele et al. 2024, §5].

MoE scaling and predicting downstream performance

Mixture-of-experts: two parameter counts

The dense laws use one \(N\). A mixture-of-experts model has an activated count \(N_{\text{act}}\) that sets FLOPs per token (so \(C \approx 6 N_{\text{act}} D\)) and a total count that sets memory and capacity (page 08). Krajewski et al. fit scaling laws for fine-grained MoE with a granularity hyperparameter and find that MoE consistently beats dense models at every compute budget they study, with the gap widening with scale, and that sizing each expert like the dense FFN is not optimal [Krajewski et al. 2024, Abstract]. Kimi K2 gives a concrete sparsity law: with sparsity defined as total experts over activated experts and activated parameters (hence FLOPs) held fixed, more sparsity lowers loss. At a validation loss of 1.5, sparsity 48 needs 1.69, 1.39 and 1.15 times fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8, 16 and 32. K2 adopted sparsity 48 (8 of 384 experts, 1.04T total, 32.6B active, 15.5T tokens) and the report stresses that the gain "comes with increased infrastructure complexity" [Kimi K2, §2.3]. So a dense-model tokens-per-parameter figure does not transfer: for MoE you fit the surface in both counts, at a fixed data distribution and optimizer.

Does lower loss mean better benchmarks?

Within one model family and data recipe, benchmark scores rise as loss falls, and this is what makes loss a usable planning metric. But loss is smooth and benchmarks are not. Schaeffer et al. argue that sharp "emergent" jumps (涌现) come mostly from the metric: if per-token accuracy improves smoothly, a metric requiring \(L\) tokens all correct behaves like \(p^{L}\) (page 01 works an example), and more than 92% of the BIG-Bench abilities annotated as emergent appear under just two metrics, multiple-choice grade (discontinuous) and exact string match (nonlinear). Rescoring the same outputs with token edit distance or Brier score removes the jump, and small test sets add a second artifact by making low accuracies look like zero. The authors add the caveat that this does not show LLMs cannot have emergent abilities, only that the claimed ones may be metric-induced [Schaeffer et al. 2023, §1, §4, §7]. The original claim they test defines emergence as abilities "that are not present in smaller-scale models but are present in large-scale models; thus they cannot be predicted by simply extrapolating the performance improvements on smaller-scale models" [Wei et al. 2022].

The two-step method. Llama 3 turned this into a forecasting recipe [Llama 3, §3.2.1]. Step 1: on scaling-law models up to \(10^{22}\) FLOPs, linearly correlate the normalized negative log-likelihood of the correct answer on a benchmark with training FLOPs (plotted on a log axis). Step 2: fit a sigmoid from that log-likelihood to accuracy, using both the scaling-law models and older, more heavily trained Llama 2 models, which extend the range of accuracies covered. On ARC Challenge the forecast extrapolates over four orders of magnitude and "only slightly underestimates" the final 405B model. Gadre et al. chain a loss law with \(\text{Err}(L) = \epsilon - k\,e^{-\gamma L}\) and predicted the average error over 17 tasks for a 6.9B model on 138B tokens to 0.05% relative error from experiments with 20 times less compute; single tasks were far noisier (1.9% to 82% relative error in their Table 2), and dropping one mid-size model from the downstream fit raised the average miss from 0.05% to 10.6%, so downstream laws need larger fitting runs than loss laws [Gadre et al. 2024, §2.3, §4, Tab. 2]. GPT-4's report shows the same in a lab: final loss predicted from runs up to 10,000 times cheaper and a HumanEval subset from runs up to 1,000 times cheaper (\(-\mathbb{E}[\log \text{pass rate}] = \alpha C^{-k}\)), while inverse-scaling tasks stayed hard to predict [OpenAI 2023, §3].

Pitfall: loss is a sufficient statistic only inside a recipe

Qin et al. find that runs with repetition or paraphrasing fall on one curve of downstream score against validation loss, so loss looks sufficient across those training settings at small scale [Qin et al. 2026, §4.5]. That does not extend across data mixtures (a code-heavy mix can lower code loss and leave other benchmarks flat), across tokenizers (compare bits per byte), or through post-training, where RL and test-time compute add axes the pretraining law does not describe (page 24).

How labs use scaling laws in practice

The recipe

  1. Freeze what can be frozen. Tokenizer, data mix, architecture family (width and depth scaled together at a fixed aspect ratio) and schedule family. Scaling-law experiments are also how data mixes are chosen: Llama 3 trained small models on candidate mixes and used them to predict large-model performance on each [Llama 3, §3.1.2].
  2. Tune per size. Set batch size, learning rate and warmup for each model size from small sweeps and fitted laws (previous section) or μP. Porian et al. measured about 0.8 with a Kaplan-style protocol (fixed warmup, fixed batch, untuned optimizer, head FLOPs ignored) and about 0.5 once tuned.
  3. Sweep IsoFLOP curves. Choose several budgets spanning at least two orders of magnitude and, at each, several sizes that bracket the minimum, each trained on \(D = C/6N\) tokens with a schedule matched to that budget. Llama 3 used budgets from \(6 \times 10^{18}\) to \(10^{22}\) FLOPs with models from 40M to 16B parameters, 2,000 warmup steps, cosine decay to 0.1 of peak, and a fixed batch size per budget between 250K and 4M tokens [Llama 3, §3.2.1].
  4. Fit. A parabola in \(\ln N\) per budget gives \((N^*, L^*)\); then fit \(N^*(C)\), \(D^*(C)\) and \(L^*(C)\) as power laws (Approach 2, what Llama 3 did), and/or fit the whole parametric surface (Approach 3).
  5. Pick on the flat part, extrapolate cautiously, and validate. Llama 3 observed that IsoFLOP curves flatten near the minimum as budgets grow, so the flagship could sit at 405B while the fit said 402B. The table below lists published extrapolation track records.
import numpy as np

def isoflop_optimum(sizes, losses):
    """Vertex of the parabola fitted to (ln N, loss) on one IsoFLOP curve."""
    x = np.log(sizes); x0 = x.mean()
    c2, c1, c0 = np.polyfit(x - x0, losses, 2)     # centered: better conditioned
    assert c2 > 0, "no interior minimum: widen the size sweep"
    t = -c1 / (2 * c2)
    return np.exp(x0 + t), c0 - c1**2 / (4 * c2)   # N*, L*

def fit_power_law(x, y):
    p, c = np.polyfit(np.log(x), np.log(y), 1)     # ln y = p ln x + c
    return p, np.exp(c)

Where the uncertainty comes from

  • Statistics. Few points, noisy measurements. Epoch's bootstrap gives a standard error of 0.018 on the exponent \(a\), an 80% interval of about 0.05, roughly 50 times wider than the interval Hoffmann et al. reported. Report bootstrap intervals, not just point fits.
  • Rounding. A 0.01 error in an exponent multiplies the extrapolation to \(3.8 \times 10^{25}\) FLOPs by 1.8. Keep full precision in the fitted constants.
  • Optimizer and protocol. Sum (not average) the Huber loss over points, use many initializations, and check the optimizer converged: Hoffmann et al.'s early-stopped L-BFGS made an Approach 3 fit inconsistent with their own Approaches 1 and 2.
  • Misspecification. The frontier is slightly curved (Hoffmann et al. App. E), the parametric form flattens at extreme tokens per parameter (Sardana and Frankle), and outlier runs at very few tokens per parameter break the fit (Epoch dropped five).
  • Data dependence. Exponents move with data quality (DeepSeek: 0.45 to 0.58) and \(N_c\), \(D_c\), \(E\) depend on tokenizer and data. Refit for every new data recipe.

Scaling curves as evidence for an architecture change

Scaling laws are also used to compare architectures and data mixes cheaply [Hägele et al. 2024, §5]. A single-size ablation cannot tell whether a change shifts the curve by a constant or changes its slope, and a gain that grows with size is worth much more than one that shrinks. A workable protocol (our recommendation, not a published standard):

  1. Train the baseline and the variant at four or more sizes spanning one to two orders of magnitude, with identical data, tuned hyperparameters for each, and enough seeds to estimate noise. Kaplan et al. estimated about 0.02 nats of seed-to-seed variation in their setting; measure yours.
  2. Fit \(L(C) = E + K C^{-\gamma}\) to each (fix \(E\) to a shared value if the data are the same) and compare the compute multiplier, the factor by which the baseline needs more compute to reach the variant's loss. With equal slopes it is \((K_{\text{base}}/K_{\text{var}})^{1/\gamma}\), a constant; if the multiplier shrinks with size, the gain is disappearing.
  3. Read the noise in compute terms. Since \(dL/d\ln C = -\gamma\,(L - E)\), at \(10^{24}\) FLOPs under the Epoch law a 12% compute multiplier is only a 0.003-nat loss difference. Small multipliers at large scale need paired comparisons on the same data order and many seeds.
  4. Add what the loss does not show: FLOPs and memory per token, kernel maturity, long-context and downstream behavior, and the extrapolation distance to your target scale. A 1.2× multiplier does not survive a 1.3× slower kernel.

Extrapolation is evidence, not proof: a method that wins at \(10^{19}\) to \(10^{21}\) FLOPs can lose at \(10^{24}\), which is why labs register predictions and check them on a larger run before committing to the flagship [OpenAI 2023, §3]. For arithmetic on parameters, FLOPs and GPU-hours around all of this, see page 34; for the distributed-training side of the budget (MFU, parallelism), page 15.

Common misconceptions

  • "Chinchilla says 20 tokens per parameter." That is where its Approach 1 and 2 curves cross for its data and tokenizer. Approach 3 as printed implies 60 to 140 (a fitting artifact), the Epoch refit gives 16 to 20 over \(10^{22}\) to \(4 \times 10^{25}\) FLOPs, the ratio drifts with compute unless \(\alpha = \beta\), it moves with data quality, and it defines the training-compute optimum only, not the deployment optimum.
  • "Kaplan was wrong, Chinchilla is right." Kaplan's \(L(N)\) and \(L(D)\) fits still describe the data. The allocation built on them inherited protocol confounds (fixed warmup and schedule, head FLOPs and embeddings excluded, small scale), and better-controlled re-measurement gives about 0.5. Both are fits.
  • "Over-training wastes compute." It wastes compute only if the model is barely served. Trading 2.4 times the training compute for a 3.2 times cheaper model breaks even near \(10^{13}\) served tokens in our worked example, and dollar costs favor it earlier.
  • "Exponents are constants of nature." They depend on protocol and data: 0.73 versus 0.5 for the same question, 0.45 to 0.58 across DeepSeek's data. \(N_c\), \(D_c\) and \(E\) also depend on the tokenizer.
  • "6ND works with any \(N\)." Say which \(N\). Non-embedding versus total parameters changed the fitted exponent (0.73 versus 0.5), and \(6ND\) omits attention. For MoE, \(N\) is the activated count, and tokens per activated parameter is not comparable to a dense ratio.
  • "Scaling laws predict benchmark scores." They predict loss. Benchmarks need a second fitted map (Llama 3's sigmoid, Gadre's exponential), single tasks stay noisy, and "emergence" is largely a property of discontinuous metrics.
  • "Never repeat data, or repeat freely." Up to about four epochs is close to free (93% effective at four, 66% at sixteen, 38% at forty), the value is exhausted near \(16\,U\), and newer evidence suggests fewer epochs at larger scale.
  • "Optimal batch size is a property of model size." Critical batch size tracks the loss reached and the data seen (Kaplan; Zhang et al.), not parameters directly. DeepSeek's fit indexes it by compute.

Interview questions

Warm-up What is a scaling law, what does each one hold fixed, and why is there an irreducible term?

A scaling law is an empirical power law relating held-out loss to a scale variable when the others are not the bottleneck. Kaplan et al. fit \(L(N) = (N_c/N)^{0.076}\) (converged models, large data), \(L(D) = (D_c/D)^{0.095}\) (large model, early stopping) and \(L(C_{\min}) \propto C^{-0.050}\) (optimal allocation); doubling \(N\) multiplies loss by \(2^{-0.076} = 0.949\). Chinchilla adds an offset, \(L = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}\): \(E\) is the entropy of text under that tokenizer and data (loss no model can beat), \(A/N^{\alpha}\) the approximation error of \(N\) parameters, \(B/D^{\beta}\) the error of a finite single pass over \(D\) tokens.

The offset matters when fitting: with the Epoch constants, a straight-line log-log fit of \(L\) over \(10^7\) to \(10^9\) parameters returns an exponent of 0.108, while the true \(\alpha\) is 0.348, because the curve bends toward \(E\). Fit \(L - E\), or fit the offset.

Warm-up Compute the training FLOPs of Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) and Llama 3 405B (15.6T tokens) with \(6ND\), and say where the 6 comes from.

Forward is 2 FLOPs per weight per token; backward is twice that (one matmul for the input gradient, one for the weight gradient), so 6 per weight per token: \(C = 6ND\). Chinchilla: \(6 \times 70 \times 10^{9} \times 1.4 \times 10^{12} = 5.88 \times 10^{23}\). Llama 3 405B: \(6 \times 405 \times 10^{9} \times 15.6 \times 10^{12} = 3.79 \times 10^{25}\), against the reported \(3.8 \times 10^{25}\) (0.24% low). GPT-3: \(6 \times 175\text{B} \times 300\text{B} = 3.15 \times 10^{23}\).

Caveats to state: \(N\) must count the matmul weights (embedding lookups cost nothing, the output head does), and \(6ND\) omits the attention-score matmuls, which are minor at short context and dominant at 128K (page 04). Hoffmann et al. found their full count within 0.99 to 1.10 of \(6ND\) for 73M to 6.8B parameters.

Warm-up What does the Chinchilla result say happens to model size and tokens when you double the compute?

With \(N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{a}\), \(D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{b}\) and \(a \approx b \approx 0.5\), doubling compute multiplies both \(N\) and \(D\) by \(\sqrt 2 = 1.41\). (Doubling the model and doubling the tokens together is 4 times the compute, a common slip.) A tenfold budget gives 3.2 times the parameters and 3.1 times the tokens under the Epoch fit, against Kaplan's 5.4 and 1.9. The rule of thumb of about 20 tokens per parameter makes \(C = 6N \cdot 20N = 120N^{2}\), so \(N = \sqrt{C/120}\). The reducible loss falls as \(C^{-\gamma}\) with \(\gamma = \alpha\beta/(\alpha+\beta) = 0.178\) (Epoch), a factor 0.884 per doubling.

Core You have \(10^{24}\) FLOPs. What model size and token count would you choose, and why might you deviate?

Baseline. \(C = 6ND\) with 20 tokens per parameter gives \(N = \sqrt{10^{24}/120} = 91\)B and \(D = 1.8\)T. The closed form with the Epoch refit gives 96B parameters and 1.74T tokens (18 tokens per parameter), predicted loss 1.96. I would mention that the Approach 3 constants as printed give 41B and 4.0T (98 tokens per parameter), which is the fitting artifact Epoch diagnosed, and that at this budget the answer is only good to roughly a factor of 2.

Why the answer is forgiving. The IsoFLOP valley is flat: a model twice too large or too small costs 0.0044 nats, four times off about 0.018.

Deviations. (1) Inference: if the model will serve a lot of traffic, go smaller and train longer. At equal loss, a 60B model needs 3.05T tokens, 10% more training compute, and serves 37% cheaper; a 30B model needs 13T tokens and 2.4 times the compute but breaks even near \(10^{13}\) served tokens. (2) Data: if you cannot get 1.8T fresh tokens of the quality you want, repeating up to about four epochs costs little (93% effective). (3) MoE: replace \(N\) by the activated count and refit. (4) Systems: a size that shards well (tensor and pipeline degrees, KV-cache budget at serving) can beat the nominal optimum for the same loss. (5) Data quality: cleaner data shifts the optimum toward more parameters (DeepSeek: exponent 0.45 to 0.58).

Core Why did Kaplan and Chinchilla reach different conclusions?

Kaplan et al. found \(N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.73}\) (tokens \(\propto C^{0.27}\)); Hoffmann et al. found about \(C^{0.5}\) for both. Documented reasons, none sufficient alone:

  • Schedule and scale (Hoffmann et al.): Kaplan used one fixed cosine schedule and token count for every run, which overstates the loss of shorter budgets and favors bigger models, and their runs were small (many under 100M parameters) on a curved frontier. Later work found the schedule is not the main cause.
  • Porian et al.: reproducing Kaplan gave exponent 0.835 (RefinedWeb); counting the output layer's FLOPs gave 0.706, scaling warmup with model size 0.602, matching decay to the token budget 0.571, and tuning learning rate, batch size and AdamW \(\beta_2\) per size 0.497.
  • Pearce and Song: Kaplan counted non-embedding parameters at small scale, where embeddings are a large share; simulating Chinchilla's surface under those conditions gives a local exponent of 0.74 to 0.78, close to 0.73.
  • Data: the exponent also moves with data quality (DeepSeek: 0.45 to 0.58).

Takeaway: exponents depend on the protocol. Extrapolating 4 to 6 orders of magnitude multiplies a small exponent error into a very large allocation error (Kaplan's rule is 8 times larger than Chinchilla's at \(10^{24}\) FLOPs). Kaplan's \(L(N)\) and \(L(D)\) fits are fine; the allocation was biased.

Core Derive the compute-optimal allocation from \(L = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}\) with \(C = 6ND\).

Substitute \(D = C/(6N)\): \(L(N) = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B (6N/C)^{\beta}\). Set \(dL/dN = -\alpha A N^{-\alpha-1} + \beta B (6/C)^{\beta} N^{\beta-1} = 0\). Multiply by \(N\): \(\alpha A N^{-\alpha} = \beta B (6N/C)^{\beta} = \beta B D^{-\beta}\), the balance of marginal returns. Then \(N^{\alpha+\beta} = (\alpha A/\beta B)(C/6)^{\beta}\), so

\[ N_{\text{opt}} = G (C/6)^{a},\quad D_{\text{opt}} = C/(6N_{\text{opt}}) = G^{-1}(C/6)^{b},\quad G = \left(\tfrac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}},\ a = \tfrac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta},\ b = \tfrac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}. \]

Notes: \(E\) drops out; \(a + b = 1\); the stationary point is a minimum because \(L\) is a sum of exponentials in \(\ln N\). The optimal loss is \(E + (1 + \alpha/\beta) A N_{\text{opt}}^{-\alpha} = E + K C^{-\gamma}\), \(\gamma = \alpha\beta/(\alpha+\beta)\). \(D/N = G^{-2}(C/6)^{b-a}\) is constant only if \(\alpha = \beta\). Numbers: Epoch constants give \(G = 0.1196\), \(a = 0.5126\), and at \(10^{24}\) FLOPs 96B parameters and 1.74T tokens.

Core Why train an 8B model on 15T tokens if Chinchilla says about 160B?

Chinchilla optimizes loss per training FLOP; a product optimizes total cost, and serving dominates for a widely used model. A compute-optimal 8B model stops at \(20 \times 8.03\text{B} = 161\)B tokens. Llama 3 8B used \(6 \times 8.03\text{B} \times 15\text{T} = 7.2 \times 10^{23}\) FLOPs instead, 93 times more tokens. Those FLOPs, spent Chinchilla-style, buy a model of about 78B parameters trained on 1.6T tokens, which is 10 times more expensive to serve.

Total cost is \(6ND_{\text{tr}} + 2ND_{\text{inf}}\), so the smaller model wins once served tokens exceed a break-even volume \(T^{*} = 3(N_2 D_2 - N_1 D_1)/(N_1 - N_2)\). Illustratively (Epoch constants, extrapolated), an equal-loss compute-optimal model would be about 34B parameters on 0.65T tokens, 5.5 times cheaper to train and 4.2 times dearer to serve, with break-even near \(10^{13}\) served tokens. Loss is nearly flat in the size-versus-tokens trade near the optimum, so shrinking costs little training compute at first (60B: +10%) and only grows near a wall \(N_{\min}\) where the parameter term alone uses up the loss budget. Real cost favors even smaller models because decoding runs at a small fraction of training's FLOP utilization. Caveats: the law is optimistic about very high tokens per parameter, and the flagship also improves the small models in post-training (Llama 3 §1).

Core Does lower pretraining loss guarantee better downstream performance?

No, though within one family and data recipe the relationship is monotone and predictable. Reasons it is not a guarantee: (1) Metrics are nonlinear in loss. If per-token accuracy is \(p\), exact-match on \(L\) tokens is about \(p^{L}\), so smooth loss gives a sharp benchmark curve; Schaeffer et al. show that more than 92% of BIG-Bench "emergent" abilities appear under two metrics and vanish under continuous ones (without claiming emergence is impossible). (2) Loss is comparable only on the same tokenizer and text (use bits per byte across tokenizers). (3) Data mixture: lowering loss on one distribution can leave others flat. (4) Post-training (RL, tool use, test-time compute) adds capability the base loss does not determine. (5) Single tasks are noisy even when averages are predictable.

What does work: a two-step map. Llama 3 fit a linear relation between the correct answer's normalized NLL and log FLOPs on models up to \(10^{22}\) FLOPs, then a sigmoid from NLL to accuracy (using Llama 2 models for the upper range), and forecast ARC Challenge for the 405B model across four orders of magnitude, slightly underestimating it. Gadre et al. predicted average top-1 error over 17 tasks for a 6.9B model within 0.05% relative error using 20 times less compute, but single tasks missed by up to 82%.

Core Design question: how would you fit a scaling law from small runs to size a large run, and how far can you trust the extrapolation?

Procedure. Freeze tokenizer, data and architecture shape. Tune batch size, learning rate and warmup per size (small sweeps, fitted laws, or μP). Pick 5 to 9 compute budgets over at least two orders of magnitude and, at each, 5 to 9 model sizes that bracket the optimum on both sides, training each on \(D = C/6N\) with a schedule matched to the budget (or a constant rate plus cooldown to reuse runs). Fit a parabola in \(\ln N\) to each IsoFLOP curve, then power laws \(N^*(C)\), \(D^*(C)\), \(L^*(C)\) (Approach 2); cross-check by fitting the parametric surface on all points (sum the Huber loss over points, many initializations, check convergence). Llama 3 did this from \(6 \times 10^{18}\) to \(10^{22}\) FLOPs with 40M to 16B parameter models, then extrapolated to \(3.8 \times 10^{25}\).

Trust. Published track records validate 3 to 4 orders of magnitude on loss (GPT-4: up to 10,000 times less compute; DeepSeek LLM: about 1,000 times; Gadre et al.: 300 times) and less on benchmarks (20 times). Sources of error: sampling noise (Epoch's exponent interval is about 0.05 wide), rounding (a 0.01 exponent error is a 1.8 times error at \(10^{25}\) FLOPs), protocol confounds, model misspecification at extreme tokens per parameter, and data dependence. Bootstrap the fit, keep full precision, validate with a larger run predicted in advance, and choose a point on the flat part of the valley.

Core You have 10T unique tokens of good text and a budget to process 40T tokens. How much is the repetition worth, and what changes at 100T?

Use the data-constrained formula with \(R^{*}_D = 15.4\): \(D' = U + U R^{*}(1 - e^{-R/R^{*}})\), \(R = D/U - 1\). Four epochs means \(R = 3\), so \(D' = 10\text{T}\,(1 + 15.39 \times 0.177) = 37.3\)T, 93% of the tokens processed. Muennighoff et al. saw only a 0.5% loss penalty at four epochs for an 8.7B model. At 100T (10 epochs, \(R = 9\)) \(D' = 78\)T, 78% effective; at 16 epochs 66%, at 40 epochs 38%; the ceiling is \(U(1 + R^{*}) = 164\)T however long you train.

Allocation: when data-constrained, add epochs faster than parameters (excess parameters decay faster, \(R^{*}_N = 5.3\)); at \(9.3 \times 10^{21}\) FLOPs with 25B unique tokens the recommended model had 27% fewer parameters than Chinchilla's and did better. Alternatives: mix in code (about 2 times more effective tokens in their experiments), filter noisy data, or add synthetic paraphrases (page 12). Caveat: a July 2026 refit finds \(R^{*}\) shrinks with model size and tokens per parameter, so at large scale expect fewer useful epochs.

Deep-dive How would you use scaling laws to decide whether a new architecture is better?

A single-size ablation cannot separate a constant shift from a change in slope. I would train baseline and variant at four or more sizes covering one to two orders of magnitude, with identical data, hyperparameters tuned per size and several seeds for a noise estimate (Kaplan et al. saw about 0.02 nats of seed noise in their setting). Fit \(L(C) = E + K C^{-\gamma}\) to each (a shared \(E\) if the data match) and compute the compute multiplier, the factor by which the baseline needs more compute to match the variant. With equal slopes it is \((K_{\text{base}}/K_{\text{var}})^{1/\gamma}\); if it shrinks with size, the gain is disappearing.

Put the noise in compute units: \(dL/d\ln C = -\gamma (L - E)\), so at \(10^{24}\) FLOPs a 12% multiplier is only 0.003 nats; small effects at scale need paired comparisons on the same data order and many seeds. Then account for costs the loss hides (FLOPs and memory per token, kernel maturity, long-context and downstream behavior) and for extrapolation distance. Be explicit that this is evidence for a decision, not proof: labs register predictions and verify on a larger run before committing the flagship (GPT-4 report). Also check the result on downstream metrics through the two-step map, since a change can help loss and not benchmarks.

Deep-dive Derive the optimality condition when serving cost is included, and explain how you would choose a size under an uncertain serving volume.

Minimize \(6ND + 2N D_{\text{inf}}\) subject to \(L(N, D) = \ell\). The Lagrange conditions are \(6D + 2D_{\text{inf}} = -\lambda \alpha A N^{-\alpha-1}\) and \(6N = -\lambda \beta B D^{-\beta-1}\). Dividing and multiplying by \(N/D\) gives \((1 + D_{\text{inf}}/(3D))\,\beta B D^{-\beta} = \alpha A N^{-\alpha}\): the parameter term is worth more than the data term by the factor \(1 + D_{\text{inf}}/3D\), so \(N\) falls and \(D\) rises as serving grows. With \(D_{\text{inf}} = 0\) it is Chinchilla's balance. There is no closed form for \(N(\ell, D_{\text{inf}})\) in general (Sardana and Frankle, Theorem A.1), so solve numerically; with the Epoch constants the cheapest models at loss 1.960 are 96B, 79B, 48B and 29B for 0, 1T, 10T and 100T served tokens.

Under uncertainty: the total-cost curve has a wall at \(N_{\min} = (A/(\ell - E))^{1/\alpha}\) (14B here) and rises slowly on the large-model side, so an error toward a slightly larger model is cheap and one toward the wall is expensive; choose \(N\) against a pessimistic (high) serving forecast that stays clear of the wall, then check dollars, not FLOPs (decode utilization is far below training's, pushing toward smaller models), latency, KV-cache memory and the fitted law's optimism at high tokens per parameter.

Deep-dive The published Approach 3 fit implies about 70 tokens per parameter while Approaches 1 and 2 and the Chinchilla run itself use 20. What went wrong, and how do you fit such a law robustly?

Besiroglu et al. reconstructed 240 runs from Hoffmann et al.'s Figure 4 and refit. They found (1) the printed constants are rounded, and rounding \(\beta\) from 0.2849 to 0.28 alone biases the data term by about 13% at \(10^{11}\) tokens and moves the implied ratio (from 62 to 98 tokens per parameter at \(10^{24}\) FLOPs in our calculation); (2) the authors' L-BFGS minimizer stopped early because the Huber loss was averaged over points rather than summed, leaving a worse fit and confidence intervals so narrow (0.454 to 0.455 on \(a\)) that they would need about 600,000 runs. The corrected fit (\(E = 1.8172\), \(\alpha = 0.3478\), \(\beta = 0.3658\), \(a = 0.5126 \pm 0.018\)) is consistent with Approaches 1 and 2.

Robust practice: fit in log space with a Huber loss, sum over points, use a grid of initializations, verify convergence (compare against a second optimizer), bootstrap for intervals, treat extreme outliers (very few tokens per parameter) explicitly, keep full precision in constants, and cross-check the parametric allocation against IsoFLOP minima. If the two disagree, distrust the parametric fit first.

Deep-dive Using DeepSeek's laws, what batch size and learning rate would you start from for a \(10^{24}\)-FLOP run, and how much would you trust them?

\(B_{\text{opt}} = 0.2920\,C^{0.3271}\) gives \(2.07 \times 10^{7}\) tokens (about 5,050 sequences of 4,096 tokens, or 84,000 steps for 1.74T tokens); \(\eta_{\text{opt}} = 0.3118\,C^{-0.1250}\) gives \(3.1 \times 10^{-4}\). Each tenfold increase in compute multiplies the batch by 2.1 and the learning rate by 0.75. Checked against DeepSeek's own 7B and 67B models, the laws come within 10% on batch size and 3% on learning rate.

Trust: moderate, as a starting point. The fit uses budgets up to \(2 \times 10^{19}\) and extrapolates about five orders of magnitude; the near-optimal region is a broad band (0.25% loss tolerance), so the order of magnitude matters more than the digits; and the optimum shifts slightly with the model-versus-data split at equal compute, which \(C\) alone does not capture. Porian et al. find batch-size predictions that agree with it in exponent but learning-rate predictions that differ by a factor of 2 to 3, so I would run a short sweep at \(10^{20}\) or use μP to confirm the learning rate. Critical batch size tracks loss and data size, not model size (Kaplan; Zhang et al.), so an over-trained run tolerates larger batches; frontier runs also ramp the batch during training (Llama 3: 4M to 16M tokens).
