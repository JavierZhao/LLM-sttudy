GPUs, kernels & FlashAttention

P1 ~60 min Prereqs: 03 Attention, 06 KV cache, 15 Memory, precision & parallelism

Whether a transformer layer runs at 5% or 70% of a GPU's peak depends less on how many FLOPs it needs than on how many bytes it moves and how well the work fits the hardware. This page builds the GPU model that lets you predict that (memory hierarchy, roofline, fusion), then works through FlashAttention 1 to 4 and the decode-time kernels built on it, including the online-softmax derivation that interviewers like to ask for on a whiteboard.

What interviewers probe
  • Roofline reasoning: "Is this op compute- or memory-bound?" Given a shape, compute the arithmetic intensity, compare it with the H100 ridge (about 295 FLOPs/byte), and say why a batch-1 decode matmul sits near 1.
  • FlashAttention in full: derive the online-softmax update, explain tiling and recomputation, and say exactly why the kernel is faster with the same FLOPs (it moves fewer bytes, it does not compute less).
  • What changed in FlashAttention-2, -3 and -4, and why: non-matmul FLOPs are 15 to 500 times more expensive than matmul FLOPs (the top end is the exponential on Blackwell), so each version restructures work around the next bottleneck of new hardware.
  • Decode-time kernels: why one query token needs a split-over-keys kernel (FlashDecoding), and how paged and MLA kernels differ.
  • Practical optimization: "How would you speed up LayerNorm, then matmul, then GELU?" Count bytes, fuse what is memory-bound, and measure before and after.

The GPU in one page

A GPU is built for throughput, not latency. An H100 SXM has 132 streaming multiprocessors (SMs) 流式多处理器 [NVIDIA Hopper in-depth]. Each SM keeps many threads resident and hides the hundreds of cycles of a memory access by switching to other ready threads, rather than by large caches. Threads execute in groups of 32 called warps 线程束. Warps are grouped into thread blocks 线程块 (also called CTAs), which live on one SM and share its shared memory. A launch of a kernel 核函数 creates a grid of thread blocks, and the hardware hands blocks to SMs as they free up. If one block fits per SM at a time, a grid of \(132k\) equal blocks runs in \(k\) "waves", and one extra block costs a whole extra wave.

Inside an SM sit four tensor cores 张量核心 (528 on the chip), CUDA cores for FP32 and integer work, a special-function unit for exp and friends, 256 KB of registers, and 256 KB of combined L1 cache and shared memory 共享内存, of which up to 228 KB can be shared memory [NVIDIA Hopper in-depth]. Three throughput numbers explain most of this page:

  • Tensor cores: 528 cores \(\times\) 1,024 dense bf16 FLOPs per clock \(\times\) 1.83 GHz = 989.4 TFLOP/s (page 06).
  • FP32 on CUDA cores: 67 TFLOP/s [H100 datasheet], 15 times lower (989.4 / 67 = 14.8).
  • Special functions such as \(e^x\): 16 per clock per SM, so \(16 \times 132 \times 1.83\text{ GHz} = 3.9\) TFLOP/s, 256 times lower [FlashAttention-3, §3.1, fn. 5].

Only matmul-shaped work runs at the headline speed. Everything that is not a matmul, including the softmax inside attention, is comparatively slow, and that fact drives every FlashAttention revision after the first.

Two consequences. First, what you may keep on chip is small: one 128 x 128 bf16 tile is 32 KiB, so a kernel that holds a Q tile, a K tile and a V tile plus buffers is already using a large part of an SM's 228 KB. Second, HBM is the level that scales with model size and the level where the two big costs of LLMs live (weights and the KV cache), which is why page 06 could bound decode speed by bytes over bandwidth alone.

Why tensor cores want particular shapes and dtypes

A tensor core executes a small fixed matrix multiply-accumulate \(D = AB + C\) per instruction. On Ampere a warp issues mma with shapes such as m16n8k16. On Hopper a warpgroup of four warps issues the asynchronous wgmma with shape m64nNk16 (N up to 256) and can read both operands straight from shared memory [Luo et al. 2024, §III.B]. On Blackwell the tile grows to 128 x N and the result goes into a new 256 KB per-SM tensor memory instead of registers [FlashAttention-4, §2.2]. Software must therefore cut every matmul into tiles of these sizes, and three consequences follow.

  • Dimensions that are not tile multiples waste work. NVIDIA recommends multiples of 8 elements for FP16 (64 on A100) [NVIDIA matmul guide]. Example with 128 x 128 output tiles and one tile per SM at a time: a 4096 x 4096 output has 32 x 32 = 1,024 tiles, which is 7.76 waves on 132 SMs and so 8 waves (97% wave efficiency). A 4097 x 4097 output has 33 x 33 = 1,089 tiles, 6.3% more tile work for 0.05% more output, and 8.25 waves rounds up to 9 (92%). These are called tile and wave quantization.
  • Only some dtypes hit the tensor cores. Hopper supports FP16, BF16, TF32, FP8 and INT8 inputs with wider accumulation; FP8 doubles the per-SM rate relative to FP16 and BF16 [FlashAttention-3, §2.2]. A plain FP32 matmul without TF32 runs on the 15 times slower CUDA cores.
  • Layout constraints leak into algorithms. FP8 wgmma accepts only operands that are contiguous along the reduction dimension, so FlashAttention-3's FP8 kernel must transpose the V tile inside the kernel [FlashAttention-3, §3.3].

The roofline model: bytes versus FLOPs

"Is this operation fast?" has no answer without a bound to compare against. Williams, Waterman and Patterson give a two-number bound. They define operational intensity as operations per byte of DRAM traffic, counted after the caches have filtered the accesses [Williams et al. 2009, p. 66]. We call it arithmetic intensity 计算强度 (also written 算术强度, as on page 06), and count a multiply-add as 2 FLOPs. A kernel with intensity \(I\), on a machine with peak compute \(P\) and memory bandwidth \(\beta\), attains at most

\[ \text{attainable FLOP/s} = \min\!\big(P,\ \beta \cdot I\big), \qquad I_{\text{ridge}} = P / \beta \]
The roofline. The ridge point is the least intensity that can reach peak compute

Left of the ridge an op is memory-bound 访存受限: its time is bytes over bandwidth. Right of it, it is compute-bound 计算受限: its time is FLOPs over peak. For the H100 SXM, \(989.4\text{ TFLOP/s} / 3.35\text{ TB/s} = 295\) FLOPs per byte, the number used on pages 06 and 07. For the B200, FlashAttention-4 lists 2.25 PFLOP/s dense BF16 [FlashAttention-4, §2.2], and NVIDIA's DGX B200 page lists 64 TB/s of HBM3e bandwidth for its 8 GPUs, so 8 TB/s each [DGX B200]. The HGX B200 table agrees on the compute side: 36 PFLOP/s of BF16 for 8 GPUs with sparsity, dense half of that, so 18 / 8 = 2.25 PFLOP/s per GPU [HGX]. The ridge is \(2250 / 8 = 281\). Tensor throughput grew 2.3 times and bandwidth 2.4 times, so the ridge barely moved: an op that is memory-bound on an H100 is memory-bound on a B200.

Pitfall: which peak

NVIDIA's headline H100 numbers (1,979 TFLOP/s for BF16) include 2:4 structured sparsity. Dense is half of that (page 04). Real kernels also run below the dense peak because of power throttling: DeepSeek measures a practical bf16 peak of about 865 TFLOP/s on an H800 that runs at roughly 1,600 MHz [FlashMLA deep dive]. A roofline drawn with the wrong peak moves the ridge by a factor of 2.

A matmul, by shape

Take \(y = xW\) with \(x \in \R^{B \times k}\) and \(W \in \R^{k \times n}\) in bf16. It does \(2Bkn\) FLOPs and, if every operand crosses HBM once, moves \(2(Bk + kn + Bn)\) bytes. The intensity is

\[ I = \frac{Bkn}{Bk + kn + Bn}\ \text{FLOPs/byte}, \qquad I = \frac{dB}{d + 2B} \ \text{ for a square } d \times d \text{ weight} \]

(NVIDIA's guide uses the same formula). For small \(B\) this is \(\approx B\), the "tokens per weight read" rule from page 06. For large \(B\) it saturates at \(d/2\), because the activations start to dominate the bytes. Kernel fusion, torch.compile and Triton

In eager PyTorch every operation is its own kernel. Each one reads its inputs from HBM and writes its output back, so a chain of \(n\) elementwise ops moves the tensor through HBM \(n\) times. For memory-bound ops the FLOPs are irrelevant, only bytes matter, and kernel fusion 算子融合 replaces the \(n\) round trips by one: load once, apply the whole chain in registers, store once. Fusion does not reduce FLOPs and does nothing for a compute-bound matmul; it removes traffic.

The reduction here (a row sum) is exactly what fusion has to handle carefully: the fused kernel keeps the whole row in registers or shared memory so the reduction never touches HBM.

torch.compile and Triton

Two tools make this practical. torch.compile (TorchDynamo plus TorchInductor) captures the Python model into a graph and generates fused Triton kernels for pointwise and reduction ops on GPU; matmuls go to cuBLAS or to Triton templates with the pointwise epilogue fused in, and mode="reduce-overhead" uses CUDA graphs to remove launch overhead [Ansel et al. 2024, §4.4 to 4.7]. Across 180+ models on an A100 it gives a 2.27 times inference and 1.41 times training geometric-mean speedup over eager (abstract). The ablation is instructive: switching off fusion drops the float16 HuggingFace inference speedup from 1.91 times to 1.68 times and training from 1.45 to 1.27 times, and switching off fusion and inlining together makes it slower than eager (0.80 times and 0.59 times), because TorchInductor decomposes big operators into many small ones and relies on fusion to put them back together [Ansel et al. 2024, Tab. 4].

Triton is "a language and compiler centered around the concept of tile" [Tillet et al. 2019]. You write the program for one thread block on a tile of data, and the compiler maps it to threads, vectorized loads and shared memory. It is a common level at which research kernels are written today, and the loop order that FlashAttention-2 adopted was first implemented in Triton (below). A fused RMSNorm forward, one program per row:

import torch, triton
import triton.language as tl

@triton.jit
def rmsnorm_fwd(x_ptr, w_ptr, y_ptr, row_stride, n_cols, eps, BLOCK: tl.constexpr):
    row = tl.program_id(0)                          # one program (thread block) per row
    cols = tl.arange(0, BLOCK)                      # BLOCK = next power of two >= n_cols
    mask = cols < n_cols
    x = tl.load(x_ptr + row * row_stride + cols, mask=mask, other=0.0).to(tl.float32)  # the only HBM read of x
    inv_rms = 1.0 / tl.sqrt(tl.sum(x * x, axis=0) / n_cols + eps)   # the reduction stays on chip
    w = tl.load(w_ptr + cols, mask=mask, other=0.0).to(tl.float32)  # small, served from L2
    y = x * inv_rms * w
    tl.store(y_ptr + row * row_stride + cols, y.to(tl.bfloat16), mask=mask)             # the only HBM write

def rmsnorm(x, w, eps=1e-6):                        # x: (n_rows, n_cols) bf16, contiguous
    y = torch.empty_like(x)
    rmsnorm_fwd[(x.shape[0],)](x, w, y, x.stride(0), x.shape[1], eps, BLOCK=triton.next_power_of_2(x.shape[1]))
    return y

Not run on a GPU in this course build (nothing here was timed). It was checked on a CPU with Triton's interpreter (TRITON_INTERPRET=1, Triton 3.3) against a PyTorch RMSNorm, matching to bf16 rounding. It assumes one row fits in a block (fine up to tens of thousands of columns) and omits the backward kernel.

Kernel fusion
Problem
Eager execution launches one kernel per op, so every intermediate tensor makes a round trip through HBM. Most non-matmul ops have intensity below 3 FLOPs/byte, so those trips are their entire cost.
Idea
Merge a chain of ops that share an iteration space into one kernel: load once, compute in registers, store once. Compilers (TorchInductor, XLA) do it automatically for pointwise and reduction chains; hand-written kernels (Triton, CUDA) do it where the compiler cannot.
Why it works
Memory-bound time is bytes over bandwidth, so cutting bytes cuts time proportionally: 9 times fewer bytes for RMSNorm, about 4 times for softmax.
Evidence
TorchInductor: combining kernels (inlining during lowering plus fusion during scheduling) is the largest source of its speedups (Tab. 4, §6.4) [Ansel et al. 2024]. Optimizing data movement in a BERT encoder layer (fusion plus data-layout selection) gave 1.30 times over PyTorch and 1.08 times over the hand-tuned DeepSpeed kernels [Ivanov et al. 2021].
Cost
Fused kernels are harder to write, debug and specialize (shape, dtype, layout). Fusing an elementwise chain into a matmul (epilogue fusion) is worthwhile; fusing a matmul with a reduction over its inputs is hard. Recompilation on shape changes and graph breaks limit torch.compile.
Used by
Every modern training and serving stack: fused RMSNorm and RoPE kernels, fused cross-entropy, fused optimizers, and FlashAttention itself (which fuses matmul, softmax and matmul).

Worked example: LayerNorm, matmul, GELU

Take one MLP up-projection over 8,192 tokens (\(BT = 8192\)), \(d = 4096\), \(d_{ff} = 14336\) (Llama-3-8B's width). The matmul is \(2 \cdot 8192 \cdot 4096 \cdot 14336 = 962\) GFLOP, which is 0.97 ms at the 989.4 TFLOP/s peak. Its intensity is 2,294 FLOPs/byte: compute-bound, so nothing to fuse there. The neighbors are different:

  • GELU unfused: the \(8192 \times 14336\) bf16 output is 235 MB; reading and writing it costs 470 MB, or 0.14 ms, which is 14% on top of the matmul.
  • LayerNorm: 8192 x 4096 bf16 is 67 MB, so a read and a write cost 134 MB, 0.04 ms, 4% of the matmul.

The fixes, in order of payoff: fuse GELU into the matmul as an epilogue (the tile is still in registers when GELU is applied, so the 470 MB disappears; cuBLASLt epilogues, CUTLASS, or a TorchInductor Triton template under mode="max-autotune"); make sure LayerNorm is a single fused kernel; and, if it still matters, fold the normalization into the matmul's prologue. The last works because \(\mathrm{LN}(x)W = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\,(\gamma \odot W) + \beta W\): the per-row statistics \(\mu, \sigma\) come from a cheap pre-pass (one read of \(x\)), \(\gamma \odot W\) is precomputed offline, and the matmul kernel applies \((x - \mu)/\sigma\) as it loads each tile. With torch.compile the first two happen with one line, and you check the result in a profile rather than assuming it.

mlp_up = torch.compile(lambda x, w, g, b: torch.nn.functional.gelu(
    torch.nn.functional.layer_norm(x, x.shape[-1:], g, b) @ w), mode="max-autotune")

FlashAttention: exact attention without the \(T \times T\) matrix

The problem: attention is an IO problem

For one head with \(Q, K, V \in \R^{T \times d_h}\), attention is \(O = \softmax(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_h})\,V\) (page 03; the FlashAttention papers write \(N\) for \(T\) and \(d\) for \(d_h\)). The standard implementation is three kernels: compute and write \(S = QK^\top\); read \(S\), compute and write \(P = \softmax(S)\); read \(P\) and \(V\), write \(O\) [Dao et al. 2022, Alg. 0]. \(S\) and \(P\) are \(T \times T\). At \(T = 8192\) with 32 heads in bf16, \(S\) alone is \(32 \cdot 8192^2 \cdot 2\) B = 4 GiB per layer per sequence, and \(P\) is another 4 GiB. Each is written once and read once, so the traffic is about \(4T^2\) elements against \(4Td_h\) for \(Q, K, V, O\). The intensity is

\[ I_{\text{naive}} = \frac{4T^2 d_h}{2 \cdot (4T^2 + 4Td_h)} = \frac{d_h}{2\,(1 + d_h/T)} \approx \frac{d_h}{2} = 64 \ \text{FLOPs/byte at } d_h = 128 \]

nearly independent of \(T\) (for \(T \gg d_h\)) and far below the ridge of 295. Standard attention is memory-bound at any sequence length, and it also stores \(O(T^2)\) numbers for the backward pass. FlashAttention's thesis is that "a missing principle is making attention algorithms IO-aware", that is, accounting for reads and writes between HBM and on-chip SRAM [Dao et al. 2022, abstract]. The plan: never write \(S\) or \(P\) to HBM. Doing that needs two ideas, an online softmax and recomputation in the backward pass.

The obstacle, and the online softmax

To normalize any entry of a row you need the row's maximum (for stability) and its sum of exponentials, both over all \(T\) keys. The standard "safe" softmax reads each row three times (max, sum, normalize), which is 4 memory accesses per element counting the write. Milakov and Gimelshein computed the max and the sum in a single pass by correcting the running sum whenever the running max changes, cutting the softmax to 3 accesses per element and running up to 1.3 times faster on a V100; the inspiration was Welford's online variance [Milakov and Gimelshein 2018, §3]. FlashAttention applies the same idea to the whole attention output, not just the normalizer.

Fix one query row with scores \(s_1, \dots, s_T\) and values \(v_i \in \R^{d_h}\). Process the keys in tiles. After some tiles, keep three quantities over the keys seen so far:

\[ m = \max_i s_i, \qquad \ell = \sum_i e^{s_i - m}, \qquad a = \sum_i e^{s_i - m}\, v_i \in \R^{d_h} \]

The output over the keys seen so far is \(a/\ell\), and the logsumexp is \(L = m + \log \ell\). Now a new tile arrives with scores \(s_i\) (\(i\) in the tile). Let \(m' = \max(m, \max_{\text{tile}} s_i)\) and \(\alpha = e^{m - m'}\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} m' &= \max\!\big(m,\ \max_{i \in \text{tile}} s_i\big), \qquad \alpha = e^{m - m'} \le 1\\ \ell' &= \alpha\, \ell + \sum_{i \in \text{tile}} e^{s_i - m'}\\ a' &= \alpha\, a + \sum_{i \in \text{tile}} e^{s_i - m'}\, v_i \end{aligned} \]
Online softmax update. Final output: O = a / ℓ. Saved for backward: L = m + log ℓ

Why it is correct. For a key seen earlier, \(e^{s_i - m'} = e^{s_i - m}\, e^{m - m'}\). So re-referencing the old sums to the new max means multiplying both \(\ell\) and \(a\) by the same factor \(\alpha\); new keys are added with the new reference directly. By induction over tiles, \(m, \ell, a\) always equal their definitions over all keys seen, and after the last tile \(a/\ell\) is exactly \(\softmax(s)^\top V\). Nothing is approximated, and \(\alpha \le 1\) so nothing overflows. The same algebra gives a merge of two independent partial results \((m_1, \ell_1, a_1)\) and \((m_2, \ell_2, a_2)\): with \(M = \max(m_1, m_2)\), \(\ell = e^{m_1 - M}\ell_1 + e^{m_2 - M}\ell_2\) and \(a = e^{m_1 - M}a_1 + e^{m_2 - M}a_2\). This operator is associative and commutative [Milakov and Gimelshein 2018, §3.1], so partial results can be combined in any order or in a tree. FlashDecoding relies on exactly that (below).

This is the same algebra as combining partial means and variances from two shards of a dataset (Welford, and its parallel form by Chan et al.): keep a few sufficient statistics per shard, then merge them with a rescaling instead of revisiting the data. Physics analyses do this whenever histograms or moments are accumulated over files in parallel. The extra twist is that the "statistic" is a softmax normalizer, and the rescaling factor \(e^{m - m'}\) keeps it in floating-point range.

The tiled algorithm

Split \(Q\) into tiles of \(B_r\) rows and \(K, V\) into tiles of \(B_c\) rows. One thread block owns one \(Q\) tile, keeps it in shared memory, and keeps \(m\), \(\ell\) and the output accumulator \(O_i\) in registers (fp32). It loops over the \(K, V\) tiles: load \(K_j, V_j\), compute the tile \(S_{ij} = Q_i K_j^\top/\sqrt{d_h}\) on chip, apply the update above with \(P_{ij} = e^{S_{ij} - m'}\) (cast to bf16 for the second matmul), and move on. After the last tile it divides by \(\ell\) and writes \(O_i\) and \(L_i = m + \log \ell\). One kernel does matmul, softmax and matmul, so the \(T \times T\) matrix only ever exists as a sequence of \(B_r \times B_c\) tiles (Figure 3). The version below is the FlashAttention-2 loop order, on one head, in PyTorch; the drill has the batched, end-aligned version.

def flash_forward(q, k, v, bq=128, bk=128, causal=True):
    # q, k, v: (T, d) for one head. Real kernels run each outer iteration as its own thread block.
    T, d = q.shape
    out, lse = torch.empty_like(q), torch.empty(T)
    for q0 in range(0, T, bq):                                # outer loop: independent per Q tile
        qi = q[q0:q0 + bq] * d**-0.5                           # (bq, d), stays resident
        m = torch.full((qi.shape[0],), -inf)                   # running max
        l = torch.zeros_like(m)                                # running sum of exp
        acc = torch.zeros_like(qi)                             # running (unnormalized) P @ V
        rows = q0 + torch.arange(qi.shape[0])
        for k0 in range(0, T, bk):                             # inner loop: stream K/V tiles
            if causal and k0 > rows[-1]:
                break                                          # tile entirely above the diagonal: skip
            s = qi @ k[k0:k0 + bk].T                           # (bq, bk): the only score tile
            if causal:                                         # (real kernels mask only diagonal tiles)
                s = s.masked_fill(k0 + torch.arange(s.shape[1]) > rows[:, None], -inf)
            m_new = torch.maximum(m, s.amax(-1))
            alpha = torch.exp(m - m_new)                       # <= 1: rescales what was accumulated
            p = torch.exp(s - m_new[:, None])
            l = l * alpha + p.sum(-1)
            acc = acc * alpha[:, None] + p @ v[k0:k0 + bk]
            m = m_new
        out[q0:q0 + bq] = acc / l[:, None]                     # normalize once, at the end
        lse[q0:q0 + bq] = m + torch.log(l)                     # all the backward pass needs
    return out, lse

Tile sizes. FlashAttention-1 sets \(B_c = \lceil M/4d \rceil\) and \(B_r = \min(\lceil M/4d \rceil, d)\) for an on-chip memory of \(M\) elements, so that the four blocks \(K_j, V_j, Q_i, O_i\) fit at once (Alg. 1). For \(M \approx 100{,}000\) and \(d = 128\) that gives \(B_c = 196\), \(B_r = 128\). FlashAttention-2 uses tiles from \(\{64, 128\} \times \{64, 128\}\) chosen by head dimension and shared-memory size, and warns that bigger tiles cut shared-memory traffic but need more registers and memory: "past a certain block size, register spilling causes significant slowdown, or the amount of shared memory required is larger than what the GPU has available" [Dao 2023, §3.3]. FlashAttention-1's own sweep shows the runtime falling as the block grows, then flattening beyond 256 [Dao et al. 2022, §3.2].

Causal masking. With a causal mask, about half of the tiles are fully masked and can be skipped, and only tiles that straddle the diagonal need the mask applied. FlashAttention-2 reports about a 1.7 to 1.8 times speedup over the unmasked case from this [Dao 2023, §3.1.1]. One trap: a row whose whole first tile is masked has \(m = -\infty\), and \(e^{-\infty - (-\infty)}\) is NaN. Start-aligned causal masks never hit it (every row sees key 0), but sliding-window or end-aligned masks can, so production kernels guard it (the drill's tests include this case).

The backward pass: recompute, do not store

Backprop through attention normally needs \(P\) (or \(S\)). FlashAttention stores neither. The forward pass saves only \(O\) and the row logsumexp \(L\), \(O(T)\) numbers, and the backward pass recomputes each tile of \(S\) and \(P\) on chip from \(Q\), \(K\) and \(L\), which is a form of selective gradient checkpointing 重计算 [Dao et al. 2022, §3.1]. Per tile:

\[ P_{ij} = e^{S_{ij} - L_i},\quad dV_j \mathrel{+}= P_{ij}^\top dO_i,\quad dP_{ij} = dO_i V_j^\top,\quad dS_{ij} = P_{ij} \circ (dP_{ij} - D_i),\quad dQ_i \mathrel{+}= \tfrac{dS_{ij} K_j}{\sqrt{d}},\quad dK_j \mathrel{+}= \tfrac{dS_{ij}^\top Q_i}{\sqrt{d}} \]

with \(D_i = \mathrm{rowsum}(dO_i \circ O_i)\) [Dao 2023, Alg. 2]. Two facts make this cheap. First, \(P = e^{S - L}\) needs no rescaling, because \(L\) already contains the full row's normalizer. Second, the softmax Jacobian needs \(\sum_j P_{ij}\, dP_{ij}\), a reduction over all \(T\) keys, but \(\sum_j P_{ij}\,(dO_i \cdot v_j) = dO_i \cdot \sum_j P_{ij} v_j = dO_i \cdot O_i\), a length-\(d\) dot product that the forward output already provides ([Dao et al. 2022, App. B.4]).

The FLOP cost. The forward pass has 2 matmuls (\(QK^\top\) and \(PV\)), \(4T^2 d\) FLOPs. Standard backward has 4 (\(dV\), \(dP\), \(dQ\), \(dK\), with \(P\) stored), FlashAttention's has 5 because it recomputes \(S = QK^\top\). Total 7 matmuls instead of 6, about 17% more FLOPs. The benchmarks accordingly count the backward pass as 2.5 times the forward pass (5 matmuls against 2) [Dao 2023, §4.1]. The paper's GPT-2-medium measurement (A100, \(N = 1024\), head dim 64, 16 heads, batch 64, forward plus backward) shows the trade: FLOPs rise about 13% (75.2 versus 66.6 GFLOPs in their table) while HBM traffic drops from 40.3 GB to 4.4 GB and runtime from 41.7 ms to 7.3 ms [Dao et al. 2022, Fig. 2].

The IO complexity

Let \(M\) be the SRAM size in elements, with \(d \le M \le Td\). Standard attention needs \(\Theta(Td + T^2)\) HBM accesses. FlashAttention needs \(\Theta(T^2 d^2 M^{-1})\): each \(K, V\) tile of size \(\Theta(M)\) is loaded once, and for each of the \(\Theta(Td/M)\) such tiles all of \(Q\) and \(O\) (\(\Theta(Td)\) elements) stream through. A matching lower bound says no exact algorithm can beat \(o(T^2 d^2 M^{-1})\) for all \(M\) in that range [Dao et al. 2022, Thm. 2, Prop. 3]. FLOPs stay \(O(T^2 d)\) and the extra memory is \(O(T)\) (Thm. 1), which is the statistics \(L\) (or \(m, \ell\)). The same tiling and recomputation idea appeared earlier for memory only: Rabe and Staats reduced the footprint but not the traffic, and ran at about the same speed as standard attention or slightly slower, while FlashAttention targets HBM accesses, which set the runtime [Dao et al. 2022, App. B.5]; [Rabe and Staats 2021].

The ratio of the first two rows is \((4T^2 + 4Td_h)/(4Td_h) = T/d_h + 1 = 65\): standard attention moves 65 times the compulsory traffic, which is the room FlashAttention exploits. FlashAttention (tiling + online softmax + recomputation)
Problem
Standard attention writes and reads two \(T \times T\) matrices in HBM. It is memory-bound (\(I \approx d_h/2\)), needs \(O(T^2)\) memory, and limits context length.
Idea
Fuse attention into one kernel; compute the softmax with running (max, sum) statistics over key tiles so the score matrix exists only as on-chip tiles; in the backward pass recompute the tiles from \(Q, K, L\).
Why it works
Runtime is set by HBM traffic (\(\Theta(T^2 d^2/M)\) instead of \(\Theta(T^2)\)), and the online softmax is an exact rewrite, so nothing is approximated. Recomputation trades about 17% more FLOPs for never storing \(P\).
Evidence
15% end-to-end on BERT-large versus the MLPerf 1.1 record and 3 times on GPT-2 [Dao et al. 2022, abstract], 2 to 4 times faster than optimized baselines for the attention layer [Dao 2023, abstract]; memory linear in \(T\) (Thm. 1).
Cost
Kernels are specialized per head dimension, dtype and GPU generation; exotic score modifications (biases, masks) need kernel support; the backward pass is complex. Numerically it is at least as accurate as standard attention: in FlashAttention-3's outlier test the FP16 RMSE is \(1.9 \times 10^{-4}\) versus \(3.2 \times 10^{-4}\) for the standard implementation, because the softmax intermediates stay in fp32 [FlashAttention-3, Tab. 3].
Used by
Essentially every LLM training and serving stack. PyTorch's scaled_dot_product_attention dispatches to FlashAttention-2 by default, or to the memory-efficient (xformers) or math implementations, and to a cuDNN backend on recent NVIDIA GPUs; another flash implementation can be registered with torch.nn.attention.activate_flash_attention_impl [PyTorch docs, 2.14].

FlashAttention-2, -3 and -4: chasing the tensor cores

FlashAttention-1 was 2 to 4 times faster than standard attention, yet reached only 25 to 40% of an A100's theoretical FLOP/s (30 to 50% forward, 25 to 35% backward), while a good GEMM gets 80 to 90% [Dao 2023, abstract, §1]. Each later version finds why on the newest hardware and restructures the work. The common cause is the asymmetry from the first section: matrix units get faster every generation while everything else does not.

FlashAttention-2 (A100, 2023)

Three changes, all about keeping the tensor cores busy [Dao 2023, §3]:

  1. Fewer non-matmul FLOPs. Each non-matmul FLOP costs as much as 16 matmul FLOPs, so the update is reorganized. Do not rescale the output by \(\ell\) at every step: keep an unnormalized \(\tilde O\) and divide once at the end, \(\tilde O' = \alpha \tilde O + \tilde P V_j\), \(O = \tilde O / \ell\). Store only the logsumexp \(L = m + \log \ell\) instead of both \(m\) and \(\ell\).
  2. Parallelize over the sequence. FlashAttention-1 used one thread block per (batch, head): \(B \cdot n_h\) blocks, which is efficient only if that number is large ("say \(\ge 80\)" on a 108-SM A100). With long sequences the batch is small and SMs sit idle. FlashAttention-2 swaps the loops (outer over \(Q\) tiles, inner over \(K, V\) tiles, as in the code above) so query tiles are independent and each becomes its own thread block; in the backward pass each block owns a column block and \(dQ\) is accumulated with atomic adds. The loop swap has a second benefit: FlashAttention-1's outer loop over \(K, V\) forces the partial \(O, m, \ell\) to be read and written to HBM at every step (Alg. 1, lines 8, 12, 13), while now the accumulator stays in registers and \(O\) is written once. The paper credits the loop order and sequence parallelism to Phil Tillet's Triton implementation.
  3. Split-Q work partitioning between warps. FlashAttention-1 split \(K, V\) across the 4 warps of a block ("split-K"), so every warp produced a partial sum of the output that had to go through shared memory, synchronize and be added. FlashAttention-2 splits \(Q\) across warps and gives every warp all of \(K, V\): each warp owns independent output rows and no communication is needed.

Grouped-query and multi-query attention are handled by indexing: the kernel reads KV head \(\lfloor i/g \rfloor\) for query head \(i\) instead of copying, and the backward pass sums \(dK, dV\) over the query heads that share a KV head (page 07). Results: about 2 times FlashAttention-1, up to 230 TFLOP/s (73% of the A100's 312) in the forward pass, and up to 225 TFLOP/s per GPU (72% MFU) when training GPT-style models (the 2.7B model at 8K context). That MFU counts attention FLOPs without halving them for the causal mask, a convention choice the paper states explicitly. Run unchanged on an H100, it reaches up to 335 TFLOP/s (Fig. 7), about a third of the peak.

FlashAttention-3 (H100, 2024)

FlashAttention-2 achieves only 35% utilization on an H100 [Shah et al. 2024, abstract]. Hopper adds asynchronous hardware (the TMA copy engine, and asynchronous wgmma tensor-core instructions), and FlashAttention-3 builds three techniques on it:

  1. Warp specialization (producer-consumer asynchrony). One producer warpgroup issues TMA loads of \(K_j, V_j\) into a circular shared-memory buffer of \(s\) stages, while consumer warpgroups issue asynchronous wgmma matmuls. Data movement and math overlap, and the producer, which needs almost no registers, gives most of its registers to the consumers (setmaxnreg).
  2. Hide the softmax under the matmuls. This is the key idea, and it has a number behind it. In the forward pass with \(d = 128\) each score element costs \(4d = 512\) matmul FLOPs but one \(\exp\), and the special-function unit is 256 times slower than the tensor cores, so \(\exp\) alone takes about \(256/512 = 50\%\) as long as the matmuls [FlashAttention-3, §3.1]. If the two ran one after the other, the tensor cores would idle about a third of the time (matmuls take 1 unit and \(\exp\) 0.5, so they are busy 1/1.5 of it). FlashAttention-3 overlaps them two ways. Pingpong scheduling: with two consumer warpgroups, barriers force the GEMMs of warpgroup 1 to issue before those of warpgroup 2, so warpgroup 1's softmax runs while warpgroup 2's GEMMs execute, then they swap. Intra-warpgroup pipelining: within one warpgroup, the softmax of tile \(j+1\) overlaps with the asynchronous \(PV\) matmul of tile \(j\), at the price of holding an extra score tile in registers. In the paper's ablation (head dim 128, sequence 8448, 16 heads, batch 4) the full kernel reaches 661 TFLOP/s, against 582 without the softmax pipelining and 570 without warp specialization (Table 2).
  3. FP8. FP8 tensor cores run at twice the BF16 rate, but need two fixes. Layout: FP8 wgmma wants \(V\) contiguous along the sequence axis, so the kernel transposes \(V\) tiles in shared memory and permutes accumulator registers. Accuracy: per-tensor FP8 scaling suffers from outliers, so FlashAttention-3 uses block quantization (one scale per tile) and incoherent processing: multiply \(Q\) and \(K\) by a random orthogonal matrix \(M\) (a product of a Hadamard matrix and random \(\pm 1\) diagonals, applied in \(O(d \log d)\)) before quantizing. Since \((QM)(KM)^\top = QK^\top\), the result is unchanged, but each entry of \(QM\) is a random mix of entries of \(Q\), which spreads outliers out. On synthetic data with 0.1% large outliers the RMSE is \(9.1 \times 10^{-3}\) versus \(2.4 \times 10^{-2}\) for a per-tensor FP8 baseline, 2.6 times lower. Removing the incoherent processing returns it to \(2.4 \times 10^{-2}\), while removing block quantization barely matters (\(9.3 \times 10^{-3}\)): on that data the rotation carries essentially all of the gain (Table 3).

Results: 1.5 to 2.0 times faster than FlashAttention-2 forward and 1.5 to 1.75 times backward; up to 740 TFLOP/s in FP16 (75% of the H100 peak); close to 1.2 PFLOP/s in FP8 [Shah et al. 2024, §4]. Note that these are attention-kernel throughputs on a synthetic benchmark (16K tokens per batch, hidden size 2048), not end-to-end training speed.

FlashAttention-4 (Blackwell, 2026)

FlashAttention-3 does not run on the B200, and a straight port would waste it. Blackwell doubles the BF16 tensor-core rate (8,192 versus 4,096 FLOPs per clock per SM, 2.25 versus about 1 PFLOP/s) while the exponential unit (16 ops per clock per SM) and the shared-memory read rate (128 B per clock per SM) do not change [Zadouri et al. 2026, §2.2] (Zadouri, Hoehnerbach, Shah, Liu, Thakkar and Dao, MLSys 2026). The paper calls this asymmetric hardware scaling. Their roofline for a 128 x 128 x 128 tile gives 1,024 cycles for the matmuls, 1,024 for the exponentials and 768 for shared-memory traffic (Table 1), so the exponential unit is now as busy as the tensor cores. You can see why from the ratio of exponential time to matmul time, \(\frac{MN/16}{4MNd/P_{\text{clk}}} = P_{\text{clk}}/(64\, d)\) with \(P_{\text{clk}}\) the tensor-core FLOPs per clock per SM: at \(d = 128\) this is 0.5 on Hopper (\(P_{\text{clk}} = 4096\)) and 1.0 on Blackwell (8192), and at \(d = 64\) it is 1.0 and 2.0. The paper states that shared-memory traffic and exponentials now exceed the matmul time by 25 to 60% for typical workloads. It also notes that the B300 and GB300 double the exponential throughput to 32 ops per clock per SM, so the exponential bottleneck is specific to the B200 generation. New hardware helps too: 256 KB of tensor memory (TMEM) per SM that holds MMA accumulators outside the register file, 128 x N MMA tiles, fully asynchronous MMAs, and a 2-CTA mode in which a pair of thread blocks jointly issue one MMA. Techniques:

  • A new pipeline. A pingpong-style schedule over two query tiles, with separate softmax warpgroups and a dedicated "correction" warpgroup that rescales the output off the critical path (possible because \(P\) and the accumulators pass through TMEM rather than registers).
  • Software-emulated exponential. Compute \(2^x = 2^{\lfloor x \rfloor} \cdot 2^{x - \lfloor x \rfloor}\) with a polynomial on the FMA units (Cody-Waite range reduction), for 10 to 25% of the entries in each row, while the rest use the hardware unit. A degree-3 polynomial has maximum relative error \(8.8 \times 10^{-5}\) in FP32 (about 600 times worse than the hardware), but after rounding to BF16, whose own error is about \(3.9 \times 10^{-3}\), the results are indistinguishable (Table 2 of the paper).
  • Conditional softmax rescaling. The correction \(\alpha O\) costs a vector multiply per tile, but it is only needed when the running max grows. FlashAttention-4 rescales only when the max increases by more than \(\tau = \log_2 256 = 8\) (in its base-2 units, a factor of 256). Otherwise it keeps the old reference max, so the accumulated exponentials can be as large as 256 but no larger (harmless for fp32 accumulators and a BF16 probability tile; my reading, the paper only states the threshold). Because \(\ell\) is accumulated relative to the same reference, the final division by \(\ell\) cancels it and the output stays exact. To avoid warp divergence, a warp rescales if any thread in it needs to.
  • Backward pass. The backward pass is bound by shared-memory traffic (3,328 cycles versus 2,560 of MMA for a 128 tile). Tensor memory holds more intermediates, and the 2-CTA mode lets each block stage half of one operand, cutting shared-memory traffic and halving the atomic adds that accumulate \(dQ\). A deterministic mode (semaphore-ordered reductions) runs at up to 75% of the non-deterministic speed, which matters for reproducible reinforcement learning.
  • Engineering. Written in CuTe-DSL embedded in Python: compile time for one kernel drops from 55 s to 2.5 s (forward) and 45 s to 1.4 s (backward) versus FlashAttention-3's C++ templates.

Result: up to 1,613 TFLOP/s of BF16 on a B200 (71% of the theoretical 2.25 PFLOP/s), up to 1.3 times faster than cuDNN 9.13 and 2.7 times faster than Triton. The authors add a caveat: since their initial release, newer cuDNN versions "have incorporated many of the techniques described in this paper, yielding similar performance to FA4" [Zadouri et al. 2026, Fig. 4 caption]. As of September 2026, treat FlashAttention-4 as the reference design for Blackwell-generation attention, and expect the vendor library to track it.

Decode-time kernels: FlashDecoding, paged and MLA

FlashAttention is designed for prefill and training, where there are thousands of query rows to spread across the GPU. Decoding has one query token per sequence (page 06), and the standard kernel launches one thread block per (batch, head, query tile). For Llama-3-8B at batch 1 that is 32 blocks (or 8 if a block serves a whole KV group) on 132 SMs: 24% or 6% of the chip is working, on a kernel whose entire job is streaming a long KV cache. The Flash-Decoding announcement makes the point for a batch smaller than the SM count (108 on an A100): "the operation will only use a small part of the GPU", and for batch 1 "FlashAttention will use less than 1% of the GPU" [Dao et al. 2023]. The blog does not show how it counts; the 24% and 6% above count one block per head.

Why idle SMs matter for a bandwidth-bound kernel: to keep HBM busy the chip needs bytes in flight equal to bandwidth times latency (Little's law; the roofline paper makes the same point when it says peak bandwidth needs many memory operations in flight). With the 479-cycle HBM latency measured on an H800 PCIe at up to 1.755 GHz, that is \(3.35 \times 10^{12} \times 273\text{ ns} \approx\) 0.9 MB in flight across the chip, about 7 KB per SM. A handful of SMs each with a few pipeline stages cannot sustain it; all of them working can.

FlashDecoding: split over the keys

The fix uses the merge property of the online softmax. Split the \(S_k\) cached keys into \(n_s\) chunks. Each (batch, head, chunk) triple becomes an independent thread block running the FlashAttention inner loop over its chunk and writing a partial output \(O_s\) (normalized within the chunk) plus one scalar per row, the logsumexp \(L_s\). A small second kernel combines them [Flash-Decoding blog]:

\[ L = \log \sum_{s} e^{L_s}, \qquad O = \sum_{s} e^{L_s - L}\, O_s \]
Exact: the outputs are weighted by each chunk's share of the softmax mass

To see that it is exact, write \(O_s = a_s/\ell_s\) and note \(\ell_s e^{m_s} = e^{L_s}\): then \(\sum_s a_s e^{m_s} \big/ \sum_s \ell_s e^{m_s} = \sum_s e^{L_s} O_s \big/ \sum_s e^{L_s}\). The whole reduction is a few lines:

def combine_splits(outs, lses):
    # outs[s]: (T, d), normalized within split s;  lses[s]: (T,), logsumexp of split s
    lse_all = torch.stack(lses)                        # (S, T)
    lse = torch.logsumexp(lse_all, dim=0)              # (T,)
    w = torch.exp(lse_all - lse)                       # (S, T), sums to 1 over the splits
    return (torch.stack(outs) * w[..., None]).sum(0), lse

The cost is small. With \(S_k = 32{,}768\) and \(d = 128\), one KV head's cache is \(2 \cdot 32768 \cdot 128 \cdot 2\) B = 16.8 MB, while 16 splits write \(16 \times (128 + 1) \times 4\) B = 8.3 KB of partial results for one query head, 0.05% as much (0.2% for a GQA group of 4 query heads). Pick \(n_s\) so that (batch \(\times\) KV heads \(\times n_s\)) fills the SMs for a few waves (for batch 1 and 8 KV heads, \(n_s \ge 17\) covers all 132 SMs). The blog reports up to 8 times faster end-to-end generation for very long sequences (CodeLlama-34B, up to 64K tokens), and in an A100 micro-benchmark (batch 1, 16 query heads of dimension 128 sharing 2 KV heads) attention run-time that stays almost constant as the sequence grows to 64K. The split_kv_attention function in the drill implements exactly this.

GQA in the decode kernel

With grouped-query attention (page 07), the \(g = n_h/n_{kv}\) query heads that share a KV head should be processed by the same thread block, so that each \(K, V\) tile loaded from HBM serves \(g\) query rows instead of one. That is the source of the intensity \(g\) derived on page 07, and the reason kernels index KV head \(\lfloor i/g \rfloor\) rather than copying keys. Multi-token verification in speculative decoding (page 17) raises the number of query rows per cache read in the same way.

Paged attention kernels

Serving engines store the KV cache in fixed-size blocks (16 tokens by default in vLLM) addressed through a per-sequence block table (page 17). The attention kernel then fetches KV blocks separately by table lookup and fuses the block read with the attention computation, assigning a warp to each block to keep loads coalesced [Kwon et al. 2023, §4.1, §5.1]. The inner loop is the same online softmax; what changes is where the tile addresses come from. The price is indirection, extra branches and variable sequence lengths: 20 to 26% higher attention-kernel latency than FasterTransformer's contiguous-cache kernel in the paper's microbenchmark (Fig. 18a). That overhead only touches attention, and the capacity it recovers is worth far more: 2 to 4 times higher serving throughput. Modern decode kernels take the block table directly; FlashMLA's flash_mla_with_kvcache takes a block_table, per-sequence lengths, and precomputed tile-scheduler metadata with the split counts [FlashMLA README].

MLA decode: FlashMLA

The absorbed MLA decode of page 07 is an MQA problem over a 576-dimensional shared key and a 512-dimensional shared value (head_dim_k = 576, head_dim_v = 512 in FlashMLA's own documentation). DeepSeek's kernel authors give the roofline directly. FLOPs are about \(2 h_q s_q s_k (d_k + d_v)\) and bytes about \(2 s_k d_k\), so the intensity is \(h_q s_q (d_k + d_v)/d_k \approx 2 h_q s_q\) (exactly \(1.89\, h_q s_q\); page 07's 242 is \(1.89 \times 128\)), with \(s_q\) the query tokens per request (above 1 with speculative decoding). Against an H800 with 3.35 TB/s and a throttled practical peak of about 865 TFLOP/s the ridge is about 258, so with the rounded intensity \(2 h_q s_q\) the kernel is compute-bound once \(h_q s_q \ge 129\), which DeepSeek states as 128 (with the exact 1.89 the threshold is 137). DeepSeek's decode instances do not use tensor parallelism, so \(h_q = 128\), and at \(s_q = 1\) the kernel sits at that threshold (242 against 258); DeepSeek calls it compute-bound [FlashMLA deep dive], and with \(s_q \ge 2\) it is clearly so. Either way this is a decode kernel that lives at the knee of the roofline, where tensor-core utilization matters as much as bandwidth, which confirms the head-count discussion on page 07.

That changes what to optimize, and the design is instructive. FlashAttention-3's pingpong needs two output accumulators per SM to alternate between softmax and matmul, but here the output tile is \(64 \times 512\) in fp32, which is 32,768 of the SM's 65,536 registers, so only one fits. DeepSeek's answer, "seesaw" scheduling, splits the accumulator into left and right halves owned by two warpgroups and orders the mathematically equivalent updates so that one warpgroup's softmax overlaps the other's matmul, using an extra rescale by the later block's factor. Results on an H800 SXM5: up to 660 TFLOP/s in the compute-bound regime (580 before the April 2025 update) and up to 3,000 GB/s in the memory-bound regime, of the 3.35 TB/s peak [FlashMLA README]. The repository also holds the token-level sparse kernels for DeepSeek Sparse Attention with an FP8 KV cache (410 TFLOP/s on an H800 in the compute-bound regime, up to 700 on a B200), and, since the V4 releases, kernels for those models. The deep dive says its algorithm and scheduling are inspired by FlashAttention, Flash-Decoding and CUTLASS, and its splitkv_mla plus combine kernels are the split-over-keys structure above.

The pattern

Every serving attention kernel is the FlashAttention inner loop plus three choices: how to get parallelism (split over batch, heads, queries, or keys), how to address the KV tiles (contiguous, paged, or a latent), and how to lay out heads (one per block, grouped for GQA, or all 128 sharing one latent for MLA). Once you can write the online-softmax loop and its merge, each variant is a change to one of those three.

The profiling mindset

Knowing the theory does not tell you where your model's time goes. The order of work matters more than any single trick.

  1. Write down the bound first. For the op or the whole step, compute the roofline time: bytes over bandwidth, FLOPs over peak, the larger of the two (as on page 06, and drilled on page 34). Without it a number like "12 ms" means nothing.
  2. Measure end to end, then break it down. Track tokens per second and MFU (page 04), then a timeline (torch.profiler, Nsight Systems) for which kernels take the time and where the gaps are. Gaps between kernels mean launch overhead, host synchronization, data loading or communication (page 15); fix those before any kernel.
  3. Classify each large kernel. Nsight Compute reports achieved DRAM bandwidth and tensor-pipe utilization. Near the bandwidth peak: memory-bound, so cut bytes. Near the tensor peak: nothing is left to gain but overlap and precision. Near neither: latency- or occupancy-bound (too few blocks, dependent chains, launch-bound), so increase parallelism or fuse small kernels.
  4. Fix the largest term, in order of payoff: do not materialize (FlashAttention); fuse memory-bound chains; lower precision (bytes and tensor rate); shapes and alignment (tile and wave quantization); CUDA graphs for launch-bound decode; overlap communication.

A minimal way to measure achieved bandwidth, with the details that make it honest:

import torch

def time_gpu(fn, *args, warmup=10, iters=100):
    for _ in range(warmup):
        fn(*args)                                       # also triggers compilation and autotuning
    torch.cuda.synchronize()
    t0, t1 = torch.cuda.Event(enable_timing=True), torch.cuda.Event(enable_timing=True)
    t0.record()
    for _ in range(iters):
        fn(*args)
    t1.record()
    torch.cuda.synchronize()                            # kernels launch asynchronously: wait before reading the clock
    return t0.elapsed_time(t1) / iters / 1e3            # seconds

z = torch.randn(8192, 14336, device="cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16)   # 235 MB: much larger than the 50 MB L2
t = time_gpu(torch.nn.functional.gelu, z)
gbps = 2 * z.numel() * z.element_size() / t / 1e9       # one read plus one write
print(f"{gbps:.0f} GB/s = {gbps / 3350:.0%} of the H100 HBM3 peak")

Conceptual code, not run on a GPU in this course build. Repeated calls on the same small tensor can report more than the HBM peak because the data stays in L2, so use tensors much larger than 50 MB or flush the cache between runs.

Pitfall: "GPU utilization 100%"

The GPU-Util figure of nvidia-smi is, per NVIDIA's NVML definition, the percent of the sample period during which one or more kernels was executing on the GPU [NVML docs]. A kernel that keeps a single SM busy scores 100%. Use tensor-pipe activity, achieved bandwidth, or MFU instead. And check the FLOP convention when comparing MFU numbers: FlashAttention-2's 72% counts attention without the causal halving.

From your jet tagger

Small models spend a large share of their step in launch overhead and data loading rather than in math: with widths near 128 and a few dozen particles per jet, each kernel does so little that the GPU waits on the launch queue (the exact split depends on your hardware and batch size, so measure it). LLM training hides this behind large matrices, and LLM decode brings it back (one token, many tiny kernels), which is why CUDA graphs and fused decode kernels matter so much for serving.

Common misconceptions

  • "FlashAttention approximates attention" or "removes the \(T^2\) cost." It is exact, and its FLOPs are still \(O(T^2 d)\) (the backward pass does about 17% more than standard). What goes away is the \(O(T^2)\) memory and most of the \(T^2\) HBM traffic. Sub-quadratic attention is a different family (page 09).
  • "FlashAttention is faster because it does less work." It does slightly more (recomputation) and is faster because it moves far fewer bytes. Standard attention has intensity \(\approx d_h/2\); the fused kernel is compute-bound. If you say "fewer FLOPs" in an interview, the follow-up question ends the conversation.
  • "Arithmetic intensity is a property of the operation." It depends on the shape (a matmul at \(B = 1\) versus \(B = 1024\) differs by 680 times), on the implementation (unfused versus fused RMSNorm: 9 times more bytes), and on which level of the hierarchy you count traffic against (HBM or L2).
  • "Memory-bound means the kernel is bad." It means the useful lever is bytes. Decode attention and every elementwise op are memory-bound by nature. Judge a memory-bound kernel by achieved bandwidth (80 to 90% of peak is good), not by TFLOP/s.
  • "Faster tensor cores speed up attention proportionally." Only the matmul part. The exponentials take about half as long as the matmuls on an H100 and as long as the matmuls on a B200 (at \(d = 128\)), and shared-memory traffic is close behind on the B200, so FlashAttention-3 and -4 exist to overlap or remove that work. On a B200 a straight port of the H100 kernel would not even run.
  • "A larger tile is always better." Larger tiles cut memory traffic but need more registers and shared memory; past a point registers spill or the kernel does not fit (FlashAttention-2 §3.3), and quantization effects (partial tiles, partial waves) grow.
  • "Decode is slow because attention needs many FLOPs." Decode attention does few FLOPs per byte and, at small batch, also launches too few thread blocks to pull the full HBM bandwidth. The fix is parallelism over the keys (FlashDecoding), not more compute.
  • "torch.compile or nvidia-smi will tell me if it is efficient." Compilation fuses memory-bound chains but does not change your algorithm, and GPU-Util of 100% only means some kernel was running. Measure achieved bandwidth and tensor-pipe utilization, and compare against the roofline bound you computed beforehand.

Interview questions

Warm-up Name the levels of the H100 memory hierarchy with rough sizes and bandwidths, and say which gap FlashAttention is built around.

Registers (256 KB per SM, 33 MB on the chip), shared memory and L1 (256 KB per SM, up to 228 KB as shared memory, about 31 TB/s aggregate at 128 B/clk/SM), L2 (50 MB), and HBM3 (80 GB, 3.35 TB/s). FlashAttention is built around the gap between on-chip SRAM and HBM: SRAM is roughly 9 times faster but only kilobytes per SM against gigabytes. So the design constraint is that the working set of one step (Q, K, V tiles plus accumulators) must fit in about 228 KB, and the goal is to keep the intermediates \(S\) and \(P\) in that memory instead of writing them to HBM.

Warm-up Define arithmetic intensity and the ridge point. What is the H100's ridge in bf16, and where do matmul, softmax, LayerNorm and GELU fall?

Arithmetic intensity is FLOPs per byte moved between HBM and the chip. The roofline says attainable FLOP/s is \(\min(P, \beta I)\), and the ridge is \(P/\beta\): for the H100 SXM, \(989.4\text{ TFLOP/s} / 3.35\text{ TB/s} \approx 295\) FLOPs/byte (dense bf16; not the 1,979 with-sparsity headline). Below the ridge an op is memory-bound and its time is bytes over bandwidth; above it, compute-bound. A large matmul (batch of about 345 rows or more against a 4096 x 4096 weight) is right of the ridge. Softmax, LayerNorm, GELU and residual adds have intensity of roughly 0.2 to 3, two orders of magnitude left of it, so their time is set by bytes alone, and a batch-1 decode matmul has intensity 1.

Warm-up Why does fusing a chain of elementwise ops speed it up when the FLOPs do not change? Give a byte count.

Each unfused op is a kernel that reads its input from HBM and writes its output back, and these ops are memory-bound, so time is bytes over bandwidth. A fused kernel reads the input once, computes the whole chain in registers, and writes once. Example: an eager RMSNorm on bf16 (upcast, square, mean, rsqrt-multiply, downcast, weight multiply) moves about 36 bytes per element, while a fused kernel moves 4 (one bf16 read, one bf16 write): 9 times fewer bytes, so about 9 times faster if bandwidth-bound. Unfused softmax reads \(5MN + 2M\) and writes \(3MN + 2M\) elements versus \(MN\) and \(MN\) fused, about 4 times. Fusion does nothing for a compute-bound matmul; it only removes traffic.

Core What is the roofline model? Is a batch-1 decode matmul compute- or memory-bound? Classify \((4096 \times 4096)\) times \((4096 \times B)\) for \(B = 1, 16, 256, 1024\).

Roofline: attainable FLOP/s \(= \min(P, \beta \cdot I)\) with \(I\) the FLOPs per byte of HBM traffic; the ridge \(P/\beta = 295\) on the H100. For a bf16 matmul with a square \(d \times d\) weight and \(B\) rows, FLOPs \(= 2d^2 B\) and bytes \(= 2(d^2 + 2dB)\), so \(I = dB/(d + 2B)\). With \(d = 4096\): \(B = 1\): \(I = 1.0\) (0.34% of peak); \(B = 16\): 15.9 (5%); \(B = 256\): 227.6 (77% of peak, still memory-bound); \(B = 1024\): 682.7 (compute-bound).

Batch-1 decode is a matrix-vector product: 33.6 MB of weights stream in 10 µs while the math needs 0.034 µs, so it is memory-bound by a factor of about 300, and its speed is bandwidth over bytes read. The crossover is \(B^{*} = I_{\text{ridge}}\, d/(d - 2I_{\text{ridge}}) \approx 345\). Batching helps because each weight read serves \(B\) tokens (\(I \approx B\)); it does not help attention against the KV cache, where every sequence has its own cache (page 07).

Core Why does FlashAttention speed up attention without changing the FLOPs? Compute the HBM traffic of standard and flash attention for \(T = 8192\), \(d_h = 128\), 32 heads.

Runtime of attention is set by HBM traffic, not FLOPs. Standard attention writes \(S = QK^\top\) and \(P = \softmax(S)\) (each \(T \times T\)) to HBM and reads each back: about \(4T^2 + 4Td_h\) elements per head. Its intensity is \(\approx d_h/2 = 64\), memory-bound at any \(T\). FlashAttention tiles the computation so that each \(S\) tile is computed in SRAM, folded into running \((m, \ell, O)\) statistics with the online softmax, and discarded; traffic is \(\Theta(T^2 d^2/M)\) instead of \(\Theta(T^2)\), extra memory is \(O(T)\), and FLOPs stay \(O(T^2 d)\) (slightly more in the backward pass, from recomputation).

Numbers (bf16, one layer, one sequence, forward): standard \(= (4 \cdot 8192^2 + 4 \cdot 8192 \cdot 128) \cdot 2\) B \(= 545\) MB per head, 17.4 GB for 32 heads, 5.2 ms at 3.35 TB/s. FlashAttention's compulsory traffic (\(Q, K, V\) read once, \(O\) written once) is \(4 \cdot 8192 \cdot 128 \cdot 2\) B = 8.4 MB per head, 268 MB in total (65 times less). If no \(K, V\) re-read hit the L2 and \(B_r = 128\), it would be 8.7 GB (2 times less than standard); real traffic is between the two, close to the lower bound when the cache serves the re-reads. The attention FLOPs are 1.10 TFLOP either way (1.11 ms at peak), so after FlashAttention the kernel is compute-bound (\(I = T/2 = 4096\) at the lower bound) and standard attention is 5 times slower than its own FLOP time.

Core Derive the online softmax update used in FlashAttention.

For one query row, keep over the keys seen so far \(m = \max_i s_i\), \(\ell = \sum_i e^{s_i - m}\) and \(a = \sum_i e^{s_i - m} v_i\); the output so far is \(a/\ell\). A new tile with scores \(s_i\) arrives. Let \(m' = \max(m, \max_{\text{tile}} s_i)\) and \(\alpha = e^{m - m'}\). Then \(\ell' = \alpha \ell + \sum_{\text{tile}} e^{s_i - m'}\) and \(a' = \alpha a + \sum_{\text{tile}} e^{s_i - m'} v_i\).

Why: for an old key, \(e^{s_i - m'} = e^{s_i - m} e^{m - m'}\), so moving the reference from \(m\) to \(m'\) multiplies the old \(\ell\) and \(a\) by the same \(\alpha\); new keys use \(m'\) directly. By induction the invariants hold for all tiles, and at the end \(O = a/\ell\) is exactly \(\softmax(s)^\top V\) with \(L = m + \log \ell\). Since \(\alpha \le 1\), nothing overflows. The merge of two partial triples is \((M, e^{m_1 - M}\ell_1 + e^{m_2 - M}\ell_2, e^{m_1 - M}a_1 + e^{m_2 - M}a_2)\), associative and commutative, so tiles can be combined in any order. Worked check: scores \([0,2,5,3]\), values \([10,20,30,40]\) in two tiles give \(m = 5\), \(\ell = 1.1919\), \(a = 36.477\), output 30.605, equal to the direct softmax. Mention the edge case: if a whole tile is masked, \(m'\) can stay \(-\infty\) and \(e^{-\infty - (-\infty)}\) is NaN, so kernels guard it.

Core What does FlashAttention store for the backward pass, what does it recompute, and what does that cost?

It stores \(O\) and the per-row logsumexp \(L = m + \log \ell\), that is \(O(T)\) extra numbers, and no \(T \times T\) matrix. The backward pass recomputes each tile \(S_{ij} = Q_i K_j^\top/\sqrt d\) and \(P_{ij} = e^{S_{ij} - L_i}\) on chip (no rescaling needed, since \(L\) contains the whole row's normalizer), then \(dV_j \mathrel{+}= P_{ij}^\top dO_i\), \(dP_{ij} = dO_i V_j^\top\), \(dS_{ij} = P_{ij} \circ (dP_{ij} - D_i)\), \(dQ_i \mathrel{+}= dS_{ij} K_j/\sqrt d\), \(dK_j \mathrel{+}= dS_{ij}^\top Q_i/\sqrt d\). The row term \(D_i = \sum_j P_{ij}\,dP_{ij}\) would need a pass over the row, but equals \(dO_i \cdot O_i\) because \(\sum_j P_{ij}\,(dO_i \cdot v_j) = dO_i \cdot O_i\).

Cost: 5 matmuls in backward (the extra one is recomputing \(QK^\top\)) versus 4 with stored \(P\); with the forward's 2, that is 7 instead of 6 matmuls, about 17% more FLOPs. It pays because HBM traffic falls: in the paper's GPT-2-medium measurement FLOPs rose about 13% while traffic fell from 40.3 GB to 4.4 GB and runtime from 41.7 ms to 7.3 ms. It is selective gradient checkpointing for the attention block.

Core What changed in FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3? Where does FlashAttention-4 fit?

FA-2 (A100): FA-1 reached only 25 to 40% of peak. (1) Fewer non-matmul FLOPs (each is 16 times a matmul FLOP): keep an unnormalized output and divide once at the end, store only the logsumexp. (2) Parallelize over the query tiles as well as batch and heads (swap the loops), so long sequences with small batch still fill the SMs; this also keeps the accumulator in registers instead of round-tripping through HBM. (3) Split \(Q\) across warps instead of \(K, V\), which removes the shared-memory reduction between warps. Result: about 2 times FA-1, up to 230 TFLOP/s (73%) on an A100, 72% MFU end to end.

FA-3 (H100): FA-2 gets 35% of the H100. Exploit Hopper's asynchrony: a producer warpgroup loads tiles with TMA while consumer warpgroups run asynchronous wgmma (warp specialization); overlap softmax with matmuls (pingpong between two warpgroups, plus intra-warpgroup pipelining), because \(\exp\) runs 256 times slower than the tensor cores and would otherwise take about half of the matmul time; FP8 with block quantization and incoherent processing (2.6 times lower error). Result: 1.5 to 2 times FA-2, up to 740 TFLOP/s (75%), about 1.2 PFLOP/s in FP8.

FA-4 (B200): tensor throughput doubled but the exp unit and shared-memory bandwidth did not, so they become the bottleneck: emulate part of the exponentials with polynomials on the FMA units, skip most softmax rescaling (only rescale when the max grows by more than a factor of 256), use tensor memory and 2-CTA MMA to cut shared-memory traffic and \(dQ\) atomics. Result: up to 1,613 TFLOP/s (71%), 1.3 times cuDNN 9.13 at release, with vendor libraries closing the gap since.

Core Why does decode need a different attention kernel (FlashDecoding)?

The prefill kernel parallelizes over batch, heads and query tiles. In decode each sequence has one query token, so at small batch there are only \(B \times n_h\) blocks (32 for Llama-3-8B at batch 1, or 8 if one block serves a KV group) on 132 SMs, and the kernel that must stream a long KV cache uses a small part of the chip. HBM bandwidth needs about bandwidth times latency in flight (roughly 0.9 MB chip-wide by my estimate), which a few SMs cannot generate.

FlashDecoding splits the keys into \(n_s\) chunks, one block per (batch, head, chunk), each running the FlashAttention inner loop and writing a partial output \(O_s\) and its logsumexp \(L_s\); a small kernel then combines \(L = \log \sum_s e^{L_s}\), \(O = \sum_s e^{L_s - L} O_s\). This is exact because the online-softmax merge is associative. The extra traffic is negligible (16 splits at 32K context write 0.2% of the KV bytes for a GQA group of 4 query heads). Choose \(n_s\) so the grid fills the SMs for a few waves. The blog reports up to 8 times faster end-to-end generation at very long context. Paged and MLA kernels (FlashMLA's split-KV plus combine) follow the same pattern.

Core How would you speed up a slow LayerNorm, then matmul, then GELU sequence?

Start with a profile and a roofline bound, not with a rewrite. Take 8192 tokens, \(d = 4096 \to d_{ff} = 14336\) in bf16. The matmul is 962 GFLOP, 0.97 ms at peak, with intensity above 2,000: compute-bound, leave it to cuBLAS and check its tensor-pipe utilization. GELU is memory-bound: unfused it reads and writes the 235 MB output, 470 MB, 0.14 ms, an extra 14%. LayerNorm reads and writes 67 MB each way, 0.04 ms, 4%.

Fixes in order of payoff: (1) fuse GELU into the matmul epilogue, so the tile is activated in registers before it is written (cuBLASLt or CUTLASS epilogues, or TorchInductor's Triton template under mode="max-autotune"); (2) make sure LayerNorm is one fused kernel (not six eager ops); (3) if it still matters, fold the normalization into the matmul prologue: \(\mathrm{LN}(x)W = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}(\gamma \odot W) + \beta W\), with \(\gamma \odot W\) precomputed offline and \(\mu, \sigma\) from one cheap pre-pass. Trade-offs: fused kernels are specialized to shapes and dtypes, and hand-written ones cost maintenance; the gain is bounded by the memory-bound share (about 18% here), so verify with a profile before and after.

Deep-dive The softmax is a tiny fraction of attention's FLOPs. Why did it become the bottleneck that shaped FlashAttention-3 and -4?

FLOPs are the wrong unit; time on the unit that executes them is what counts. Per score element, attention does \(4d\) matmul FLOPs (512 at \(d = 128\)) but only one \(\exp\), which runs on the special-function unit at 16 ops per clock per SM. The ratio of exp time to matmul time is \(P_{\text{clk}}/(64d)\) with \(P_{\text{clk}}\) the tensor FLOPs per clock per SM: 0.5 on Hopper at \(d = 128\) (4,096 per clock: 3.9 TFLOP/s of exp against 989.4 of matmul, so exp takes half as long as the matmuls), and 1.0 on Blackwell (8,192 per clock, exp unchanged), where it equals the matmul time. Executed serially, the tensor cores would idle a third of the time on Hopper and half on Blackwell.

FA-3 overlaps the two (pingpong across warpgroups and softmax/GEMM pipelining within one, gaining from 570 to 661 TFLOP/s in the ablation). FA-4, where the overlap alone cannot help because exp is as long as the matmuls, reduces exp work itself: a polynomial 2x on the FMA units for 10 to 25% of entries (accurate enough after BF16 rounding), and conditional rescaling that skips the output correction unless the max grows by more than 256. Lesson to state: when one unit scales faster than the rest, the fix is to restructure the algorithm around the slowest unit.

Deep-dive Design question: you must write a causal FlashAttention forward kernel. How do you choose tile sizes, handle the mask, and what can go wrong numerically?

Tiles. One thread block owns a \(Q\) tile of \(B_r\) rows (64 or 128), streams \(K, V\) tiles of \(B_c\) rows, and keeps \(Q_i\), \(K_j\), \(V_j\) in shared memory (a 128 x 128 bf16 tile is 32 KiB) and \(m, \ell, O_i\) in registers. Larger tiles cut shared-memory traffic and the number of passes but raise register and shared-memory use; spills or a kernel that does not fit are the limit. Tile height is also the intensity in the worst-case (no L2) traffic model, so \(B_r = 128\) is about 128 FLOPs/byte. The grid is \(\lceil T/B_r \rceil \times B \times n_h\) blocks: check it against the 132 SMs for wave quantization and schedule long causal rows first (longest-processing-time first), because row \(i\) does \(i\) tiles of work.

Mask. Skip \(K\) tiles entirely above the diagonal (about half of them, a 1.7 to 1.8 times gain) and apply the elementwise mask only to tiles that straddle it. With a KV cache the mask is end-aligned (query \(i\) sits at position \(T_k - T_q + i\)).

Numerics. Keep \(m, \ell\) and the accumulator in fp32 and cast \(P\) to bf16 only for the \(PV\) matmul; scale \(Q\) by \(1/\sqrt d\) up front; store \(L = m + \log\ell\) in fp32. Guard \(m = -\infty\) (fully masked tile for a row: \(-\infty - (-\infty) = \) NaN), which start-aligned causal masks avoid but sliding windows and end-aligned masks can hit. For GQA, index KV head \(\lfloor i/g \rfloor\) and sum \(dK, dV\) over the group in backward. For MLA-style head dims (192 for \(QK\), 128 for \(V\)) the tile shapes must be specialized.

Deep-dive A decode attention kernel reaches only 40% of HBM bandwidth on an H100 at batch 8 and 32K context on Llama-3-8B (GQA with 8 KV heads). How do you diagnose and fix it?

First the bound. The KV cache is 128 KiB per token, so 4 GiB per 32K sequence and 32 GiB for 8 sequences: reading it takes 34.4 GB / 3.35 TB/s = 10.3 ms per step, plus 4.8 ms for the 16 GB of weights. Attention is the majority of the step and its intensity is only \(g = 4\), so the kernel should sit at 80 to 90% of HBM bandwidth. 40% means it is not bandwidth-limited but concurrency-limited or overhead-limited.

Diagnose with the profiler. Grid size: if one block handles a (sequence, KV head) pair there are \(8 \times 8 = 64\) blocks on 132 SMs, which is 48% of the chip, matching the symptom. Little's law says about 0.9 MB must be in flight, which 64 blocks cannot keep. Fix: split over keys (FlashDecoding), say \(n_s = 4\) to 8 chunks of 4K to 8K tokens giving 256 to 512 blocks, and check the tiny combine kernel is overlapped or fused. Then check secondary causes: paged-cache indirection (measured 20 to 26% kernel overhead in the vLLM paper, which already uses one warp per block; the block size trades kernel efficiency against fragmentation), an L2-friendly ordering, GQA grouping so each KV tile serves all 4 query heads, launch overhead between tiny kernels (CUDA graphs), and finally reducing the bytes: an FP8 KV cache halves the traffic (page 17).

Deep-dive Is MLA decode attention with 128 heads memory- or compute-bound on a Hopper GPU, and how does that shape the kernel?

Absorbed MLA is MQA over a 576-dimensional shared key and 512-dimensional shared value. Per cached token, all heads do \(2 h_q (576 + 512)\) FLOPs while the token is read once, \(576 \times 2\) bytes, so \(I = 1.89\,h_q\) per query token: 242 for \(h_q = 128\). The ridge is 295 at the 989.4 TFLOP/s datasheet peak and about 258 at DeepSeek's measured throttled peak of 865 TFLOP/s. So at \(s_q = 1\) it sits just below the ridge (94% of the throttled ridge, 82% of the datasheet ridge). DeepSeek, using the rounded intensity \(2 h_q s_q = 256\), calls its decode (no tensor parallelism, so \(h_q = 128\)) compute-bound; with multi-token prediction (\(s_q > 1\)) it is clearly above the ridge. With tensor parallelism splitting the heads it would move left and be memory-bound.

Consequences for the kernel: the goal is tensor-core utilization, not bandwidth. The output accumulator (\(64 \times 512\) fp32) fills half the register file, so FlashAttention-3's two-accumulator pingpong does not fit, and FlashMLA uses "seesaw" scheduling that splits the accumulator across two warpgroups; it reaches up to 660 TFLOP/s (about 76% of the throttled peak) in the compute-bound regime and up to 3,000 GB/s in the memory-bound regime. This is also why head count is a cost knob (page 07): more heads add FLOPs per cached byte, which is free below the ridge and costly above it.

Cheat sheet

  • H100 SXM: 132 SMs; 989.4 dense bf16 TFLOP/s (1,979 with sparsity); HBM3 80 GB at 3.35 TB/s; 50 MB L2; 256 KB registers and 256 KB L1/shared per SM (228 KB shared max); ridge \(\approx 295\) FLOPs/byte. B200: 2,250 TFLOP/s, 8 TB/s, ridge \(\approx 281\).
  • Roofline: attainable \(= \min(P, \beta I)\). Matmul \(I = Bkn/(Bk + kn + Bn)\); square: \(dB/(d+2B) \to d/2\). \(d = 4096\): \(B = 1, 16, 256, 1024\) gives \(I = 1, 15.9, 228, 683\); crossover \(B^{*} \approx 345\). A square bf16 matmul reaches the ridge only if \(d > 590\).
  • Elementwise ops, softmax, norms: \(I \approx 0.2\) to 3, so time \(=\) bytes / bandwidth. Eager RMSNorm 36 B/element versus 4 B fused. Unfused softmax \(8MN + 4M\) versus \(2MN\) elements.
  • Non-matmul FLOPs are expensive: A100 16 times, H100 FP32 15 times and \(\exp\) 256 times, B200 \(\exp\) about 510 times. Exp time / matmul time \(= P_{\text{clk}}/(64d)\): 0.5 on H100, 1.0 on B200 at \(d = 128\).
  • Online softmax: \(m' = \max(m, \max s)\), \(\alpha = e^{m-m'}\), \(\ell' = \alpha\ell + \sum e^{s-m'}\), \(a' = \alpha a + \sum e^{s-m'}v\); \(O = a/\ell\), \(L = m + \log\ell\). The merge is associative.
  • FlashAttention: exact; FLOPs \(O(T^2d)\) (backward 5 matmuls versus 4: about 17% more in total); memory \(O(T)\); HBM accesses \(\Theta(T^2d^2/M)\) versus \(\Theta(T^2)\). Naive intensity \(\approx d_h/2\); flash \(\le T/2\).
  • \(T = 8192\), \(d_h = 128\), 32 heads, bf16: standard 17.4 GB (5.2 ms); flash lower bound 268 MB (0.08 ms); no-cache worst case 8.7 GB; FLOPs 1.10 TFLOP (1.11 ms). \(S\) alone is 4 GiB.
  • Backward: save \(O, L\); recompute \(P = e^{S-L}\); \(D_i = dO_i \cdot O_i\); \(dS = P \circ (dP - D)\).
  • FA-2: unnormalized \(\tilde O\), parallel over query tiles, split-Q; 230 TFLOP/s (73%) on A100. FA-3: TMA warp specialization, pingpong, FP8 + incoherent processing; 740 TFLOP/s (75%) on H100, 1.2 PFLOP/s FP8. FA-4: emulated exp, rescale only if max grows by more than 256, TMEM, 2-CTA; 1,613 TFLOP/s (71%) on B200.
  • FlashDecoding: split keys into \(n_s\) chunks, save \(L_s\), combine \(O = \sum e^{L_s-L}O_s\). Paged kernels: block table, 20 to 26% kernel overhead. FlashMLA: \(I \approx 1.89h_q\), at the compute-bound knee at 128 heads (242 against a ridge of 258 to 295), 660 TFLOP/s on H800.
  • Profile in order: roofline bound, end-to-end (MFU), timeline gaps, per-kernel bandwidth and tensor-pipe utilization, then fix the largest term. nvidia-smi utilization only says a kernel was running.
