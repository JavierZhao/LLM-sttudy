Evaluation

P1 ~45 min Prereqs: 01 Big picture, 06 Decoding & KV cache

A benchmark number is a sample mean over a few hundred to a few thousand questions, produced by one prompt, one decoding rule and one scorer, on a test set that may have leaked into training. This page covers how language models are scored during pretraining and after post-training, the statistics that say whether a difference is real, and the ways evaluations fail.

What interviewers probe
  • Judgment on a claimed gain. "This change improves MMLU by 0.8 points: is it real?" A strong answer names a paired test, seed noise, multiple comparisons and contamination instead of saying yes or no.
  • Exact metrics. The unbiased pass@k estimator with a derivation, why the plug-in \(1-(1-\hat p)^k\) is biased, and the standard error of an accuracy with the arithmetic.
  • Scoring mechanics. Cloze versus multiple-choice formulation, length normalization, generation with answer extraction, and when likelihood and generative scores disagree.
  • Pretraining evals. Which benchmarks carry signal at 1B to 7B parameters, which are noise, and how you would know.
  • Failure modes. LLM-judge biases, contamination detection and its limits, and what to report for a reasoning model.

After this page you can

  • Give a measured example for each of five reasons benchmark scores mislead: open-ended outputs, prompt sensitivity, contamination, saturation and sampling noise.
  • Place about twenty benchmarks by what they measure and name each one's known defect.
  • Derive the unbiased pass@k estimator and its stable product form, show that the plug-in estimate is biased low, and compute pass@1, 5 and 10 for \(n=20\), \(c=3\).
  • Compute the standard error, confidence interval and minimum detectable difference for an accuracy on \(N\) questions, paired and unpaired, and run a paired bootstrap.
  • Choose a scoring method (cloze, multiple choice, generative) and a normalization for a checkpoint suite at a given model scale.
  • Design a contamination audit and state what each detector can and cannot prove.
  • State what to report for a reasoning-model or agent evaluation: samples, temperature, token budget and harness.

Why evaluation is hard

Evaluation 评测 looks like the easy part of building a model: run it on a benchmark 基准 and report a number. In practice the number estimates an expectation over choices that the headline figure hides:

\[ \text{score} \;=\; \E_{q \sim \mathcal{Q}}\ \E_{y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid q;\ \text{prompt},\ \text{decoding})}\big[\, s(q, y) \,\big] \]

The benchmark is a finite sample of questions \(q\) from a distribution \(\mathcal{Q}\). The prompt (template, shots, chat format), the decoding rule (greedy, temperature, token budget) and the scorer \(s\) (exact match, unit tests, likelihood rank, a judge) each move the number, and the finite sample adds sampling error. Five failure sources follow. Learn one measured number for each.

  1. Open-ended outputs. There is no single reference string, so you need a verifier, a fixed candidate set or a judge. String overlap is a poor stand-in: Chen et al. found that BLEU improvements "may not indicate improved rates of functional correctness in practice" [Chen et al. 2021, Fig. 8].
  2. Prompt and format sensitivity. Formatting choices alone moved LLaMA-2-13B accuracy by up to 76 points, and the effect persisted with larger models, more shots and instruction tuning [Sclar et al. 2023]. Across 24 prompt styles, MMLU scores typically varied by 4 to 5 points (up to 10.98), against about 2 (up to 3.74) on MMLU-Pro [MMLU-Pro, §6.3]. "ARC-Challenge" for Llama-3-8B appears as 60.2 (cloze, Hugging Face leaderboard) and 78.6 (multiple choice, Meta's model card): 18 points from formulation alone [OLMES, Tab. 1].
  3. Contamination 数据污染. Test items, or close variants, in the training data turn a capability measurement into a memory measurement. On GSM1k, 1,205 freshly written problems matched to GSM8K, accuracy dropped by up to 8 points for some model families [Zhang et al. 2024]. A Llama-2-13B fine-tuned on rephrased MMLU test questions went from 54.8 to 85.9 while 10-gram decontamination flagged nothing [Yang et al. 2023, Tab. 2]. The data-side view is on page 12.
  4. Saturation 饱和. GPT-4 scored 86.4% on MMLU in March 2023, and frontier models released in early to mid 2024 all sat at 86 to 87% [MMLU-Pro, §1]. The ceiling is below 100%: an audit estimates that 6.49% of MMLU questions contain errors, and 57% of the Virology subset [Gema et al. 2024]. At the top, score differences measure label noise as much as skill.
  5. Sampling noise. A 500-question benchmark gives a 95% interval of about \(\pm 4\) points around 70% (worked below). AIME has 30 problems, so one problem moves pass@1 by 3.3 points [Hochlehnert et al. 2025]. Add training-seed noise and hardware noise on top.
Key idea

An evaluation is a measurement: it has an estimand (which distribution, prompt, decoding rule and scorer), a sampling design and an error bar. Most weak interview answers fail because they treat the reported score as a fact about the model instead of an estimate with a protocol. Ask which of the four things was held fixed, and how large the error bar is.

From your jet tagger

Your tagger was scored on simulated jets with exact truth labels, hundreds of thousands to millions of them, held out by construction, and you would not quote an AUC without a spread over training seeds. LLM benchmarks invert those comforts: hundreds to thousands of items, labels that are sometimes wrong, a test set that may have been in the training data, and a metric that depends on the prompt. Your instincts still transfer: error bars, a blinded held-out set (a private test set is the LLM version of a blinded analysis), and treating prompt and decoding choices as systematic uncertainties.

Scoring methods

A scorer is one of four things: likelihood over a fixed candidate set, generation followed by answer extraction, a sampling metric such as pass@k or majority vote, or a judge (a model or humans). Each answers a slightly different question, and choosing between them is a real design decision.

Log-likelihood multiple choice: cloze versus multiple-choice formulation

There are two ways to pose a multiple-choice question to a base model [OLMES, §2.1]. In the multiple-choice formulation (MCF) the prompt lists lettered options and the model's next-token distribution over "A", "B", "C", "D" is the answer, exactly as a human sits the exam. In the cloze formulation (CF) 完形填空 the prompt is just the question, each candidate answer is appended as a continuation, and the candidate the model finds most likely wins. MCF needs one forward pass and requires the model to have learned to bind letters to options. CF needs one pass per candidate and needs no such skill.

CF scores need normalization 长度归一化. With \(a_i\) the candidate and \(q\) the question, the raw score is a sum of per-token log-probabilities, so longer candidates are penalized just for having more factors below 1. OLMES lists four options [OLMES, §3.3]:

\[ \underbrace{\log P(a_i \mid q)}_{\text{none}}, \qquad \underbrace{\frac{\log P(a_i \mid q)}{\#\text{tokens}(a_i)}}_{\text{token}}, \qquad \underbrace{\frac{\log P(a_i \mid q)}{\#\text{chars}(a_i)}}_{\text{character (}\texttt{acc\_norm}\text{)}}, \qquad \underbrace{\log \frac{P(a_i \mid q)}{P(a_i \mid \text{"Answer:"})}}_{\text{PMI}} \]
Cloze scoring rules. The PMI form divides out the answer string's prior plausibility.
@torch.no_grad()
def continuation_logprob(model, tok, context, continuation):
    ctx  = tok(context, return_tensors="pt").input_ids                  # (1, Tc)
    full = tok(context + continuation, return_tensors="pt").input_ids   # (1, Tc + Tk)
    logits = model(full).logits[:, :-1]                                 # (1, T-1, V): position t predicts token t+1
    logp = logits.log_softmax(-1).gather(-1, full[:, 1:, None])[..., 0] # (1, T-1) log-prob of each realized token
    return logp[0, ctx.shape[1] - 1:].sum().item()                      # keep only the continuation tokens

Tokenizing context + continuation jointly can merge tokens across the boundary, which is why OLMES puts a leading space on each candidate and specifies the exact prompt format.

Which formulation, when. MCF is at chance for weak models and better for strong ones. OLMo-7B-0424 on MMLU is at chance in MCF until roughly 400B training tokens, while CF gives signal from the start [OLMES, Fig. 1]. In a 7B run of 210B tokens, standard MMLU sat at chance with seed standard deviation 0.57 points, while MMLU-cloze scored 37.5 with standard deviation 0.22 [Madaan et al. 2024, Tab. 1]. For strong models the order flips: Llama-3-70B scores 78.7 on standard MMLU and 60.6 on cloze, and ARC-Challenge 93.7 (MCF) against 69.0 (CF) [Madaan, §3.3], [OLMES, Tab. 6]. The two formulations correlate strongly across 70 fully trained models (Pearson 0.92), so CF is a good early proxy for final MCF quality [Madaan, §3.3].

Cloze first, multiple choice later
Problem
Small or early-checkpoint base models score at chance on lettered multiple choice, so a standard MMLU curve is flat and noisy exactly when you need signal.
Idea
Score each candidate answer by likelihood (cloze) with a task-appropriate normalization, and switch to MCF, or report both, once the model has learned symbol binding.
Why it works
Cloze measures how well the language model already predicts the right text, which improves smoothly from the start. MCF also requires a format skill that appears abruptly, around 400B to 800B tokens in the runs cited.
Evidence
MMLU-cloze monotonicity 0.95 against 0.09 for standard MMLU at 7B, 210B tokens [Madaan, Tab. 1]; OLMES reports the better of the two per model.
Cost
Cloze underrates strong models (ARC-Challenge 69.0 against 93.7 for Llama-3-70B) and cannot handle "none of the above". It measures surface-form likelihood, not the ability to compare options.
Used by
OLMES (developed with the OLMo models), DataDecide and the FineWeb ablations (see page 12).

Generation with answer extraction

Chain-of-thought and open-ended tasks need generation: sample a solution, extract the final answer, compare it to the reference by exact match, symbolic equivalence, unit tests or a judge. Every step can fail for reasons unrelated to capability. MMLU-Pro extracts with two regular expressions and falls back to a random option; in GPT-4o's error analysis 5% of errors were "no selection made" and 1% were extraction errors [MMLU-Pro, §4, §5.3]. MATH fixes a canonical answer format and equivalence rules so exact match works [Hendrycks et al. 2021]. IFEval reports a strict score and a loose one that also tries eight transformations of the response; the loose score removes false negatives at the cost of false positives [IFEval]. HLE grades free-form answers with a judge model against the reference [HLE].

Generation also changes the conclusion. On BBH, answer-only prompting understates capability by 13 to 17 points for three model families (PaLM-540B 52.3 to 65.2; code-davinci-002 56.6 to 73.9) [BBH, Tab. 2]. Likelihood scoring measures direct-answer knowledge, generation measures the ability to compute an answer, and a benchmark can rank models differently under the two.

Sampling metrics: pass@k and majority vote

For code and math with a checker, the natural question is "does at least one of \(k\) attempts succeed?" Drawing exactly \(k\) samples per problem gives a noisy estimate of that. Chen et al. draw \(n \ge k\) samples, count the \(c\) that pass, and use the unbiased estimator 无偏估计 [Chen et al. 2021, §2.1]:

\[ \widehat{\text{pass@}k} \;=\; 1 - \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}} \;=\; 1 - \prod_{i=n-c+1}^{n} \Big(1 - \frac{k}{i}\Big), \qquad \text{pass@}k = \E_{\text{problems}}\Big[\,\widehat{\text{pass@}k}\,\Big] \]
Estimate per problem, then average over problems. If \(n-c<k\) the estimate is 1.

Derivation. Suppose one problem has success probability \(p\) per sample, so \(\text{pass@}k = 1-(1-p)^k\). The tempting plug-in is \(1-(1-\hat p)^k\) with \(\hat p = c/n\). It is biased: \((1-x)^k\) is convex in \(x\), so by Jensen \(\E[(1-\hat p)^k] \ge (1-p)^k\) and the plug-in underestimates, and the gap does not vanish even for \(n > 5k\) [Chen, App. A]. Instead estimate the failure probability \((1-p)^k\) directly. Among the \(n\) samples, the fraction of size-\(k\) subsets that contain no correct sample is \(\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}\). Each fixed subset is all-wrong with probability exactly \((1-p)^k\), because its members are independent, so the expected fraction over \(c \sim \text{Binomial}(n,p)\) is \((1-p)^k\). That is unbiasedness. For the stable form, write the ratio of factorials and cancel:

\[ \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}} = \frac{(n-c)!\,(n-k)!}{(n-c-k)!\,n!} = \prod_{i=n-c+1}^{n} \frac{i-k}{i} = \prod_{i=n-c+1}^{n}\Big(1-\frac{k}{i}\Big). \]

Every factor lies in \((0,1]\), so nothing overflows, whereas \(\binom{10^6}{100}\) does. Two consequences follow. The estimator needs \(n \ge k\), and its variance grows as \(k\) approaches \(n\) (Chen et al. use \(n=200\) for \(k \le 100\)). And pass@\(k\) depends on temperature: the best temperature for a 679M model was 0.2 for pass@1 and 0.8 for pass@100 [Chen, §3.3], so always report it (page 06).

import numpy as np

def pass_at_k(n: int, c: int, k: int) -> float:
    """Unbiased pass@k for one problem: n samples, c correct (Chen et al. 2021, Fig. 3)."""
    if n - c < k:                      # every size-k subset must contain a correct sample
        return 1.0
    i = np.arange(n - c + 1, n + 1)    # C(n-c, k) / C(n, k) = prod_{i=n-c+1}^{n} (1 - k / i)
    return float(1.0 - np.prod(1.0 - k / i))

def benchmark_pass_at_k(correct_counts, n, k):
    return float(np.mean([pass_at_k(n, c, k) for c in correct_counts]))  # average AFTER the estimator

The unbiased pass@k estimator
Problem
Drawing exactly \(k\) samples per problem gives a high-variance estimate of pass@\(k\), and estimating it from mean pass@1 as \(1-(1-\bar p)^k\) is biased and wrong for a mixture of easy and hard problems.
Idea
Draw \(n \ge k\) samples once, count \(c\) successes per problem, and average \(1-\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}\) over problems.
Why it works
The ratio is the probability that a random size-\(k\) subset of your own samples is all wrong, which has expectation \((1-p)^k\). One set of samples serves every \(k \le n\).
Evidence
Unbiasedness proof and bias of the plug-in [Chen, App. A, Fig. 13]. DeepSeek-V4's Codeforces rating uses the same idea: 32 candidates per problem, 10 sampled without replacement, and the expectation of the resulting rating over all selections [V4, §5.3.1].
Cost
Needs \(n\) samples per problem (200 in Chen et al.), and assumes an oracle checker, so pass@\(k\) is an upper bound on what a deployed system that must choose one answer achieves.
Used by
HumanEval, MBPP, LiveCodeBench-style code evals, and the pass@1-averaged-over-samples convention for reasoning models (below).

Two relatives. Reliability asks that all \(k\) attempts succeed: \(\text{pass}^k = \E[\binom{c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}]\), which \(\tau\)-bench introduced because a customer-service agent that solves a task only sometimes is not deployable [Yao et al. 2024]. GPT-4o's success on \(\tau\)-retail was 61.2% for one trial and below 25% for \(\text{pass}^8\) [Yao et al.]. Majority vote (self-consistency 自洽性, cons@\(k\)) needs no checker: sample \(k\) reasoning paths and return the most common final answer. PaLM-540B on GSM8K goes from 56.5% (greedy) to 74.4% with 40 paths [Wang et al. 2023]. DeepSeek-R1 on AIME 2024 reports 79.8% pass@1, 86.7% cons@64 and 90.0% pass@64, three different quantities [R1]. Pass@\(k\) uses an oracle; cons@\(k\) does not.

LLM-as-judge and human preference

For open-ended chat there is no checker, so a strong model judges: pairwise (which answer is better), single-answer grading, or reference-guided grading [Zheng et al. 2023, §3.1]. It scales, and GPT-4 agreed with expert humans 85% of the time on non-tied MT-Bench votes against 81% human-human agreement [§4.2]. But judges have measurable biases:

  • Position bias. Swapping the order of two near-identical answers, GPT-4 was consistent in only 65.0% of cases and Claude-v1 in 23.8% [Zheng, Tab. 2].
  • Verbosity bias. Padding an answer with a rephrased duplicate list fooled Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 91.3% of the time and GPT-4 8.7% [Tab. 3].
  • Self-preference. GPT-4 favored itself with a 10-point higher win rate and Claude-v1 with 25, though the authors could not separate bias from real quality [§3.3]. Models can recognize their own outputs, and after fine-tuning self-recognition correlates linearly with self-preference [Panickssery et al. 2024].
  • Weak grading of reasoning. On 10 math questions the default judge accepted a wrong answer in 14 of 20 trials, falling to 3 of 20 when given a reference solution [Tab. 4].

Mitigations: judge both orders and count a win only if it holds in both (else a tie); few-shot judge examples raised GPT-4's swap consistency from 65.0% to 77.5% [App. D.2]; give a reference answer for verifiable questions; use a judge from a different family than the models judged; and control length statistically.

Length-controlled win rate
Problem
Judges prefer longer answers, so a model can raise its win rate by being wordy. Prompting GPT-4-turbo to be concise or verbose moved its AlpacaEval win rate from 22.9% to 64.3%.
Idea
Fit a logistic regression of the judge's preference on model identity, instruction difficulty and a normalized length difference \(\tanh\big(\Delta\text{len}/\text{std}\big)\), then predict the win rate with the length term set to zero.
Why it works
It answers a counterfactual: what would the preference be if both answers had the same length? Setting the mediator to zero removes its direct effect while keeping win-rate properties (the baseline scores 50%, and swapping models gives \(100\%\) minus the score).
Evidence
Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena rose from 0.94 to 0.98, and the spread across verbosity prompts fell from 22.9 to 64.3 (raw) to 41.9 to 51.6 [Dubois et al. 2024, §4.1, §4.2].
Cost
Corrects only the features you model, needs weak regularization against truncation attacks, and inherits every other judge bias. Arena's style control does the same with length and markdown counts in its Bradley-Terry regression [LMSYS 2024].
Used by
AlpacaEval 2 (LC is the default), Arena's style-controlled ranking.

Human preference arenas replace the judge with crowds. Chatbot Arena shows two anonymous models, collects a vote, and fits Bradley-Terry coefficients: \(P(m \text{ beats } m') = 1/(1+e^{\xi_{m'}-\xi_m})\), estimated by maximum likelihood with sandwich-estimator confidence intervals [Chiang et al. 2024]. Crowd votes agreed with experts 72 to 83% of the time, close to the 79 to 90% expert-expert agreement (Tab. 3). The weaknesses are the population (hobbyists and researchers), style effects, and the incentives of a public leaderboard: The Leaderboard Illusion reports that Meta tested as many as 27 private variants in one month in the lead-up to Llama 4, that two large labs received about 19.2% and 20.4% of all arena data against 29.7% for 83 open-weight models combined, and that training on more arena data raised ArenaHard performance by up to 112% in relative terms [Singh et al. 2025]. The platform has since been rebranded Arena (January 2026).

Evaluation during pretraining

Mid-training evaluation has different goals from a release comparison. You want to see that the run is healthy, to choose between data and architecture options on small proxy runs, and to forecast the final model (page 13). That favors signals that are cheap, smooth and comparable over time, even if they are not what you will advertise. A useful panel has five parts:

  1. Held-out loss on domain slices (web, code, math, books, each language): the smoothest signal, and comparable across tokenizers only as bits per byte (page 01). It depends on the reference distribution and misleads across different training mixtures (page 12).
  2. Cloze multiple-choice suites: ARC-Easy and Challenge, HellaSwag, PIQA, WinoGrande, OpenBookQA, CommonsenseQA, SIQA, BoolQ, and MMLU in cloze form, with OLMES normalizations. FineWeb chose its ablation tasks by three rules: low variance across random samples of the same data, near-monotonic improvement during training, and scores above chance at the model size used [FineWeb, §3.1].
  3. Continuous proxies for hard generative tasks. GSM8K and HumanEval pass@1 are near zero for small models. The likelihood of the reference answer is not: switching from accuracy to the probability of the correct answer made MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, MBPP and HumanEval more than 80% predictable at 1B parameters from 0.01% of the compute [DataDecide, Abstract]. Signal-to-noise on GSM8K rose from 1.2 to 7.0 and on MBPP from 2.0 to 41.8 when the metric was bits per byte of the answer [Heineman et al. 2025, §5.3].
  4. Sampled generative evals (GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, MBPP) once the model is above the noise floor, and long-context probes later (page 10).
  5. Fresh or dated slices to catch contamination, and regression checks for capabilities that must not degrade (other languages, safety behavior).

Know your noise. Two notions matter. Signal is how far apart different models score, and noise is how much one model's score jitters, measured as the relative standard deviation over its final checkpoints. Their ratio predicts whether a small-scale ranking transfers: the correlation of signal-to-noise with decision accuracy was 0.79 across OLMES tasks, while signal alone or noise alone were nearly uncorrelated with it [Heineman, §3, §4.1]. On a 1B model's last 30 checkpoints, ARC-Challenge accuracy ranged over 1.7 points. Averaging the last several checkpoints, and using bits per byte instead of accuracy, both raised the ratio. Seed noise is also measurable directly. Across ten 7B runs on 210B tokens, the seed standard deviation was 0.21 for HellaSwag, 0.41 for PIQA, 0.55 for SIQA, 0.80 for ARC-Challenge and 1.11 for HumanEval, and generally below the 95% interval from the question count [Madaan, Tab. 1]. Run-to-run standard deviations at 1B parameters reach 2 accuracy points for some data recipes [DataDecide].

A practical rule that follows: two runs differ only if the gap exceeds \(\sqrt{2}\) times the seed standard deviation of that benchmark, times about 2 for confidence. For HellaSwag that is about 0.6 points, for ARC-Challenge about 2.3. Checkpoints taken at different points of the learning-rate schedule are not comparable, so compare after the decay phase (page 14).

Pitfall: tuning your suite to the run

Choosing the tasks that "work" on the runs you already have, or picking the checkpoint and prompt that maximize a benchmark you later report, is selection on the test set. Fix the suite, formulation and normalization before comparing runs, and report the held-out benchmarks you did not tune on.

Statistics: is the difference real?

Evaluations are experiments, and the standard tools apply [Miller 2024]. Treat the questions as drawn from a super-population. For a 0/1 score with observed accuracy \(\hat p\) on \(n\) independent questions, the standard error 标准误 and 95% confidence interval 置信区间 are

\[ \text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat p\,(1-\hat p)}{n}}, \qquad \text{CI}_{95\%} = \hat p \pm 1.96\,\text{SE} \]
Miller 2024, Eq. 2 and 3. Largest at \(\hat p = 0.5\).

Worked numbers for \(\hat p = 0.7\). With \(n=500\): \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.21/500} = \sqrt{0.00042} = 0.0205\), so 2.05 points and a 95% interval of \(\pm 4.02\) (66.0 to 74.0). With \(n = 14{,}000\): \(\sqrt{0.21/14000} = 0.00387\), so 0.39 points and \(\pm 0.76\). The interval shrinks as \(1/\sqrt{n}\): 28 times more questions buys a 5.3 times narrower interval. Figure 3 shows the curve with benchmark sizes marked.

The Wald form fails near 0 and 1 and at small \(n\): with 0 correct of 30 it gives the interval \([0, 0]\), while the Wilson score interval gives \([0, 11.4\%]\) (\(z^2/(n+z^2)\) at the upper end). The drill implements both.

Comparing two models

Unpaired, the difference has \(\text{SE}_{A-B} = \sqrt{\text{SE}_A^2 + \text{SE}_B^2}\). For two 70% models at \(n=500\) that is \(\sqrt{2}\times 2.05 = 2.90\) points, so a difference must exceed \(1.96 \times 2.90 = 5.7\) points before the interval excludes zero. At \(n=14{,}000\) the threshold is 1.07 points. But both models answer the same questions, and question difficulty is shared. Working with the per-question differences \(d_i = s_{A,i} - s_{B,i}\) gives a paired standard error that shrinks with the correlation \(\rho\) of the two models' scores:

\[ \text{SE}_{A-B,\text{paired}} = \sqrt{\text{SE}_A^2 + \text{SE}_B^2 - 2\rho\,\text{SE}_A\text{SE}_B} \;=\; \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{Var}(d)}{n}} \;\approx\; \sqrt{\frac{\delta}{n}}, \]

where \(\delta\) is the fraction of questions on which the models disagree (the approximation holds when the true gap is small). For Bernoulli scores at \(p=0.7\), \(\delta = 2p(1-p)(1-\rho) = 0.42\,(1-\rho)\), so \(\delta = 0.20\) means \(\rho \approx 0.52\), close to the 0.37 to 0.64 correlations in Miller's illustrative example [§4.2, Tab. 5].

How many questions detect a one-point gap? Unpaired between two 70% models: \(n = 1.96^2 \cdot 0.42 / 0.01^2 \approx 16{,}100\), and about 33,000 for 80% power. A paired test in practice. Cross-tabulate the models: \(b\) questions where only A is right, \(c\) where only B is. With \(n=500\), \(b=40\), \(c=25\): the accuracy gap is \(15/500 = 3.0\) points. Unpaired (72% against 69%) the gap is \(3.0/2.88 = 1.04\) standard errors, hopeless. Paired, \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{(65/500 - 0.03^2)/500} = 1.61\) points, so \(z = 1.87\), and the exact McNemar test (a binomial test of \(40\) against \(25\) on 65 discordant pairs) gives \(p = 0.082\), with a 95% interval of \(-0.15\) to \(+6.15\) points. Suggestive, not significant. The bootstrap gives the same answer without formulas: resample question indices with replacement, apply the same indices to both models, and take percentiles of the mean difference.

def paired_bootstrap(a, b, n_boot=2000, seed=0, alpha=0.05):
    d = np.asarray(a, float) - np.asarray(b, float)                 # per-question difference in {-1, 0, +1}
    idx = np.random.default_rng(seed).integers(0, d.size, (n_boot, d.size))
    boots = d[idx].mean(axis=1)                                     # same questions for both models
    return d.mean(), *np.quantile(boots, [alpha / 2, 1 - alpha / 2])

More than one source of noise

The variance of a benchmark mean splits into two parts, \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu) = \big(\mathrm{Var}(x) + \E[\sigma^2_i]\big)/n\): how difficulty varies across questions (\(x\), the per-question success probability) and how much a single question's outcome varies between samples (\(\sigma_i^2\)). Resampling each question \(K\) times shrinks only the second term to \(\E[\sigma_i^2]/K\) [Miller, §3.1]. The first term does not shrink, so averaging 64 samples cannot fix a small question set. In Miller's toy model with uniformly distributed difficulty (\(\mathrm{Var}(x) = 1/12\), \(\E[\sigma^2] = 1/6\), total \(1/4\) at \(p = 0.5\)), AIME-sized \(n=30\) has \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.25/30} = 9.1\) points at \(K=1\) and \(\sqrt{(1/12 + 1/(6\cdot 64))/30} = 5.4\) points at \(K=64\). Across 20 seeds, AIME'24 pass@1 for 1.5B and 7B reasoning models had a standard deviation of 5 to 15 points, and averaging over \(K \ge 30\) seeds is needed to stabilize it (for R1-Distill-1.5B, the variance of the mean fell from 40.0 at \(K=1\) to 1.05 at \(K=30\)). Hardware alone moved scores by up to 8 points for one model, and repeated runs on the same A100 cluster gave 19.3% to 23.0% [Hochlehnert et al., §3.2, §3.4].

Clustered questions. If questions come in groups (several questions per reading passage, one question translated into many languages), treat clusters as the sampling unit. On DROP, clustered standard errors were 3.05 times the naive ones, on MGSM 1.88 times, on RACE-H 1.10 times [Miller, §2.2, Tab. 4]. Multiple comparisons. Reporting 20 benchmarks, each with a 5% false-positive rate, gives \(1 - 0.95^{20} = 64\%\) chance that at least one improvement is spurious if the benchmarks were independent. Fix a primary metric in advance, correct for the rest, and report all of them.

Contamination and leakage

Contamination is any path by which test information reaches training: exact copies, near-duplicates, paraphrases and translations, the same source material, synthetic data from a model that saw the benchmark, or tuning checkpoints and hyperparameters on the test set. Data-side filtering by n-gram overlap is on page 12. The evaluation-side toolbox has four parts:

  • Likelihood-based detection. Min-K% Prob averages the log-probability of the 20% least likely tokens of a text, on the idea that unseen text contains a few surprising tokens while seen text does not [Shi et al. 2024]. On WikiMIA (394 recent events as non-members) it reached AUC 0.72 on average against 0.67 for plain perplexity, and 0.86 against 0.84 for detecting benchmark examples injected into continued pretraining. That is modest, and detection is harder for short texts and low duplication counts.
  • Order tests. If the benchmark is exchangeable, no ordering should be likelier than another. Compare the model's log-likelihood of the canonical order with shuffled orders, with a permutation test that has a false-positive guarantee [Oren et al. 2023]. On a 1.4B model with injected test sets it detected duplication of 10 or more but not a single copy, and it found p = 0.014 (LLaMA-2) and 0.011 (Mistral) for MMLU after filtering non-exchangeable subsets. Test sets can also carry a canary string so training corpora can filter them out, as GPQA does [GPQA].
  • Fresh or rewritten variants. GSM1k measures the gap between GSM8K and a matched, never-released set. Drops reached 8 points (Phi and Mistral families), and the per-character likelihood of GSM8K samples correlated with the gap (Spearman 0.36), while frontier models showed almost no drop [Zhang et al., §5.4]. The correlation is modest: overfitting also arises indirectly, for instance from training on benchmark-shaped data or selecting checkpoints on the test set.
  • Temporal splits. LiveCodeBench dates each problem, so a model can be scored only on problems released after its cutoff. DeepSeek models drop sharply on LeetCode problems released after August 2023, GPT-4-O declines on problems since November 2023 (its stated cutoff), and DeepSeek-Base-33B fell from about 60 pass@1 on May problems to about 0 on September LeetCode ones [LiveCodeBench, §5.1].

N-gram filters miss paraphrase and translation: rephrased test items evaded 10-gram matching entirely, and embedding similarity needs subject-specific thresholds while an LLM decontaminator did better [Yang et al., §5.2.1]. A clean-subset versus full-set comparison is itself biased when the clean subset differs in difficulty. Contamination effects also vary by benchmark and model size, and DeepSeek-R1's report itself notes that 10-gram decontamination cannot stop paraphrase [R1, Supp. D.1]. A defensible practice: decontaminate before training, evaluate on dated or private sets, keep a held-out slice you never publish, and read a benchmark's score against its release date.

Evaluating reasoning models and agents

Long chains of thought, stochastic sampling, a token budget that changes results, and tool harnesses all add sources of variance. The reporting conventions that have emerged:

  • Sample, do not go greedy, and average. DeepSeek-R1 found that greedy decoding of long-output reasoning models raised repetition and gave large variability across checkpoints, so it reports pass@1 as the mean correctness over \(k\) samples at temperature 0.6 and top-\(p\) 0.95, with \(k=64\) for AIME and GPQA, 16 for MATH and Codeforces, 8 for LiveCodeBench, and outputs capped at 32,768 tokens [R1, Supp. D.1]. That estimator is just \(\frac{1}{k}\sum_i p_i\). It matches Miller's advice against lowering temperature to reduce variance, since that changes the quantity being estimated [§3.3].
  • Report the token budget or effort level. DeepSeek-V4 offers three modes (Non-think, High, Max) and evaluates them with context windows of 8K, 128K and 384K tokens. V4-Pro scores 7.7, 34.5 and 37.7 on HLE and 72.9, 89.1 and 90.1 on GPQA Diamond across the three [V4, §5.3.1, Tab. 7]. A model's "score" without its effort level is not a number. Models are also very sensitive to a maximum-token cap because truncation is scored as failure [Hochlehnert, Fig. 8]. The right object is an accuracy-versus-tokens curve.
  • Use many seeds on small sets, and test paired. On AIME'24 a single question is 3.3 points and a claimed gain has to beat that noise. Re-evaluated with a paired t-test against the R1-Distill 1.5B baseline, the OpenRS models showed no statistically significant improvement [Hochlehnert, §4.3].
  • Report the harness for agents. DeepSeek-V4's code-agent runs use a minimal bash-plus-file-edit harness with at most 500 steps and a 512K context [V4, §5.3.1]. Scores depend on tools, step limits, timeouts and environment fixes, which is why the Terminal-Bench "Verified" subset differs by about 4 points. Add reliability: pass^k on stochastic tasks, not just pass@1.

A compact reporting checklist: number of questions and clustering; samples per question; temperature and top-\(p\); token budget or effort; extraction and grader; harness, tools and step limit; seeds; a standard error or interval; the model's data cutoff against the benchmark's release date.

Common misconceptions

  • "Higher MMLU means a better model." Above about 85% the differences are within label noise (6.49% erroneous questions) and prompt sensitivity (4 to 5 points), and the benchmark no longer ranks frontier models. Use a discriminating set and an interval.
  • "pass@k equals \(1-(1-\text{pass@1})^k\)." Only for a single problem with a known \(p\). Across problems of different difficulty it fails: half the problems at \(p=0.9\) and half at \(p=0.1\) give mean pass@1 of 0.5 and pass@5 of \(0.5(1-0.1^5) + 0.5(1-0.9^5) = 0.705\), not \(1-0.5^5 = 0.969\). Estimate per problem, then average. Plugging in \(c/n\) is also biased low.
  • "Overlapping confidence intervals mean no difference." Non-overlap is sufficient, not necessary. Two models share question difficulty, and a paired test can be significant when the marginal intervals overlap (72% against 69% at \(n=500\) has \(z=1.04\) unpaired and 1.87 paired).
  • "Temperature 0 gives the true, reproducible score." Greedy decoding changes the estimand and can hurt long reasoning traces, and it is not reproducible anyway: identical settings on one A100 cluster gave 19.3% to 23.0% on AIME'24.
  • "Averaging 64 samples removes the noise." It removes the within-question sampling term only. The question-sampling floor remains, about 5 points on a 30-problem set.
  • "Contamination means exact string overlap." Paraphrased and translated test items evade n-gram filters and still lift scores (Llama-2-13B on MMLU: 54.8 to 85.9), and indirect leakage such as benchmark-shaped data or checkpoint selection shows up without any overlap.
  • "A stronger judge is an unbiased judge." GPT-4 was still consistent under order swaps in only 65.0% of cases and prefers longer answers. Swap positions, give references, and control for length.
  • "Cloze and multiple choice measure the same thing." Llama-3-70B scores 69.0 (cloze) and 93.7 (multiple choice) on ARC-Challenge. The first is likelihood of answer text, the second is symbol binding, and they diverge by model strength.

Interview questions

Warm-up What is the standard error of an accuracy measured on \(n\) questions? Compute the 95% interval for 70% accuracy at \(n=500\) and \(n=14{,}000\).

Treat each question as an independent Bernoulli trial: \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\) and the interval is \(\hat p \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}\). At \(n=500\): \(\sqrt{0.21/500} = 0.0205\), so 2.05 points and \(\pm 4.02\), i.e. 66.0 to 74.0. At \(n=14{,}000\): \(\sqrt{0.21/14000} = 0.00387\), so 0.39 points and \(\pm 0.76\).

Caveats worth saying: this counts only question-sampling noise (not seeds, prompts, or hardware), assumes independent questions (clustered sets like DROP need clustered errors, up to 3 times larger), and the Wald form breaks near 0% or 100% and at small \(n\), where Wilson is better. To compare two models use the paired standard error of the difference, not two separate intervals.

Warm-up Why do we normalize log-likelihoods when scoring multiple choice by cloze? Name the options and one failure of each.

A candidate's score is a sum of per-token log-probabilities, each at most 0, so a longer candidate is penalized for length alone and the raw rule favors short answers. Options: per-token division (depends on the tokenizer, and penalizes multi-token names that the tokenizer splits oddly), per-character division (lm-evaluation-harness acc_norm; ignores that some characters are far more predictable than others), and PMI, \(\log P(a\mid q) - \log P(a\mid\text{generic})\), which divides out the answer's prior plausibility but costs a second forward pass and can over-reward rare strings. Raw likelihood is right when candidates are the same length (WinoGrande) or single tokens (BoolQ).

The page's toy example shows all four rules picking different answers from the same log-probabilities. OLMES chooses per task by which rule works best across 15 models: PMI for ARC-Challenge, CommonsenseQA and OpenBookQA, per-character for MMLU and HellaSwag, none for BoolQ and WinoGrande.

Warm-up Define pass@k, pass^k and majority vote@k. What does each measure and which needs a checker?

pass@k: the probability that at least one of \(k\) samples is correct. It is an oracle metric (a checker selects the winner) and rises with \(k\). pass^k (pass hat k): the probability that all \(k\) samples are correct, \(\E[\binom{c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}]\). It measures reliability and falls with \(k\). maj@k or cons@k: sample \(k\) answers and return the most frequent; it needs no checker at inference time, only a canonical answer form. For one problem with \(n=20\), \(c=3\): pass@1 = 0.15, pass@5 = 0.60, pass@10 = 0.89, pass^2 = 0.016. DeepSeek-R1 on AIME 2024: pass@1 79.8, cons@64 86.7, pass@64 90.0, and a deployed system without an oracle can at best get near the middle one.

Core Derive the unbiased pass@k estimator. Why not just use \(1-(1-\hat p)^k\)? Compute it for \(n=20\), \(c=3\), \(k=1,5,10\).

For a problem with per-sample success probability \(p\), \(\text{pass@}k = 1-(1-p)^k\). Draw \(n \ge k\) samples, observe \(c\) correct. The plug-in \(1-(1-c/n)^k\) is biased low: \((1-x)^k\) is convex, so Jensen gives \(\E[(1-\hat p)^k] \ge (1-p)^k\).

Instead estimate \((1-p)^k\) by the probability that a uniformly random size-\(k\) subset of the \(n\) samples has no correct one, \(\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}\). Each fixed subset is all-wrong with probability \((1-p)^k\) because its samples are independent, so averaging over subsets and over \(c\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)\) keeps the expectation \((1-p)^k\): unbiased. Cancelling factorials gives \(\prod_{i=n-c+1}^{n}(1-k/i)\), a product of terms in \((0,1]\) that cannot overflow, whereas \(\binom{n}{k}\) can. If \(n-c < k\) the answer is 1. Benchmark pass@k is the mean over problems.

Numbers: \(k=1\): \(1-17/20 = 0.150\). \(k=5\): \(1-6188/15504 = 0.601\) (plug-in 0.556). \(k=10\): \(1-19448/184756 = 0.895\) (plug-in 0.803).

Core A new method improves MMLU by 0.8 points. Is it real? What would you check?

I would not answer yes or no. Checks, in order:

  1. Same protocol. Identical prompt, shots, formulation (cloze or multiple choice), normalization, harness version and tokenization for both models. Many "gains" are an eval change.
  2. Question-sampling noise, paired. \(n \approx 14{,}000\). If the models disagree on 10% of questions the paired standard error is 0.27 points, \(z=3.0\); at 20% it is 0.38 points, \(z=2.1\); at 30%, \(z=1.7\). Run McNemar or a paired bootstrap on the per-question outcomes rather than eyeballing marginal intervals.
  3. Training noise. One run each is one sample. Seed standard deviations at 7B are 0.2 to 0.8 points per benchmark, so two seeds differ by 0.3 to 1.1 points. I would rerun the baseline (or use the noise of the last checkpoints) and require the gain to exceed about \(2\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_{\text{seed}}\).
  4. Multiple comparisons. Was MMLU picked because it moved? Look at the pre-registered suite, all benchmarks, and the held-out loss. A real improvement in knowledge should not appear on only one benchmark.
  5. Contamination and data. Did the new method or its data change exposure to MMLU-like text? Compare on a fresh or rephrased set.
  6. Ceiling. With about 6.5% of questions erroneous, remaining gains near the top are partly noise, and prompt variation (4 to 5 points) exceeds 0.8. Check robustness to prompt and option order.
  7. Practical significance and scale. Does the gain hold at a second scale, and is 0.8 points worth the cost?
Core Log-likelihood versus generative evaluation: when do they disagree, and why?

They estimate different things. Likelihood scoring (cloze or letter probabilities) measures how much probability the model puts on the right text or symbol given a fixed prompt: direct-answer knowledge plus format skill. Generation with extraction measures whether the model can produce the answer, including reasoning, formatting and stopping. They disagree in five situations:

  • Reasoning tasks. Chain of thought adds 19.1 points on MMLU-Pro for GPT-4o but only 1.5 on MMLU, and hurts small models on MMLU (Llama-3-8B −3.9). Likelihood scoring cannot see the computation.
  • Formulation and model strength. Cloze beats multiple choice for weak models and loses badly for strong ones (ARC-Challenge Llama-3-70B: 69.0 cloze, 93.7 multiple choice).
  • Surface-form effects. Cloze is confounded by answer length and prior string likelihood, hence normalization.
  • Post-trained models. A chat model may put its mass on a preamble ("Sure, ...") so letter or continuation likelihoods are meaningless, while generation works.
  • Extraction failures. Generative scores drop when the answer format is not followed (MMLU-Pro falls back to random), which is a format failure, not a knowledge failure.

Use likelihood metrics for base models and early checkpoints (lower variance: MMLU-cloze seed standard deviation 0.22 against 0.57) and generation for post-trained and reasoning models. Across 70 trained models the two MMLU formulations correlate at 0.92, so rankings usually agree while absolute levels do not.

Core What are the biases of LLM-as-judge and how do you mitigate them?

Position bias (GPT-4 consistent under swapping in 65.0% of pairs, Claude-v1 23.8%), verbosity bias (a padded duplicate list fooled Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 91.3% of the time and GPT-4 8.7%), self-preference (GPT-4's own-answer win rate about 10 points higher, Claude-v1 25; models can recognize their outputs, and self-recognition correlates linearly with self-preference after fine-tuning), and weak reasoning when grading (14 of 20 wrong answers accepted on math, 3 of 20 with a reference answer).

Mitigations: evaluate both orders and count a win only when it survives the swap; few-shot judge examples (65.0 to 77.5% consistency); reference-guided grading for verifiable questions; a judge from a different model family than those judged; length control by regression (AlpacaEval LC: Arena correlation 0.94 to 0.98) or style features in the Bradley-Terry fit; multiple judges; and calibrating against human labels on a sample. Report agreement with humans, since even good judges drift with prompt and version.

Core How do you detect benchmark contamination?

It depends on what you can access. With the training data: n-gram overlap (GPT-3 used 13-grams, DeepSeek-R1 10-grams) plus embedding or LLM-based matching for paraphrases. Without it, statistical tests on the model: Min-K% Prob (mean log-probability of the 20% least likely tokens; AUC about 0.72 on WikiMIA against 0.67 for perplexity), and order tests that compare the log-likelihood of the canonical benchmark order with shuffled orders (detects duplication of 10 or more in a 1.4B model with a false-positive guarantee, but not one copy). Behavioral checks: a matched fresh set (GSM1k showed drops up to 8 points), rephrased variants, and temporal splits (LiveCodeBench shows sharp drops after a model's cutoff). Canary strings help filtering.

Limits: paraphrase and translation defeat n-grams (rephrased MMLU took Llama-2-13B from 54.8 to 85.9 undetected); detectors have power only above some duplication level; and indirect leakage (benchmark-shaped data, checkpoint selection) leaves no overlap at all. So combine detection with evaluation designed to be robust: dated or private sets and a clean-versus-full comparison.

Core Which evals would you track during pretraining of a 7B model, and why?

A panel, chosen for signal-to-noise rather than prestige:

  1. Held-out bits per byte on domain slices (web, code, math, multilingual): smooth, cheap, tokenizer-independent. Not comparable across different training mixtures.
  2. Cloze multiple-choice suite with OLMES normalizations (ARC-E/C, HellaSwag, PIQA, WinoGrande, OpenBookQA, CommonsenseQA, SIQA, BoolQ, MMLU-cloze): above chance early, near monotonic, low seed noise (0.2 to 0.8 points). Add MCF MMLU once the model passes roughly 400B to 800B tokens.
  3. Continuous proxies for GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, MBPP (probability or bits per byte of the reference answer) while pass@1 is near zero, then sampled pass@1 with multiple samples.
  4. Long-context probes and a fresh or dated slice late in training, and regression checks for languages and safety.

Protocol: fix the suite and formulation up front; measure noise from the last 10 to 20 checkpoints and average them; use cheaper sub-sampling only where signal-to-noise survives (tiny IRT-selected subsets increased seed variance); compare runs only after the learning-rate decay; and treat differences below about \(2\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_{\text{seed}}\) as ties. Core Why is a paired comparison more powerful than comparing two accuracies with error bars? Derive the minimum detectable difference for two models on \(n\) questions.

The models answer the same questions, so their per-question scores are positively correlated (hard questions are hard for both). \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu_A - \hat\mu_B) = [\mathrm{Var}(s_A) + \mathrm{Var}(s_B) - 2\mathrm{Cov}(s_A,s_B)]/n\). The unpaired formula drops the covariance and so overstates the variance, and overlapping marginal intervals say little.

Take the per-question difference \(d_i \in \{-1,0,1\}\). If the models disagree on a fraction \(\delta\) of questions, \(\mathrm{Var}(d) = \delta - \Delta^2 \approx \delta\), so \(\text{SE} \approx \sqrt{\delta/n}\) and the minimum detectable difference at 95% is \(1.96\sqrt{\delta/n}\). For \(n=500\) and \(\delta=0.2\): 3.92 points, versus 5.68 unpaired for two 70% models. For \(n=14{,}000\): 0.74 versus 1.07. Equivalently, the questions needed for a gap \(\Delta\) is \(n \approx 1.96^2\,\delta/\Delta^2\), doubled or so for 80% power. Test it with McNemar (exact binomial on the \(b\) and \(c\) discordant counts) or a paired bootstrap that resamples questions once for both models. On the page example (\(b=40\), \(c=25\), \(n=500\)) that gives \(p=0.082\), while the unpaired \(z\) is 1.04.

Deep-dive You compare two reasoning models on AIME with 30 problems and 64 samples each. Model A scores 68.0 and B scores 63.5. How uncertain is the difference, and what would you do?

Two variance components. Seed or sampling noise: with 64 samples per question the within-question term is small (in Miller's toy model about 0.9 points of standard error for one model). Question sampling: only 30 questions, so one problem is 3.3 points and the floor is around \(\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(x)/30} \approx 5.3\) points per model in the toy model, more for a single-sample score (9.1). A 4.5-point gap is within one standard error of a difference even if 64 samples eliminated all sampling noise.

What I would do: (1) a paired analysis on the per-question mean scores (the two models' difficulty profiles correlate), with a paired bootstrap or Wilcoxon test over the 30 problems, since the floor is about questions, not samples; (2) look at per-problem disagreement (is the gap driven by 2 problems?); (3) add more questions: AIME 2025, HMMT, and other sets, and aggregate across them with clustered errors; (4) check that budget, temperature and truncation were identical, since max-token caps alone shift results; (5) run repeated evaluations on the same hardware to see the reproducibility floor (up to 8 points across clusters in one study). I would report an interval and say the models are not separated on this set.

Deep-dive Design question: you must choose between two pretraining data mixtures using 1B-parameter proxy runs. Design the evaluation and the decision rule.

Evidence to plan around. Ranking data recipes at 150M predicts the 1B winner for about 80% of pairs, and none of eight scaling-law baselines did better; seed standard deviation at 1B reaches 2 points for some recipes; continuous likelihood metrics are more predictable at small scale [DataDecide].

Design. (a) Match compute and tokens across arms and use at least two seeds each (FineWeb used two seeds). (b) Choose tasks by signal-to-noise on existing runs, not by name: keep those above chance at the proxy size with monotone curves, drop those at chance (MMLU multiple choice at 1B). (c) Use cloze formulations with OLMES normalizations, plus bits per byte on held-out domains and a continuous proxy for code and math. (d) Average the last several checkpoints to cut checkpoint noise. (e) Decision rule: pre-declare a primary aggregate; call a winner only if the paired difference exceeds \(2\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_{\text{seed}}\) on it and the arms agree in sign on most individual tasks and on held-out loss; else run more seeds or a second scale. (f) Confirm finalists at a larger scale, since recipes can cross over with scale and some effects (dedup, repetition, unique-token supply) are invisible in small runs. (g) Check for contamination differences between mixtures and do not tune the mixture on a benchmark you will report.

Trade-off: bigger proxies and more seeds cost compute but reduce wrong decisions, so spend it on the closest pairs, where a wrong call is most likely.

Deep-dive Design question: evaluate a new coding-agent model. SWE-bench Verified is contaminated and saturating, and agents are stochastic. What is your protocol?

Tasks. Do not rely on one public set. Combine a public benchmark for comparability (SWE-bench variants, Terminal-Bench) with tasks that are dated after the training cutoff, a private held-out set from internal repositories with human-written tests, and a few realistic long-horizon tasks (DeepSeek-V4 kept 30 of about 200 internal tasks after quality filtering). Audit test quality: flawed or over-specific tests were the main reason a leading benchmark was set aside.

Harness. Fix and publish tools, step limit (V4: 500), context size, timeouts, sandbox and scaffold, and run the baselines through the same harness. Harness differences move scores as much as model differences (Terminal-Bench environment fixes shifted V4-Pro by about 4 points).

Statistics. Multiple runs per task (\(n\) large enough for pass^k), paired comparisons over tasks, clustered standard errors when tasks share a repository, and intervals on everything. With 89 to 500 tasks, standard errors are 2 to 5 points, so only larger gaps count.

Metrics. pass@1 as an average over runs, pass^k for reliability, cost and tokens or steps per solved task, and rubric or judge-based review for quality (does the patch pass tests but break style or safety?). Contamination checks: performance versus task date, and a memorization probe such as asking for the gold patch from the task ID.

Deep-dive Explain how length-controlled AlpacaEval works, what it assumes, and how it can be gamed.

The judge's preference is modeled by a logistic regression with three terms: a model-identity term \(\theta_m - \theta_b\), a length term \(\phi_{m,b}\tanh(\Delta\text{len}/\text{std}(\Delta\text{len}))\), and an instruction-difficulty term \((\psi_m - \psi_b)\gamma_x\), fit by cross-entropy with L2 regularization. The length-controlled win rate is the model's implied win rate with the length term set to 0: \(\text{winrate}_{LC} = 100\cdot\E_x[\sigma(\theta_m-\theta_b + (\psi_m-\psi_b)\gamma_x)]\). This is a counterfactual (a controlled direct effect): what would the preference be if both answers had equal length. The \(\tanh\) gives diminishing returns to length differences in log-odds, and the form keeps the baseline at 50% and preserves swap symmetry.

Assumptions: length is the only confounder you modeled (formatting, lists and tone remain), the effect of length on preference is additive in log-odds and shared in form across models, and equal-length comparison is what you want. It cut the gap between concise and verbose prompting (22.9 to 64.3% raw versus 41.9 to 51.6%) and raised Arena correlation from 0.94 to 0.98.

Gaming: the authors test an adversarial case, truncating GPT-4's outputs. The raw AlpacaEval 2 win rate is 3.7%, but without regularization the length-controlled one rises to 25.9%, presumably because the regression can blame the poor results of very short outputs on the length term, which is then zeroed. Regularizing the length term so that the model terms explain performance first brings it down to 12.2% [Dubois et al., §4.3]. The lesson is that any post-hoc correction becomes a new optimization target.

Cheat sheet

  • Score = expectation over questions, prompt, decoding rule and scorer. Report all four, plus \(n\) and an error bar.
  • \(\text{SE} = \sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\); 95% CI \(= \hat p \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}\). At \(p=0.7\): \(n=500\) gives 2.05 points (\(\pm 4.02\)); \(n=14{,}000\) gives 0.39 (\(\pm 0.76\)). Use Wilson near 0 or 1.
  • Minimum detectable gap \(=1.96\sqrt{2}\,\text{SE}\) unpaired (5.7 points at 500, 1.07 at 14,000); paired \(\approx 1.96\sqrt{\delta/n}\) with disagreement \(\delta\). Test with McNemar or a paired bootstrap. 80% power needs 2.04 times the questions.
  • \(\widehat{\text{pass@}k} = 1 - \binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k} = 1-\prod_{i=n-c+1}^{n}(1-k/i)\); unbiased because a fixed \(k\)-subset is all-wrong with probability \((1-p)^k\). Plug-in \(1-(1-c/n)^k\) is biased low. \(n=20\), \(c=3\): 0.150, 0.601, 0.895 for \(k=1,5,10\). Average over problems, never from mean pass@1.
  • pass^k \(=\binom{c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}\) (reliability, falls with \(k\)); cons@\(k\) needs no checker. R1 on AIME 2024: 79.8 pass@1, 86.7 cons@64, 90.0 pass@64.
  • Cloze: score continuations; raw, per-token, per-character or PMI normalization can each pick a different answer. Cloze for weak or early models, multiple choice for strong ones (MMLU MCF at chance until about 400B to 800B tokens).
  • Judges: position (GPT-4 65% swap-consistent), verbosity, self-preference, weak reasoning. Swap and require agreement, give references, control length (LC AlpacaEval: Arena correlation 0.94 to 0.98).
  • Pretraining panel: held-out bits per byte, cloze suite, continuous proxies for generative tasks, later sampled generative and long-context. Noise = relative SD of last checkpoints; compare after decay; gap \(> 2\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_{\text{seed}}\).
  • Variance splits into question difficulty and sampling: resampling \(K\) times fixes only the second. AIME: one problem = 3.3 points; 64 samples leaves about 5 points of question noise.
  • Contamination: n-gram (data side), Min-K% Prob, order tests, fresh matched sets (GSM1k), dated splits (LiveCodeBench), private sets. Paraphrase beats n-grams (MMLU 54.8 to 85.9).
  • Reasoning models: sample (T 0.6, top-\(p\) 0.95 in R1), average pass@1 over \(k\), state token budget and effort (V4-Pro HLE 7.7, 34.5, 37.7 for Non-think, High, Max), state the harness.
  • Benchmarks expire: MMLU to MMLU-Pro to HLE; HumanEval to LiveCodeBench; SWE-bench Verified was retired by one lab in 2026. Keep a private refreshed set.
