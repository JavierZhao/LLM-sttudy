Parameter-efficient finetuning (LoRA, QLoRA)

P1 ~40 min Prereqs: 04 Transformer block, 19 SFT

Finetuning all of Llama-3-8B needs about 128 GB of GPU memory for weights, gradients and optimizer states alone. LoRA freezes the pretrained weights and trains a low-rank update, which cuts that to about 17 GB, and QLoRA also stores the frozen weights in 4 bits, which cuts it to about 6.4 GB. This page covers the math, the initialization and scaling choices interviewers ask about, the QLoRA quantization tricks, the main variants, and the evidence on what low-rank updates can and cannot learn, including for RL.

Why finetune parameter-efficiently

The memory arithmetic

Full finetuning 全参数微调 updates every weight. With AdamW in the standard mixed-precision recipe it costs 16 bytes per parameter before activations: bf16 weights (2), bf16 gradients (2), an fp32 master copy (4) and two fp32 Adam moments (4 + 4), as derived on page 15. For Llama-3-8B (8,030,261,248 parameters):

\[ 8{,}030{,}261{,}248 \times 16\ \text{B} = 128{,}484{,}179{,}968\ \text{B} \approx 128.5\ \text{GB} \quad (119.7\ \text{GiB}) \]

Three quarters of that is optimizer state and master weights, so a single 80 GB GPU cannot even hold the states. Every finetuned variant is also a separate 16 GB bf16 checkpoint to store and serve. At GPT-3 scale, Hu et al. report that a LoRA cut training memory from 1.2 TB to 350 GB and a checkpoint from 350 GB to 35 MB [Hu et al. 2021, §4.2].

Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) 参数高效微调 freezes the pretrained weights and trains a small set of new or selected parameters, so gradients and optimizer states exist only for those. The families are: insert small modules (adapters), train extra inputs or key/value prefixes (prompt and prefix tuning), rescale activations with learned vectors (IA³), or learn a low-rank weight update (LoRA). LoRA 低秩适配 dominates in practice because its update can be merged into the weights, so serving costs nothing extra (Variants covers the others).

What PEFT saves, and what it does not

It saves gradients, master weights and Adam states for every frozen weight, and the per-task storage. It does not save the frozen weights themselves (2 bytes per parameter in bf16) or the activations: backpropagation still runs through every frozen layer, so activations must be stored (or recomputed) exactly as in full finetuning. For a 7B model at batch size 1, the QLoRA paper measures 567 MB of LoRA input gradients against 26 MB of adapter parameters [Dettmers et al. 2023, §2].

Why so few parameters can be enough: intrinsic dimension

Aghajanyan et al. measured the intrinsic dimension 内在维度 of finetuning. Instead of optimizing \(\theta \in \R^{D}\), optimize \(\theta = \theta_0 + P\,\theta_d\) with a fixed random projection \(P: \R^d \to \R^D\) (they use the Fastfood transform to make \(P\) cheap) and \(\theta_d\) initialized to 0, so training starts at the pretrained model. The smallest \(d\) that reaches 90% of full-finetuning performance is \(d_{90}\). For RoBERTa-Large, \(d_{90}\) is about 200 on MRPC (207 with their layer-aware variant, 322 with the plain one) and 774 on QQP [Aghajanyan et al. 2020, Table 1]. Two more findings matter: \(d_{90}\) falls as pretraining proceeds (their Fig. 2), and larger pretrained models have smaller \(d_{90}\) (Fig. 3).

Hu et al. cite this as inspiration but make a different and stronger hypothesis: the update \(\Delta W\) of each weight matrix has low rank [Hu et al. 2021, §4.1]. That is a hypothesis, not a consequence. A random low-dimensional subspace of all parameters sufficing is a different statement from each matrix update being low rank, and the measured tasks (MRPC, QQP) are sentence-pair classification, easy for a large pretrained model. Section 5 shows what happens on harder tasks.

From your jet tagger

If you trained your tagger from scratch, every parameter was capacity you needed to learn the whole jet-to-label function. Finetuning a pretrained LLM starts from a function that is already close to what you want, and post-training data is tiny next to the model. That is the regime where a low-rank update is plausible. It is also why LoRA struggles once the "finetuning" set is billions of tokens.

LoRA: low-rank adaptation

The parametrization

For a frozen pretrained weight \(W_0 \in \R^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}\), LoRA learns \(W = W_0 + \Delta W\) with a rank-\(r\) factorized update scaled by \(\alpha/r\):

\[ h = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r}\, B A\, x, \qquad A \in \R^{r \times d_{\text{in}}},\quad B \in \R^{d_{\text{out}} \times r},\quad r \ll \min(d_{\text{in}}, d_{\text{out}}) \]
Hu et al. 2021, Eq. 3 and §4.1 (the paper writes \(d \times k\) for \(d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}\))
Layout

This is the one place in the course where the weight is stored transposed relative to "\(xW\)". PyTorch's nn.Linear stores \(W\) as \((d_{\text{out}}, d_{\text{in}})\) and computes \(y = xW^\top\) for a row-vector \(x\). The LoRA paper and the PEFT library use the same layout, so in code \(y = xW_0^\top + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}(xA^\top)B^\top\).

\(\Delta W\) has rank at most \(r\), and it is never formed during training: \(x\) is projected down to \(r\) dimensions by \(A\), then back up by \(B\). The adapter adds \(r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}})\) parameters instead of \(d_{\text{in}} d_{\text{out}}\), and the same fraction of extra FLOPs per token. For a \(4096 \times 4096\) matrix at \(r = 16\) that is \(131{,}072 / 16{,}777{,}216 = 0.78\%\).

Initialization: why \(B = 0\)

Hu et al. use a random Gaussian \(A\) and \(B = 0\), "so \(\Delta W = BA\) is zero at the beginning of training". With \(g = \partial L/\partial h\) and \(\gamma = \alpha/r\), the gradients are

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial B} = \gamma\, g\,(Ax)^\top, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial A} = \gamma\, B^\top g\, x^\top . \]
  1. The model starts exactly at the pretrained function. With \(B = 0\), \(\Delta W = 0\), and finetuning begins from the only function you know works. A random \(B\) and \(A\) would inject noise of size \(\gamma\|BA\|\) into every layer.
  2. Not both zero. \(\partial L/\partial B\) is proportional to \(A\) and \(\partial L/\partial A\) to \(B\), so with both zero neither ever moves: a saddle point [Hayou et al. 2024, §3.1].
  3. One of the two must be random, and either works. LoRA+ analyzes both choices. The convention is random \(A\) with variance about \(1/d_{\text{in}}\) (PEFT: uniform on \(\pm 1/\sqrt{d_{\text{in}}}\)), so \(Ax\) has \(O(1)\) entries whatever the width, and zero \(B\).

A consequence worth stating in an interview: at step 0, \(\partial L/\partial A = 0\) exactly, so only \(B\) moves. \(A\) starts learning once \(B\) is nonzero, so the adapter's effective learning rate grows over training as \(B\) reaches the scale of \(A\). Thinking Machines report that this makes the best learning-rate multiplier larger for short runs (about 15× the full-finetuning rate under roughly 100 steps, converging to 10×) [Schulman & Thinking Machines 2025].

The scale \(\alpha/r\)

Hu et al.: "When optimizing with Adam, tuning \(\alpha\) is roughly the same as tuning the learning rate if we scale the initialization appropriately. As a result, we simply set \(\alpha\) to the first \(r\) we try and do not tune it. This scaling helps to reduce the need to retune hyperparameters when we vary \(r\)" [Hu et al. 2021, §4.1].

Why \(1/r\). \(BA = \sum_{i=1}^{r} b_i a_i^\top\) is a sum of \(r\) rank-1 terms. Thinking Machines note that after the first update each term has the same expected value regardless of \(r\), so \((1/r)\sum_i\) is an average of \(r\) samples whose expectation does not depend on \(r\). Empirically, with \(\alpha\) fixed the learning curves for different ranks coincide at the start of training, and the best learning rate moves by less than 2× between \(r = 4\) and \(r = 512\) in their sweeps.

Change \(r\) but not \(\alpha\): \(\gamma\) shrinks like \(1/r\). More rank adds capacity that is used later, not faster initial learning, and the same learning rate keeps working. Change \(\alpha\) but not \(r\): you rescale the update, which is a learning-rate change. Thinking Machines show that with Adam (\(\epsilon = 0\)) training is exactly invariant under \(\alpha \to \alpha/(pq)\), \(\text{init}_A \to p\,\text{init}_A\), \(\text{LR}_A \to p\,\text{LR}_A\), \(\text{LR}_B \to q\,\text{LR}_B\). Of the four hyperparameters \((\alpha, \text{init}_A, \text{LR}_A, \text{LR}_B)\) only two matter.

rsLoRA's Theorem 3.2 says that for \(B = 0\) and \(A\) entries i.i.d. with variance independent of \(r\), the adapter is rank-stabilized (activations and gradients through it stay \(\Theta(1)\) as \(r \to \infty\)) if and only if \(\gamma_r \in \Theta(1/\sqrt{r})\). A rank-4 learning-rate sweep reaches perplexity 1.863 at best, against 1.836 for rank 2048 at the default rate, so it is not only a learning-rate effect [Kalajdzievski 2023, §4, App. B.4].

These positions are less contradictory than they look, because they hold different things fixed: rsLoRA's proof follows SGD-style updates at one learning rate for all ranks, Thinking Machines' observation is for Adam with a swept learning rate, and Biderman et al. use a swept rate with \(\gamma\) constant. Whatever convention you use, re-sweep the learning rate when you change \(r\) if \(\gamma\) does not scale as \(1/r\).

Which matrices, which rank, which learning rate

Modules. The LoRA paper adapted attention only, and its own budget study (18M parameters on GPT-3) shows spreading a fixed budget over more matrices beats one matrix at higher rank: \(W_q\) alone at \(r=8\) gives 70.4 on WikiSQL, \(W_q, W_v\) at \(r=4\) gives 73.7, all four attention matrices at \(r=2\) give 73.7 [Hu et al. 2021, Table 5]. Later work extends this to the MLP. QLoRA finds that LoRA on all linear layers is required to match full finetuning of LLaMA-7B on Alpaca [Dettmers et al. 2023, Fig. 2]. Thinking Machines find attention-only LoRA underperforms MLP-only even at matched parameters (attention rank 256 loses to MLP rank 128 at about the same parameter count) and that adding attention to MLP-only gives no further gain [Schulman & Thinking Machines 2025]. Biderman et al. also locate most of the gain in the MLP modules. A plausible reason, offered by Thinking Machines via the empirical NTK, is that the matrices with the most parameters dominate the kernel. In Llama-3-8B the MLP holds \(176{,}160{,}768\) of the \(218{,}103{,}808\) linear parameters per layer, 80.8%.

Rank. Hu et al. find \(r = 1\) already competitive on GPT-3 for \(W_q, W_v\) (WikiSQL 73.4 at \(r=1\) against 73.5 at \(r=64\), Table 6), while QLoRA reports that \(r\) does not affect results on their instruction-tuning tasks. Neither holds on harder targets: Biderman et al. find code instruction tuning is ordered by rank (HumanEval 0.358, 0.417, 0.498 at \(r = 16, 64, 256\), epoch 4). Thinking Machines see lower-rank adapters fall off the best loss curve at a step count that grows with rank, "when the adapter runs out of capacity". The rank you need scales with how much you are teaching, not with model width.

Learning rate. LoRA's best rate is about 10× the full-finetuning rate: Biderman et al. report "one order of magnitude higher", typically \(5\times 10^{-5}\) to \(5\times10^{-4}\) for Llama-2-7B, and Thinking Machines fit a multiplier of 9.8 across 14 Llama and Qwen models. Documented recipes:

Merging

Because \(\Delta W\) has the shape of \(W_0\), merging 权重合并 is possible: deployment can compute \(W = W_0 + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BA\) once and serve the ordinary model, with no added latency "by construction". Switching tasks is a subtraction and an addition [Hu et al. 2021, §4.1]. Adapters cannot do this because of the nonlinearity between their projections: on GPT-2 medium at batch size 1 and sequence length 128 they add 20.7% and 30.3% latency for the two variants tested [Hu et al. 2021, Table 1]. Three caveats:

  • One full copy per adapter. A merged model gives up sharing the base across adapters and the option of batching requests for different adapters through one weight matrix (Section 6).
  • Precision. Do the addition in fp32 and round once. bf16 keeps 8 significant bits, so rounding \(W_0 + \Delta W\) into bf16 perturbs an entry by up to about 0.2 to 0.4% of its own size. When \(\|\Delta W\|_F / \|W_0\|_F\) is 6 to 11% (3.57/61.95 and 6.91/61.95 for one GPT-3 layer, Hu et al. Table 7) that is a few percent of the update: harmless but not zero.
  • A quantized base. Merging into 4-bit weights is much worse: see below.
import math
import torch
from torch import nn

class LoRALinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, base: nn.Linear, r: int, alpha: float):
        super().__init__()
        self.base, self.scaling, self.merged = base, alpha / r, False
        for p in base.parameters():
            p.requires_grad_(False)                       # W0 (and bias) never train
        d_out, d_in = base.weight.shape                   # PyTorch layout: (d_out, d_in)
        bound = 1 / math.sqrt(d_in)
        self.lora_A = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(r, d_in).uniform_(-bound, bound))  # random
        self.lora_B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d_out, r))                         # zero: delta W = 0 at step 0

    def forward(self, x):                                 # x: (..., d_in)
        y = self.base(x)                                  # (..., d_out)
        if self.merged:
            return y
        return y + self.scaling * ((x @ self.lora_A.T) @ self.lora_B.T)          # (..., r), then (..., d_out)

    @torch.no_grad()
    def merge(self):                                      # W = W0 + (alpha / r) B A, same shape as W0
        if not self.merged:
            self.base.weight += self.scaling * (self.lora_B @ self.lora_A)
            self.merged = True

Compute. Per weight matrix, full finetuning does three matmuls (forward, gradient to the input, gradient to the weight), each \(N^2\) multiply-adds for an \(N \times N\) matrix. LoRA still does the first two through \(W_0\) and replaces the third with the much smaller adapter products: \(2N^2 + 6NR\) against \(3N^2\), "slightly more than ⅔" [Schulman & Thinking Machines 2025]. This ignores attention FLOPs, and Biderman et al. measured that with standard implementations at a fixed batch size LoRA can train slower per step than full finetuning (their App. I), so treat the 0.67 as a FLOP count, not a wall-clock promise.

LoRA
Problem
Full finetuning costs 16 bytes per parameter of training state and produces a full-size checkpoint per task.
Idea
Freeze \(W_0\) and learn \(\Delta W = \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BA\) with \(A\) random and \(B = 0\).
Why it works
Post-training updates are small relative to the pretrained function and, for typical instruction or reasoning data, fit in a low-rank subspace (intrinsic-dimension evidence). \(B = 0\) starts at the pretrained model, and the linear form can be merged into the weights.
Evidence
GPT-3 175B: 10,000× fewer trainable parameters, with WikiSQL and MNLI accuracy at or above full finetuning (73.8 and 91.6 at \(r_q = r_v = 8\), against 73.8 and 89.5) [Hu et al. 2021, Abstract, Table 15]. RoBERTa-large on GLUE: 89.0 average with 0.8M parameters against 88.9 with 355M [Table 2].
Cost
Limited capacity (rank), sensitivity to \(\alpha\), learning rate and target modules, worse tolerance of large batches [Schulman & Thinking Machines 2025], activations unchanged, and a clear gap when the data far exceeds adapter capacity (Section 5).
Used by
The default PEFT method in the Hugging Face PEFT library; multi-tenant serving in vLLM and SGLang, per Thinking Machines; the base of QLoRA and of the LoRA variants below.

QLoRA: a 4-bit frozen base

The idea

LoRA makes the trainable states small but leaves the frozen base at 2 bytes per weight: 16 GB for Llama-3-8B, 141 GB for Llama-3-70B. The base is only read, never updated, so its precision matters only through the error it adds to the forward and backward passes, and the adapters can learn to absorb some of that error. QLoRA stores the base in 4 bits, dequantizes each weight matrix to bf16 just before its matmul, and keeps adapters and all arithmetic in bf16 [Dettmers et al. 2023, §3, Eq. 5]:

\[ Y = X\,\text{dq}\big(W^{\text{NF4}}\big)^{\!\top} + \gamma\,(XA^\top)B^\top \qquad (\text{all in bf16}) \]

In the backward pass \(\partial L/\partial X\) flows through the dequantized \(W\), so earlier layers still get gradients, but no weight gradient is computed for the 4-bit weights. There is one storage type (4-bit) and one compute type (bf16). Three techniques make it work.

Block-wise quantization and the NormalFloat grid

Block-wise absmax quantization 分块量化. Flatten the weights and cut them into blocks of 64. For each block store its absolute maximum \(c = \max|w|\), divide the block by \(c\) so it lies in \([-1, 1]\), and replace each value by the index of the nearest of 16 grid levels. A single outlier would otherwise stretch the scale of a whole tensor and waste most codes. Blocks confine its damage to 63 neighbors, at the price of one FP32 constant per block: \(32/64 = 0.5\) bits per parameter [Dettmers et al. 2023, §3].

NormalFloat-4 (NF4). A uniform 16-level grid wastes codes, because trained weights are close to zero-centered normal (the paper's Shapiro-Wilk test on LLaMA-7B finds 7.5% of hidden units non-normal against 5% expected by chance, App. F). Quantile quantization places the levels so each bin has equal probability mass under \(\mathcal N(0,1)\), then rescales to \([-1, 1]\). The paper builds an asymmetric grid so that 0 is exactly representable (padding and zeros quantize without error): quantiles for the negative half (including 0) and the positive half (including 0), one duplicate zero removed, giving 7 negative levels, 0, and 8 positive levels. Round-to-nearest therefore has worst-case error half the largest gap, \(0.1519 \times c\), in a block with absmax \(c\).

Does the normal grid actually win? The paper's evidence is empirical: mean Pile-CC perplexity over 125M to 13B OPT, BLOOM, LLaMA and Pythia models is 34.34 for Int4, 31.07 and 29.48 for two FP4 layouts, and 27.41 for NF4 with double quantization [Dettmers et al. 2023, Table 2]. In my own check on \(\mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)\) weights with block 64, the relative error \(\|w - \hat w\|/\|w\|\) is 9.2% for NF4, 10.0% for a uniform 16-level grid, and 10.8% for symmetric int4. One caveat: "information-theoretically optimal" holds for exactly normal data with known variance, while a block of 64 samples its own absmax (about \(2.6\sigma\) in my simulation), so after absmax normalization the grid is not exactly optimal. The perplexity table is the real justification.

Double quantization

Double quantization 双重量化 quantizes the quantization constants. The block scales \(c_2\) (FP32, one per 64 weights) become 8-bit values in groups of 256, with one FP32 second-level constant \(c_1\) per group. The scales are all positive, so the paper subtracts their mean first to use a symmetric code. The cost per weight:

\[ \underbrace{4}_{\text{code}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{8}{64}}_{c_2\ \text{(8-bit)}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{32}{64 \cdot 256}}_{c_1\ \text{(FP32)}} = 4.127\ \text{bits} \quad\text{versus}\quad 4 + \tfrac{32}{64} = 4.5 \text{ without.} \]

The saving is 0.373 bits per parameter, or \(65.29 \times 10^9 \times 0.373 / 8 = 3.04\) GB for a 65B model ("approximately 3 GB" in the paper). Per 16,384 weights the storage is \(65{,}536 + 2{,}048 + 32 = 67{,}616\) bits, and \(67{,}616 / 16{,}384 = 4.127\) exactly.

Paged optimizers

Paged optimizers 分页优化器 use NVIDIA unified memory: optimizer state is evicted to CPU RAM when the GPU runs out of memory and paged back for the update. They target transient spikes, such as a long sequence under gradient checkpointing, not the average footprint. The paper gives no hard measurement of paging cost, only that a 65B model on a 48 GB GPU at batch size 16 trains at the same speed as with a regular optimizer [Dettmers et al. 2023, §4].

Worked numbers: NF4 memory for a 70B model, and the 65B claim

Evidence for QLoRA, and how far it goes

On RoBERTa-large GLUE and T5 80M to 11B on Super-NaturalInstructions, 4-bit NF4 QLoRA matches 16-bit LoRA and full finetuning (RoBERTa-large 88.6 for the BF16 baseline, 88.8 for BF16 LoRA, 88.6 for FP4 QLoRA; Table 3). On LLaMA 7B to 65B finetuned on Alpaca and FLAN v2, mean 5-shot MMLU is 53.0 for BF16 LoRA, 52.2 for FP4 and 53.1 for NF4 with double quantization (Table 4; differences of a few tenths are inside evaluation noise, which the paper does not quantify). The headline Guanaco 65B reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark with a 95% interval of ±4.4, judged by GPT-4 on 80 prompts (Table 6). Read these as "parity with 16-bit LoRA on these benchmarks". Independent evidence is less uniform: Biderman et al. cite Ivison et al. finding QLoRA below full finetuning for Llama-2 7B, 13B and 70B on Tülu-v2-mix [Biderman et al. 2024, §5].

Can you merge into a quantized base?

Not without paying. The merged weight \(\hat W + \gamma BA\) is not on the NF4 grid, so a 4-bit model must re-quantize, and the rounding step is large compared with the update. The smallest NF4 step is \(0.0796\) of the block absmax, about \(0.2\sigma\) for a block of Gaussian weights, while real updates are small: \(\|\Delta W\|_F / \|W\|_F\) was 6 to 11% in the one GPT-3 layer Hu et al. analyzed, so a typical entry of \(\Delta W\) is a fraction of one grid step. A toy simulation makes this concrete (\(W \sim \mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)\), \(4096 \times 4096\), NF4 block 64, \(\Delta W\) a random rank-16 matrix with \(\|\Delta W\|_F = \rho \|W\|_F\); I compare \(Q(\hat W + \Delta W) - \hat W\) with \(\Delta W\)):

\(\rho\)Fraction of codes that changeCosine(recovered update, true update)

Real updates are structured, not random, so the numbers will differ, but the mechanism is general: a small update is mostly rounded away. The options are: keep the adapter separate at inference, as in training (bf16 branch next to the NF4 matmul); dequantize, merge in 16 bits and serve a 16-bit model; or merge and re-quantize with a calibration-based method, then re-evaluate (page 17). There is a second issue. The adapter was trained against the NF4 rounding error and may partly compensate for it, so merging it into the original unquantized weights is a slightly different function from the one that was trained. In all three cases, evaluate the merged model; do not assume it equals the trained one.

Prefix and prompt tuning: why they fell out of favor

Prefix tuning 前缀微调 prepends trainable key/value vectors at every layer; prompt tuning 提示微调 trains soft token embeddings at the input only. Both keep the model untouched and allow mixed-task batches (Li & Liang §8.2; Lester Fig. 2). Prompt tuning "closes the gap" with model tuning only at scale, matching it at T5-XXL (11B) on SuperGLUE [Lester et al. 2021, Fig. 1]. The evidence in the sources points to four reasons they lost to LoRA:

  1. Sequence budget. They "reduce the model's usable sequence length" [Hu et al. 2021, §1] and cost attention compute at every token.
  2. Scale dependence. Below billions of parameters they lag (Lester), and the benefit of a good initialization also disappears only at XXL.
  3. Optimization fragility. Lester used a learning rate of 0.3 and found class-label initialization clearly best at small sizes; Li & Liang needed an MLP reparametrization because directly updating the prefix "leads to unstable optimization and a slight drop in performance" (§4.3); the (IA)³ authors "were unable to attain reasonable accuracy with prompt tuning" on T0-3B few-shot, seeing validation performance fluctuate wildly; Hu et al. find prefix accuracy non-monotonic in parameter count (WikiSQL 63.1 at 3.2M parameters, 55.9 at 6.4M, their Table 15).
  4. No merge. The prefix stays in the context at inference.

They keep two genuine strengths: cheap mixed-task batching, and, for prompt tuning, better zero-shot transfer under domain shift (Lester §5). Both matter less once LoRA serving is batched (Section 6).

(IA)³

(IA)³ learns three vectors per layer, \(l_k\), \(l_v\), \(l_{ff}\), that rescale keys, values and the FFN's inner activations elementwise. They start at 1, so the model is unchanged at step 0. Since \(l \odot (Wx) = (l \odot W)x\), a single-task (IA)³ folds into the weights at no cost, and mixed-task batches are cheap because each sequence multiplies by its own vector. In the T-Few few-shot recipe on T0 (11B) it stores 4.2 MB per task and reached 75.8% on RAFT against a 73.5% human baseline [Liu et al. 2022, Tables 1-2]. Its capacity is far below LoRA's, and the paper evaluates it only on classification and multiple-choice tasks, listing generative tasks as future work.

DoRA: magnitude and direction

DoRA decomposes each weight into a magnitude and a direction, \(W = m\, V/\|V\|_c\), initializes \(m = \|W_0\|_c\) and \(V = W_0\), keeps \(V\) frozen apart from a LoRA update, and trains \(m\):

\[ W' = m\,\frac{W_0 + BA}{\|W_0 + BA\|_c} \]

Motivation: on VL-BART query matrices, the changes in magnitude \(\Delta M\) and direction \(\Delta D\) are negatively correlated in full finetuning (−0.62) and positively correlated in LoRA (+0.83); DoRA is at −0.31. The authors read this as LoRA tying the two changes together proportionally, which full finetuning does not do [Liu et al. 2024, §3.2, §4.1]. Results: commonsense-reasoning average 74.7 for LoRA against 78.4 for DoRA on LLaMA-7B (+3.7), and 80.8 against 85.2 on LLaMA3-8B (+4.4); with the rank halved, DoRA is still ahead on LLaMA-7B (77.5) [Table 1]. Costs: the weight norm of \(W_0 + \gamma BA\) must be recomputed every step, which means materializing that matrix; treating the norm as a constant in the backward pass cuts training memory 24.4% (37.3 to 28.2 GB on LLaMA-7B) at a 0.2-point accuracy cost [Table 7]. It merges like LoRA. Two caveats. The gains are the authors' runs on one benchmark suite, with the LoRA baseline hyperparameters taken from earlier work. And the paper's \(\|\cdot\|_c\) is a column norm of \(W \in \R^{d \times k}\), while Hugging Face PEFT computes torch.linalg.norm(weight, dim=1) on the \((d_{\text{out}}, d_{\text{in}})\) weight, one magnitude per output unit.

LoRA+ and rsLoRA: optimization variants

LoRA+ sets \(\eta_B = \lambda\,\eta_A\) with \(\lambda \gg 1\). The argument is an infinite-width analysis: with equal learning rates for \(A\) and \(B\), feature learning cannot be efficient, but \(\eta_A = \Theta(n^{-1})\) and \(\eta_B = \Theta(1)\) is (Theorem 1, informal). The constant is not derived, so \(\lambda\) is tuned: \(2^4\) worked for RoBERTa with \(A\) zero and \(B\) random, \(2^2\) to \(2^3\) with the standard initialization, and \(2^1\) to \(2^2\) for Llama-7B. The reported gains are 1 to 2% and up to 2× faster convergence [Hayou et al. 2024, Abstract, §5, Fig. 7]. The gain is larger on harder tasks (MNLI, QQP, MMLU +1.3% for Llama-7B on Flan-v2) and negligible on easy ones (Llama on MNLI). rsLoRA replaces \(\alpha/r\) with \(\alpha/\sqrt{r}\) (Section 2). Thinking Machines point out that both are reparametrizations of the same two-dimensional hyperparameter space: raising \(\text{LR}_B\) is equivalent to raising \(\text{init}_A/\text{LR}_A\), and "use a larger \(\alpha\) at high rank" is the same move. They report they "were unable to improve on" plain LoRA hyperparameters in their sweeps [Schulman & Thinking Machines 2025]. Other variants exist: AdaLoRA allocates rank per matrix, and PiSSA initializes the adapter from the top singular directions of \(W_0\). Shuttleworth et al. find intruder dimensions (below) in all of LoRA+, PiSSA, VeRA and AdaLoRA.

The study is Llama-2-7B only, and other work finds mixed results (the same paper cites Astraios, where rank-8 LoRA trailed full finetuning on code tasks across model sizes to 16B).

"Forgets less" is conditional

The forgetting in question is catastrophic forgetting 灾难性遗忘: loss of pretrained abilities during finetuning. Shuttleworth et al. compare the SVD of finetuned weights. LoRA updates introduce intruder dimensions: high-ranking singular vectors nearly orthogonal to every pretrained singular vector, which full finetuning does not produce. Scaling those singular values down after training lowers forgetting of the pretraining distribution with little loss on the finetuning task, so they cause forgetting. Consequences: LoRA usually forgets less, but with \(\alpha = 8\) instead of \(2r\), or with large learning rates, it can forget more than full finetuning; and when RoBERTa-base is finetuned sequentially on six tasks LoRA accumulates intruder dimensions and degrades faster than full finetuning. Very high rank (\(r = 2048\)) behaves like full finetuning, with none [Shuttleworth et al. 2025, §3-5].

"LoRA Without Regret"

Thinking Machines (a 2025 blog post by John Schulman and colleagues, not peer reviewed) ask under what conditions LoRA matches full finetuning, sweeping rank from 1 to 512 and the learning rate for every condition, on Llama-3 and Qwen3 models with Tülu3 and OpenThoughts3 for supervised learning and math tasks for RL. Their conclusions [Schulman & Thinking Machines 2025]:

  • For small to medium supervised datasets, LoRA matches full finetuning in sample efficiency and final loss, provided it is applied to all layers (MLP essential) and is not capacity-limited. For datasets beyond LoRA's capacity it underperforms, and the loss does not hit a hard floor but trains less efficiently.
  • LoRA is less tolerant of large batch sizes than full finetuning, and the gap does not depend on rank, so they attribute it to the product parametrization \(BA\).
  • For RL, LoRA matches full finetuning "even with ranks as low as 1" (MATH and GSM8K on Llama-3.1-8B; DeepMath on Qwen3-8B-base, where learning curves, AIME scores and chain-of-thought lengths track full finetuning).

The RL argument is an information count. Supervised learning gives \(O(\text{tokens})\) bits per example, but a policy-gradient episode gives one scalar advantage, so \(O(1)\) bits (their mutual-information bound \(I(G;R) \le H(\text{Adv}) \lesssim \log B\) for \(B\) advantage bins). For MATH they train on about 10,000 problems with 32 samples each: at one bit per completion, 320,000 bits. A rank-1 LoRA on all linear layers of Llama-3-8B has 2,621,440 parameters by my count (they say 3M), about 8 times more. DeepSeek-R1-Zero's 10,400 steps of 32 questions with 16 samples each is 5.3M episodes, which they predict rank-1 LoRA could absorb; that is a prediction, not an experiment. Their caveats: "1 bit per episode" holds for policy-gradient methods with a scalar reward, not for every RL algorithm (model-based methods could extract more); the 1 bit/token estimate for supervised data is an over-estimate of what generalizable learning needs, and how capacity interacts with parameters is listed as an open question.

From your jet tagger

The same information count applies to your training set. A binary tagging label is at most 1 bit per jet, so 107 labeled jets carry at most 107 bits of supervision, far fewer than the parameters of a modest transformer. LLM supervised finetuning supervises every token (about 1 bit each), and policy-gradient RL supervises a whole episode with about 1 bit. That is why RL is the regime where a tiny adapter suffices, and why 20B tokens of continued pretraining (about 20 Gbit) is not.

Serving many adapters

One merged copy per customer means one full copy of the base model per customer. Unmerged, an adapter for Llama-3-8B at \(r = 16\) is 41,943,040 parameters, 83.9 MB in bf16, so 1,000 of them are 83.9 GB and fit in host RAM next to a single 16 GB base. Serving them unmerged costs extra FLOPs, \(2 \times 41.9\)M against \(2 \times 8.03\)B per token (0.52%), but decoding is memory-bound: each distinct adapter in a batch has to be read separately, while the base weights are read once for the whole batch. With 32 distinct adapters in a batch that is \(32 \times 83.9\) MB = 2.68 GB against 16.06 GB of base weights, 16.7% more bytes per step.

S-LoRA keeps all adapters in host memory, fetches those needed by the running batch, and computes \(xW_0\) as one batched matmul plus \(xAB\) per adapter with custom kernels for mixed ranks and lengths. Its Unified Paging exploits that an adapter matrix of rank \(R\) has shape \((R, H)\) and a KV cache of length \(S\) has shape \((S, H)\), so both fit one pool of pages of \(H\) elements and fragmentation drops. On Llama-7B (rank 8) it serves 2,000 adapters on one A100 80GB at 7.61 requests per second against 8.05 with 5 adapters, while vLLM with merged copies manages 5 adapters at 2.04 requests per second and runs out of memory at 100; the abstract's headline is up to 4× the throughput of vLLM and up to 30× that of Hugging Face PEFT [Sheng et al. 2023, Table 3, Abstract]. Punica contributes the SGMV kernel (segmented gather matrix-vector multiplication): requests are grouped by adapter, and \(y \mathrel{+}= xAB\) runs as two launches ("shrink" to rank \(r\), "expand" back). With every request on a different adapter it is a memory-bound matrix-vector product; the paper reports 12× the throughput of existing systems at +2 ms per token [Chen et al. 2023, Abstract, §4]. Note the flip side of merging: the LoRA paper itself says batching inputs for different tasks is not straightforward once the adapters are merged [Hu et al. 2021, §4.2].

Common misconceptions

  • "LoRA makes finetuning use far less memory in every respect." It removes gradients, master weights and Adam states for frozen weights. The frozen weights (2 bytes per parameter) and the activations remain; for a 7B model at batch size 1 the LoRA input gradients (567 MB) outweigh the adapter (26 MB).
  • "Initialize \(A\) and \(B\) both randomly (or both to zero)." Both random starts from a perturbed model; both zero has zero gradient everywhere. One is random, the other zero, and at step 0 only \(B\) receives gradient.
  • "\(\alpha\) is an independent knob from the learning rate." With Adam it is redundant with the learning rate and the initialization scale (two of the four hyperparameters are free). Its job in \(\alpha/r\) is to keep the best learning rate roughly rank-independent.
  • "Higher rank always means better." Rank buys capacity that matters only when data exceeds it. On small SFT sets ranks from 1 to 64 look the same, and with \(\gamma = \alpha/r\) and a fixed learning rate a higher rank may not even learn faster.
  • "NF4 is 4 bits per weight." It is 4.127 bits with double quantization and 4.5 without, and the embeddings and head usually stay in bf16.
  • "You can merge a QLoRA adapter into the 4-bit model for free." The merged weights fall off the NF4 grid; re-quantizing rounds away much of a small update.
  • "LoRA always matches full finetuning" or "LoRA never does." It matches on small to medium SFT and, per one report, on RL; it loses on continued pretraining and other capacity-limited settings.
  • "LoRA forgets less, so it is safe for sequential finetuning." Forgetting depends on \(\alpha\), learning rate and rank, and intruder dimensions accumulate across tasks.

Interview questions

Warm-up What does LoRA add to a frozen linear layer, and how many parameters is that for a 4096 × 4096 matrix at rank 16?

Two matrices, \(A \in \R^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}\) and \(B \in \R^{d_{\text{out}} \times r}\), in parallel with the frozen \(W_0\): \(h = W_0 x + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BAx\). The update has rank at most \(r\). Parameters: \(r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}}) = 16 \times 8192 = 131{,}072\), against \(16{,}777{,}216\) for the matrix, so 0.78%. \(B\) starts at 0 so the model begins at the pretrained function, and since the update is linear it can be merged into \(W_0\) for serving.

Warm-up Where does LoRA's memory saving come from, and what does it not reduce?

Full finetuning carries 16 bytes per parameter (bf16 weight and gradient, fp32 master copy, two fp32 Adam moments). LoRA needs the gradient, master copy and Adam states only for the adapter. For Llama-3-8B that is 128.5 GB of states against 16.06 GB of frozen bf16 weights plus 0.67 GB of adapter states, 16.7 GB total.

It does not reduce the frozen weights (2 bytes per parameter), the activations (backpropagation still passes through all layers, so long sequences still need checkpointing), or most of the compute: the forward and input-gradient matmuls through \(W_0\) remain, so FLOPs drop to roughly two thirds, not to a few percent. The QLoRA paper measures 567 MB of LoRA input gradients against 26 MB of adapter for a 7B model at batch size 1.

Warm-up What is NF4, and what does double quantization save?

NF4 is a 16-level code whose levels are quantiles of a standard normal, rescaled to \([-1, 1]\), with an exact zero (7 negative levels, 0, 8 positive). Weights are quantized in blocks of 64: divide by the block's absmax, store the index of the nearest level, and store the absmax. Normally distributed weights cluster near zero, so equal-probability bins beat a uniform grid; the paper measures perplexity 27.41 for NF4+DQ against 29.48 to 34.34 for FP4 and Int4.

The per-block FP32 scale costs \(32/64 = 0.5\) bits per weight. Double quantization stores the scales as 8-bit values in groups of 256 with one FP32 constant per group: \(8/64 + 32/(64 \cdot 256) = 0.127\) bits. Total 4.127 bits per weight instead of 4.5, saving 0.373 bits, about 3 GB at 65B.

Core Implement a LoRA linear layer. Why initialize \(B\) to zero?

Freeze the base layer; create lora_A of shape \((r, d_{\text{in}})\), uniform on \(\pm 1/\sqrt{d_{\text{in}}}\), and lora_B of shape \((d_{\text{out}}, r)\), zeros; scale \(\alpha/r\). Forward: base(x) + scale * (x @ A.T) @ B.T, two skinny matmuls, never forming \(BA\) (that would cost \(d_{\text{out}} d_{\text{in}} r\) and defeat the purpose). Merge: W += scale * B @ A; unmerge subtracts it.

Why \(B = 0\): (1) \(\Delta W = 0\) at step 0, so training starts at the pretrained function. (2) The gradients are \(\partial L/\partial B = \gamma g (Ax)^\top\) and \(\partial L/\partial A = \gamma B^\top g x^\top\), so if both were zero nothing would ever move (a saddle); with \(A\) random and \(B = 0\), \(B\) gets a nonzero gradient immediately and \(A\) starts learning once \(B\) is nonzero. (3) Random \(A\) with variance \(\sim 1/d_{\text{in}}\) gives \(Ax\) entries of order 1 independent of width. Zero \(A\) and random \(B\) is also workable (LoRA+ analyzes both); the convention is a choice, not a law.

Core What does \(\alpha/r\) do? What happens if you change \(r\) but not \(\alpha\)?

The adapter output is multiplied by \(\gamma = \alpha/r\). \(BA = \sum_{i=1}^r b_i a_i^\top\) sums \(r\) terms whose expected first update does not depend on \(r\), so dividing by \(r\) keeps the initial change in function independent of rank. With \(\alpha\) fixed, learning curves for different ranks coincide at the start and the best learning rate moves by less than 2× from \(r = 4\) to 512 (Thinking Machines). So raising \(r\) at fixed \(\alpha\) does not make the adapter learn faster, it adds capacity that pays off later, and you can keep your learning rate.

Changing \(\alpha\) at fixed \(r\) rescales the update, equivalent to a learning-rate change: with Adam (\(\epsilon = 0\)) training is invariant under \(\alpha \to \alpha/(pq)\), \(\text{init}_A \to p\,\text{init}_A\), \(\text{LR}_A \to p\,\text{LR}_A\), \(\text{LR}_B \to q\,\text{LR}_B\), leaving two effective hyperparameters. Alternatives: rsLoRA's \(\alpha/\sqrt{r}\) (stable as \(r \to \infty\), higher rank helps at a fixed learning rate), and Biderman et al.'s \(\alpha = 2r\) (constant \(\gamma\) = 2). Whichever you use, re-sweep the learning rate when changing \(r\) unless \(\gamma \propto 1/r\).

Core Calculate the trainable parameters and training-state memory for LoRA \(r = 16\) on all linear layers of Llama-3-8B, against full finetuning.

Per layer, \(r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}})\) summed over q (4096→4096), k and v (4096→1024), o (4096→4096), gate and up (4096→14336), down (14336→4096): \(16 \times (8192 + 5120 + 5120 + 8192 + 2 \times 18432 + 18432) = 16 \times 81{,}920 = 1{,}310{,}720\). Times 32 layers: 41,943,040, which is 0.52% of 8,030,261,248.

Full finetuning at 16 B/parameter: 128.48 GB. LoRA: bf16 frozen base 16.06 GB plus adapter states \(41{,}943{,}040 \times 16\) B = 0.67 GB, total 16.73 GB, 7.7× less. QLoRA with an NF4+DQ base (4.127 bits on 6.98B linear parameters = 3.60 GB, plus 2.10 GB of bf16 embeddings and head): 6.37 GB. Activations are extra and unchanged by LoRA.

Core How does QLoRA fit a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU?

Regular 16-bit finetuning needs over 780 GB (65.2B × 12 bytes). QLoRA changes three things. (1) The frozen base goes to NF4 with blocks of 64 and double-quantized scales, 4.127 bits per weight: about 33.4 GB for the linear layers plus 1.05 GB of bf16 embeddings and head, 34.5 GB. (2) Only LoRA adapters (all linear layers, \(r = 64\), about 0.8B parameters, 12 bytes each with bf16 weights and gradients and fp32 Adam states, 9.6 GB) are trained, in bf16 after dequantizing each weight on the fly. (3) Paged optimizers move optimizer state to CPU RAM when a long sequence under gradient checkpointing spikes memory. The sum is 44 GB by my arithmetic and 45.0 GB in the paper's Fig. 6 at batch size 1 and sequence length 512.

Caveats: 3 GB of headroom at batch 1; the paper shows parity with 16-bit LoRA, not with full finetuning, at 33B and 65B; and dequantization adds work to every matmul.

Core Can you merge LoRA weights? What about with a quantized base?

Yes for a 16-bit base: \(W = W_0 + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BA\) has the shape of \(W_0\), so inference has no extra latency, and unmerging is a subtraction. Do the addition in fp32 and round once; bf16 rounding perturbs weights by up to about 0.2 to 0.4% of their magnitude, a few percent of a typical update. Merging costs you multi-adapter batching, since each merged model is a separate weight set.

Not cleanly for an NF4 base. The merged weight is off the 4-bit grid; re-quantizing with a smallest step of about \(0.2\sigma\) rounds away much of an update whose entries are a fraction of a step (in my toy simulation, only 2.4% of codes change and the cosine between recovered and true update is 0.35 at \(\|\Delta W\|/\|W\| = 5\%\)). Options: keep the adapter separate, dequantize and merge into 16 bits, or merge then re-quantize with a calibrated method and re-evaluate. The adapter was also trained against the NF4 rounding error, so merging into the original unquantized weights changes the function slightly.

Core When does LoRA underperform full finetuning, and what would you change first?

When the data exceeds adapter capacity or the domain shift is large: continued pretraining, code and other domains far from pretraining. Biderman et al. (Llama-2-7B): code CPT at 20B tokens HumanEval 0.263 full against 0.224 for the best LoRA (\(r = 256\)); code instruction data at epoch 4, 0.358 and 0.417 for \(r = 16, 64\) against 0.497 for full. The full-finetuning \(\Delta W\) there needs rank 10 to 100× higher than typical LoRA ranks. Thinking Machines see the same capacity effect: low-rank adapters fall off the loss curve when they run out of room, and LoRA also degrades at large batch sizes.

What to change, in order: (1) target all linear layers, especially the MLP (attention-only underperforms even at matched parameters); (2) raise the rank if the data warrants it (capacity check: rank 16 is about 80 Mbit at 2 bits per parameter, code instruction data about 290 Mbit); (3) use \(\alpha = 2r\) or re-sweep the learning rate (about 10× the full-finetuning value); (4) reduce batch size; (5) if it is really continued pretraining, use full finetuning.

Deep-dive Design question: a service hosts 1,000 customer-specific finetunes of Llama-3-8B. How do you train, store and serve them?

Training: LoRA on all linear layers at rank 16 to 64 (QLoRA if the training GPUs are small), learning rate about 10× full-finetuning rate, sweep it per dataset type. Each run needs about 17 GB of states rather than 128 GB, so one GPU per job.

Storage: adapters only: 83.9 MB each in bf16 at \(r = 16\) (41.9M parameters), 84 GB for 1,000, against 16 TB as full copies.

Serving: do not merge. Keep one base model resident and adapters in host RAM, fetch those in the running batch, and use a batched-adapter kernel (S-LoRA's unified paging with custom kernels, or Punica's SGMV). Cost: 0.52% extra FLOPs, but 16.7% extra bytes per decode step if 32 distinct adapters share a batch, since each adapter is read separately. Cluster requests by adapter where latency allows. Merge only for a customer whose traffic justifies a dedicated replica. Evaluate per customer: LoRA can lose on large or out-of-domain data, and isolation (no cross-tenant weights) comes for free.

Deep-dive Biderman et al. say LoRA learns less and forgets less; Thinking Machines say LoRA matches full finetuning in RL; Shuttleworth et al. say LoRA forgets in its own way. Reconcile these.

Capacity versus information. Biderman et al. train on billions of tokens of continued pretraining or hundreds of millions of instruction tokens: at about 1 bit per token that is more information than a rank-16 to 256 adapter stores (1.28 Gbit at 2 bits per parameter for rank 256), so LoRA "learns less". Thinking Machines' RL runs supply about 1 bit per episode: 320,000 bits for their MATH run, less than the 2.6M parameters of a rank-1 adapter, so capacity is not binding. Both are consistent with one rule: LoRA matches full finetuning until the information in the data exceeds adapter capacity. Both also agree LoRA underperforms in pretraining-like regimes.

Forgetting. Constraining the update to low rank keeps the model closer to the base, so LoRA forgets less (Biderman et al.), even at equal fit on the target task (Shuttleworth et al.). But the constraint is not the whole story: high-ranking intruder dimensions, nearly orthogonal to the pretrained singular vectors, cause forgetting, and they appear more with \(\alpha = 8\) than \(2r\), with high learning rates, and across sequential tasks, where LoRA can degrade faster than full finetuning. Prescriptions: \(\alpha = 2r\), moderate learning rate, higher rank, and separate rather than sequentially merged adapters.

Caveats on the evidence: Thinking Machines is a blog post, RL only with policy gradient and scalar rewards, and the R1-Zero case is a prediction. Biderman et al. use Llama-2-7B, LionW and analyze the spectrum only for continued pretraining. Each finding is one setup.

Deep-dive DoRA, LoRA+ and rsLoRA each claim to improve on LoRA. What does each change, and how would you decide whether any is worth it?

DoRA changes the parametrization: \(W' = m(W_0 + BA)/\|W_0 + BA\|_c\), a trainable magnitude plus a LoRA direction. It targets the observation that LoRA ties magnitude and direction changes together (correlation +0.83 against −0.62 for full finetuning). It reports +3.7 to +4.4 points on commonsense reasoning for LLaMA-7B and LLaMA3-8B at a cost of recomputing the weight norm every step. LoRA+ changes the optimization: \(\eta_B = \lambda \eta_A\) with \(\lambda \gg 1\), from an infinite-width argument that equal rates cannot learn features efficiently; 1 to 2% and up to 2× faster on harder tasks. rsLoRA changes the scale: \(\alpha/\sqrt{r}\), so higher ranks learn at a fixed learning rate.

Decision: LoRA+ and rsLoRA are reparametrizations of the same two-dimensional space (\(\alpha\), init, \(\text{LR}_A\), \(\text{LR}_B\) with two redundancies), so first check whether a well-swept plain LoRA (learning rate, \(\alpha\), rank, all modules) closes the gap; Thinking Machines could not improve on it. DoRA changes the function class and is the one worth an ablation, but its evidence is the authors' runs on one suite with baseline hyperparameters from earlier work, and it costs memory. Run the same sweep on both with your data, compare at matched trainable parameters and matched tuning budget, and check forgetting as well as target accuracy.

Cheat sheet

  • LoRA: \(h = W_0 x + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BAx\), \(A \in \R^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}\) random (uniform \(\pm 1/\sqrt{d_{\text{in}}}\)), \(B \in \R^{d_{\text{out}} \times r}\) zero. Params \(r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}})\) per matrix. At step 0 \(\partial L/\partial A = 0\), only \(B\) moves.
  • Llama-3-8B, \(r = 16\), all 7 linear matrices: 1,310,720 per layer, 41,943,040 total (0.52%). \(q, v\) only: 6,815,744.
  • Training state: full finetuning 16 B/param (128.48 GB for 8B). LoRA: 16.06 GB frozen bf16 + 16 B per adapter param (0.67 GB) = 16.73 GB. QLoRA: 5.70 GB base + 0.67 = 6.37 GB. Activations extra, unchanged.
  • \(\alpha/r\): fixed \(\alpha\) keeps the best LR nearly rank-independent (Adam). With Adam, \((\alpha, \text{init}_A, \text{LR}_A, \text{LR}_B)\) has two redundancies. rsLoRA \(\alpha/\sqrt{r}\); Biderman \(\alpha = 2r\); QLoRA \(r=64, \alpha=16\).
  • Recipe: all linear layers (MLP is 81% of linear params), LR about 10× full-finetuning (\(5\times10^{-5}\) to \(5\times10^{-4}\)), rank 16 to 64 for SFT, 256 for hard domains, re-sweep LR when \(\gamma \not\propto 1/r\).
  • NF4: 16 normal-quantile levels, exact 0, blocks of 64, absmax scale; worst-case error \(0.1519 \times\) absmax. Double quantization: 8-bit scales in groups of 256 + FP32: \(4 + 8/64 + 32/(64\cdot 256) = 4.127\) bits vs 4.5.
  • 70B NF4+DQ: linears 68.45 G × 4.127 / 8 = 35.31 GB, + 4.21 GB bf16 embeddings/head = 39.5 GB (141.1 GB in bf16). 65B: ~34.5 GB base, 45.0 GB training total in the paper (48 GB card, batch 1).
  • Merge: exact in fp32 then round; free at inference; one copy per adapter. With NF4: off-grid, small updates rounded away; keep separate or merge in 16-bit and re-evaluate.
  • Limits: LoRA learns less (code CPT 0.224 vs 0.263 HumanEval; rank 16 code SFT 0.358 vs 0.497) and forgets less, conditionally (intruder dimensions; \(\alpha = 8\), high LR, sequential tasks). Rule: LoRA matches full finetuning until data information (about 1 bit/token) exceeds capacity (about 2 bits/param).
  • RL: about 1 bit per episode, so rank-1 LoRA matches full finetuning in Thinking Machines' policy-gradient runs (blog, not peer reviewed). LoRA is less tolerant of large batches.
  • Variants: DoRA (magnitude + direction, +3.7 to +4.4 commonsense points, norm cost), LoRA+ (\(\eta_B = \lambda \eta_A\)), adapters (not mergeable, +20 to 30% latency), prefix/prompt tuning (need scale, fragile), (IA)³ (rescaling vectors).
  • Serving: adapter 83.9 MB at \(r=16\); unmerged batched kernels (S-LoRA, Punica SGMV); 32 distinct adapters per batch adds 16.7% bytes per decode step.
