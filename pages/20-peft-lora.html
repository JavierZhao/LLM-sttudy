Parameter-efficient finetuning (LoRA, QLoRA)

P1 ~40 min Prereqs: 04 Transformer block, 19 SFT

Finetuning all of Llama-3-8B needs about 128 GB for weights, gradients and optimizer states alone. LoRA freezes the pretrained weights and trains a low-rank update (about 17 GB); QLoRA also stores the frozen weights in 4 bits (about 6.4 GB). This page covers the math, the initialization and scaling questions interviewers ask, QLoRA's quantization tricks, the main variants, and the evidence on what low-rank updates can and cannot learn, including for RL.

  • Systems: when merging is exact, why merging into a 4-bit base is not, and how thousands of adapters are served from one base model.

Why finetune parameter-efficiently

The memory arithmetic

Full finetuning 全参数微调 updates every weight. With AdamW in the standard mixed-precision recipe it costs 16 bytes per parameter before activations: bf16 weights (2) and gradients (2), an fp32 master copy (4) and two fp32 Adam moments (4 + 4), as derived on page 15. For Llama-3-8B (8,030,261,248 parameters):

\[ 8{,}030{,}261{,}248 \times 16\ \text{B} = 128{,}484{,}179{,}968\ \text{B} \approx 128.5\ \text{GB} \quad (119.7\ \text{GiB}) \]

A single 80 GB GPU cannot hold even the states, and every finetuned variant is another 16 GB bf16 checkpoint. At GPT-3 scale, Hu et al. report a LoRA cut training memory from 1.2 TB to 350 GB and a checkpoint from 350 GB to 35 MB [Hu et al. 2021, §4.2].

Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) 参数高效微调 freezes the pretrained weights and trains a small set of new or selected parameters, so gradients and optimizer states exist only for those. The families: insert small modules (adapters), train extra inputs or key/value prefixes (prompt and prefix tuning), rescale activations (IA³), or learn a low-rank weight update (LoRA 低秩适配). LoRA dominates because its update can be merged into the weights, so serving costs nothing extra.

What PEFT saves, and what it does not

It saves gradients, master weights and Adam states for frozen weights, plus per-task storage. It does not save the frozen weights (2 bytes each in bf16) or the activations, since backpropagation still runs through every layer. For a 7B model at batch size 1 the QLoRA paper measures 567 MB of LoRA input gradients without gradient checkpointing (18 MB per sequence with it) against 26 MB of adapter parameters [Dettmers et al. 2023, §2].

Why so few parameters can be enough: intrinsic dimension

Aghajanyan et al. measured the intrinsic dimension 内在维度 of finetuning: optimize \(\theta = \theta_0 + P\theta_d\) in a \(d\)-dimensional random subspace (fixed random \(P\), \(\theta_d = 0\) at the start, so training begins at the pretrained model), and call the smallest \(d\) reaching 90% of full-finetuning performance \(d_{90}\). For RoBERTa-Large, \(d_{90}\) is about 200 on MRPC (207 or 322 depending on the variant) and 774 on QQP [Aghajanyan et al. 2020, Table 1]; it falls as pretraining proceeds and is smaller for larger models.

Hu et al. take this as inspiration but hypothesize something different and stronger: each weight update \(\Delta W\) has low rank [Hu et al. 2021, §4.1]. A random low-dimensional subspace sufficing is not the same statement, and MRPC and QQP are easy for a large model. The limits section shows what happens on harder tasks.

From your jet tagger

If you trained your tagger from scratch, every parameter was capacity you needed to learn the whole jet-to-label function. Finetuning a pretrained LLM starts from a function already close to the target, with post-training data tiny next to the model. That is the regime where a low-rank update is plausible, and it is why LoRA struggles once the "finetuning" set is billions of tokens.

LoRA: low-rank adaptation

The parametrization

For a frozen pretrained weight \(W_0 \in \R^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}\), LoRA learns a rank-\(r\) update scaled by \(\alpha/r\):

\[ h = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r}\, B A\, x, \qquad A \in \R^{r \times d_{\text{in}}},\quad B \in \R^{d_{\text{out}} \times r},\quad r \ll \min(d_{\text{in}}, d_{\text{out}}) \]
Hu et al. 2021, Eq. 3 and §4.1 (the paper writes \(d \times k\) for \(d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}\))
Layout

This is the one place in the course where the weight is transposed relative to "\(xW\)". PyTorch's nn.Linear stores \(W\) as \((d_{\text{out}}, d_{\text{in}})\) and computes \(y = xW^\top\) for a row-vector \(x\); the LoRA paper and PEFT use the same layout, so in code \(y = xW_0^\top + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}(xA^\top)B^\top\).

\(\Delta W\) has rank at most \(r\) and is never formed: \(x\) is projected down to \(r\) dimensions by \(A\), then up by \(B\). The adapter adds \(r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}})\) parameters (and the same fraction of FLOPs) instead of \(d_{\text{in}} d_{\text{out}}\). For a \(4096 \times 4096\) matrix at \(r = 16\): \(131{,}072 / 16{,}777{,}216 = 0.78\%\).

Initialization: why B starts at zero

Hu et al. use a random Gaussian \(A\) and \(B = 0\), "so \(\Delta W = BA\) is zero at the beginning of training". With \(g = \partial L/\partial h\) and \(\gamma = \alpha/r\), the gradients are

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial B} = \gamma\, g\,(Ax)^\top, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial A} = \gamma\, B^\top g\, x^\top . \]
  1. The model starts exactly at the pretrained function. Random \(B\) and \(A\) would inject noise of size \(\gamma\|BA\|\) into every layer.
  2. Not both zero. \(\partial L/\partial B \propto A\) and \(\partial L/\partial A \propto B\), so with both zero nothing ever moves: a saddle point [Hayou et al. 2024, §3.1].
  3. One random, either works (LoRA+ analyzes both). The convention is random \(A\) with variance about \(1/d_{\text{in}}\) (PEFT: uniform on \(\pm 1/\sqrt{d_{\text{in}}}\)), so \(Ax\) has \(O(1)\) entries at any width, and zero \(B\).

Consequence: at step 0, \(\partial L/\partial A = 0\) exactly and only \(B\) moves; \(A\) starts learning once \(B\) is nonzero, so the adapter's effective learning rate grows during training. Thinking Machines report a best multiplier over the full-finetuning rate of about 15× for runs under roughly 100 steps, converging to 10× [Schulman & Thinking Machines 2025].

The scale α/r

Hu et al.: "When optimizing with Adam, tuning \(\alpha\) is roughly the same as tuning the learning rate if we scale the initialization appropriately. As a result, we simply set \(\alpha\) to the first \(r\) we try and do not tune it. This scaling helps to reduce the need to retune hyperparameters when we vary \(r\)" [Hu et al. 2021, §4.1].

Why \(1/r\). \(BA = \sum_{i=1}^{r} b_i a_i^\top\) sums \(r\) rank-1 terms, each with the same expected first update regardless of \(r\), so \((1/r)\sum_i\) is an average whose expectation is rank-independent (Thinking Machines). With \(\alpha\) fixed, learning curves for different ranks coincide at the start and the best learning rate moves by less than 2× between \(r = 4\) and \(r = 512\). So changing \(r\) without \(\alpha\) adds capacity that pays off later, not faster initial learning, and the same learning rate keeps working. Changing \(\alpha\) at fixed \(r\) rescales the update, which is a learning-rate change: with Adam (\(\epsilon = 0\)) training is exactly invariant under \(\alpha \to \alpha/(pq)\), \(\text{init}_A \to p\,\text{init}_A\), \(\text{LR}_A \to p\,\text{LR}_A\), \(\text{LR}_B \to q\,\text{LR}_B\), so only two of the four hyperparameters matter.

Which matrices, which rank, which learning rate

Modules. Hu et al. adapted attention only; their budget study (18M parameters on GPT-3) shows spreading a budget over more matrices beats a higher rank on one: \(W_q\) at \(r=8\) gives 70.4 on WikiSQL, \(W_q, W_v\) at \(r=4\) gives 73.7, all four at \(r=2\) give 73.7 [Hu et al. 2021, Table 5]. QLoRA needs all linear layers to match full finetuning of LLaMA-7B on Alpaca [Dettmers et al. 2023, Fig. 2]. Thinking Machines find attention-only LoRA underperforms MLP-only even at matched parameters (attention rank 256 loses to MLP rank 128) and that attention adds nothing on top of the MLP; Biderman et al. also find most of the gain in the MLP. Thinking Machines suggest, via the empirical NTK, that the matrices with the most parameters dominate: in Llama-3-8B the MLP holds \(176{,}160{,}768\) of the \(218{,}103{,}808\) linear parameters per layer, 80.8%.

Rank. \(r = 1\) is already competitive on GPT-3 (WikiSQL 73.4 against 73.5 at \(r=64\) when adapting \(W_q, W_v\), Table 6), and QLoRA finds \(r\) immaterial on its tasks, but neither holds on harder targets: code instruction tuning is ordered by rank (HumanEval 0.358, 0.417, 0.498 at \(r = 16, 64, 256\)) [Biderman et al. 2024, §4.1]. Low-rank adapters fall off the loss curve "when the adapter runs out of capacity", at a step count that grows with rank (Thinking Machines). The rank you need scales with how much you are teaching, not with model width.

Learning rate. About 10× the full-finetuning rate: Biderman et al. report "one order of magnitude higher", \(5\times 10^{-5}\) to \(5\times10^{-4}\) for Llama-2-7B, and Thinking Machines fit a multiplier of 9.8 over 14 Llama and Qwen models. Three documented recipes:

Merging

Because \(\Delta W\) has the shape of \(W_0\), merging 权重合并 works: compute \(W = W_0 + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BA\) once and serve the ordinary model with no added latency "by construction"; switching tasks is a subtraction and an addition [Hu et al. 2021, §4.1]. Adapters cannot do this (nonlinearity): on GPT-2 medium at batch 1 and length 128 they add 20.7% and 30.3% latency [Hu et al. 2021, Table 1]. Caveats:

  • One full copy per adapter, which forfeits sharing the base and batching across adapters (see serving many adapters).
  • Precision. Add in fp32 and round once. bf16 keeps 8 significant bits, so rounding \(W_0 + \Delta W\) perturbs an entry by up to about 0.2 to 0.4% of its size; with \(\|\Delta W\|_F / \|W_0\|_F\) at 6 to 11% (3.57/61.95 and 6.91/61.95 in one GPT-3 layer, Hu et al. Table 7) that is a few percent of the update.
  • A quantized base is much worse: see below.
import math
import torch
from torch import nn

class LoRALinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, base: nn.Linear, r: int, alpha: float):
        super().__init__()
        self.base, self.scaling, self.merged = base, alpha / r, False
        for p in base.parameters():
            p.requires_grad_(False)          # W0 and bias never train
        d_out, d_in = base.weight.shape      # PyTorch layout (d_out, d_in)
        bound = 1 / math.sqrt(d_in)
        # A random, B zero: delta W = 0 at step 0
        kw = dict(dtype=base.weight.dtype, device=base.weight.device)
        self.lora_A = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(r, d_in, **kw).uniform_(-bound, bound))
        self.lora_B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d_out, r, **kw))

    def forward(self, x):                    # x: (..., d_in)
        y = self.base(x)                     # (..., d_out)
        if self.merged:
            return y
        # (..., d_in) -> (..., r) -> (..., d_out); never form B @ A here
        return y + self.scaling * ((x @ self.lora_A.T) @ self.lora_B.T)

    @torch.no_grad()
    def merge(self):                         # W = W0 + (alpha / r) B A
        if not self.merged:
            self.base.weight += self.scaling * (self.lora_B @ self.lora_A)
            self.merged = True

    @torch.no_grad()
    def unmerge(self):                       # back to W0
        if self.merged:
            self.base.weight -= self.scaling * (self.lora_B @ self.lora_A)
            self.merged = False

LoRA
Problem
Full finetuning costs 16 bytes per parameter of training state and a full-size checkpoint per task.
Idea
Freeze \(W_0\) and learn \(\Delta W = \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BA\) with \(A\) random and \(B = 0\).
Why it works
Post-training updates are small relative to the pretrained function and, for typical instruction or reasoning data, fit a low-rank subspace. \(B = 0\) starts at the pretrained model, and the linear form merges into the weights.
Evidence
GPT-3 175B: 10,000× fewer trainable parameters, with WikiSQL and MNLI at or above full finetuning (73.8 and 91.6 at \(r_q = r_v = 8\), against 73.8 and 89.5) [Hu et al. 2021, Abstract, Table 15]. RoBERTa-large on GLUE: 89.0 with 0.8M parameters against 88.9 with 355M [Table 2].
Cost
Limited capacity, sensitivity to \(\alpha\), learning rate and modules, worse tolerance of large batches, activations unchanged, and a clear gap when data far exceeds adapter capacity (see the limits section).
Used by
The default PEFT method in Hugging Face PEFT; multi-tenant serving in vLLM and SGLang (per Thinking Machines); the base of QLoRA and the variants below.

QLoRA: a 4-bit frozen base

The idea

LoRA shrinks the trainable states but leaves the frozen base at 2 bytes per weight: 16 GB for Llama-3-8B, 141 GB for 70B. The base is only read, so its precision matters through the error it adds to the forward and backward passes, and the adapters can absorb some of that error. QLoRA stores the base in 4 bits, dequantizes each weight matrix to bf16 just before its matmul, and keeps adapters and arithmetic in bf16 [Dettmers et al. 2023, §3, Eq. 5]:

\[ Y = X\,\text{dq}\big(W^{\text{NF4}}\big)^{\!\top} + \gamma\,(XA^\top)B^\top \qquad (\text{all in bf16}) \]

In the backward pass \(\partial L/\partial X\) flows through the dequantized \(W\), so earlier layers still get gradients, but no weight gradient is computed for the 4-bit weights. One storage type (4-bit), one compute type (bf16). Three techniques make it work.

Block-wise quantization and the NormalFloat grid

Block-wise absmax quantization 分块量化: cut the flattened weights into blocks of 64, store each block's \(c = \max|w|\), divide by \(c\) to land in \([-1, 1]\), and store for each value the index of the nearest of 16 levels. An outlier then hurts only its 63 neighbors instead of a whole tensor's scale, at the cost of one FP32 constant per block: \(32/64 = 0.5\) bits per parameter [Dettmers et al. 2023, §3].

NormalFloat-4 (NF4). Trained weights are close to zero-centered normal (the paper's Shapiro-Wilk test on LLaMA-7B finds 7.5% of hidden units non-normal against 5% expected by chance, App. F), so a uniform grid wastes codes. Quantile quantization places the levels so each bin has equal probability mass under \(\mathcal N(0,1)\), rescaled to \([-1, 1]\). The paper makes the grid asymmetric so 0 is exact: 7 negative levels, 0, and 8 positive levels (App. E):

The evidence is empirical: mean Pile-CC perplexity over 125M to 13B models is 34.34 for Int4, 31.07 and 29.48 for two FP4 layouts, and 27.41 for NF4 with double quantization [Dettmers et al. 2023, Table 2]. In my check on \(\mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)\) weights with block 64, relative error \(\|w - \hat w\|/\|w\|\) is 9.2% for NF4, 10.0% for a uniform 16-level grid and 10.8% for symmetric int4. "Information-theoretically optimal" holds for exactly normal data with known variance, while a block of 64 samples its own absmax (about \(2.6\sigma\) in my simulation), so the perplexity table is the real justification.

Double quantization

Double quantization 双重量化 quantizes the constants: the FP32 block scales \(c_2\) are stored as 8-bit floats in groups of 256 (no degradation was observed at 8 bits; the scales are positive, so their mean is subtracted first), plus one FP32 constant \(c_1\) per group. The cost per weight:

\[ \underbrace{4}_{\text{code}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{8}{64}}_{c_2\ \text{(8-bit)}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{32}{64 \cdot 256}}_{c_1\ \text{(FP32)}} = 4.127\ \text{bits} \quad\text{versus}\quad 4 + \tfrac{32}{64} = 4.5 \text{ without.} \]

The saving is 0.373 bits per parameter, \(65.29 \times 10^9 \times 0.373 / 8 = 3.04\) GB at 65B ("approximately 3 GB" in the paper). Per 16,384 weights: \(65{,}536 + 2{,}048 + 32 = 67{,}616\) bits, and \(67{,}616 / 16{,}384 = 4.127\).

Paged optimizers

Paged optimizers 分页优化器 use NVIDIA unified memory: optimizer state is evicted to CPU RAM when the GPU runs out of memory and paged back for the update. They target transient spikes (a long sequence under gradient checkpointing), not the average footprint. The paper gives no paging measurement beyond equal speed at batch size 16 for a 65B model on a 48 GB GPU [Dettmers et al. 2023, §4].

Evidence for QLoRA, and how far it goes

On RoBERTa-large GLUE and T5 80M to 3B, 4-bit QLoRA matches 16-bit LoRA and full finetuning (RoBERTa-large: 88.6 BF16, 88.8 BF16 LoRA, 88.6 FP4 QLoRA, with no NF4 entry; T5-3B: 54.3, 55.4, 55.3 with NF4+DQ; Table 3). At T5-11B, NF4+DQ (60.9) equals BF16 LoRA (60.7) but sits 1.1 points below the 62.0 full-finetuning number. On LLaMA 7B to 65B finetuned on Alpaca and FLAN v2, mean 5-shot MMLU is 53.0 for BF16 LoRA, 52.2 for FP4 and 53.1 for NF4+DQ (Table 4; differences of tenths are inside noise the paper does not quantify). That is parity with 16-bit LoRA. Independent evidence is mixed: Biderman et al. cite Ivison et al. finding QLoRA below full finetuning for Llama-2 on Tülu-v2-mix [Biderman et al. 2024, §5].

Can you merge into a quantized base?

Not without paying. The merged weight \(\hat W + \gamma BA\) is off the NF4 grid, so a 4-bit model must re-quantize, and the rounding step is large next to the update: the smallest NF4 step is 0.0796 of the block absmax, about \(0.2\sigma\) for Gaussian weights, while \(\|\Delta W\|_F / \|W\|_F\) was 6 to 11% in Hu et al.'s one GPT-3 layer, so a typical entry of \(\Delta W\) is a fraction of a step. In a toy simulation (\(W \sim \mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)\), \(4096 \times 4096\), NF4 block 64, \(\Delta W\) a random rank-16 matrix with \(\|\Delta W\|_F = \rho \|W\|_F\), comparing \(Q(\hat W + \Delta W) - \hat W\) with \(\Delta W\)): at \(\rho = 5\%\) only 2.4% of codes change and the cosine with the true update is 0.35; at \(\rho = 10\%\), 19.6% of codes change and the cosine is 0.74. Real updates are structured, so the numbers will differ, but a small update is mostly rounded away.

The options: keep the adapter separate, as in training; dequantize, merge in 16 bits and serve 16-bit; or merge, re-quantize with a calibrated method and re-evaluate (page 17). The adapter was also trained against the NF4 rounding error and may partly compensate for it, so merging it into the original unquantized weights is a slightly different function from the one trained. Evaluate the merged model; do not assume it equals the trained one.

Adapters

Adapters 适配器: Houlsby et al. insert two bottleneck modules per Transformer layer (after the attention projection and the FFN): project \(d \to m\), apply a nonlinearity, project back, add a skip connection, with near-zero initialization so the module starts as the identity. The bottleneck cannot be merged and adds sequential layers, hence the latency numbers above.

Prefix and prompt tuning: why they fell out of favor

Prefix tuning 前缀微调 prepends trainable key/value vectors at every layer; prompt tuning 提示微调 trains soft token embeddings at the input only. Both leave the model untouched and allow mixed-task batches. Prompt tuning "closes the gap" with model tuning only at scale, matching it at T5-XXL (11B) on SuperGLUE [Lester et al. 2021, Fig. 1]. The sources point to four reasons they lost to LoRA:

  1. Sequence budget. They "reduce the model's usable sequence length" [Hu et al. 2021, §1] and cost attention compute at every token.
  2. Scale dependence. They lag below billions of parameters (Lester), and the benefit of a good initialization disappears only at XXL.
  3. Optimization fragility. Lester used a learning rate of 0.3 and found class-label initialization clearly best at small sizes; Li & Liang needed an MLP reparametrization because directly updating the prefix "leads to unstable optimization and a slight drop in performance" (§4.3); the (IA)³ authors "were unable to attain reasonable accuracy with prompt tuning" on T0-3B few-shot; Hu et al. find prefix accuracy non-monotonic in parameter count (WikiSQL 63.1 at 3.2M parameters, 55.9 at 6.4M, their Table 15).
  4. No merge. The prefix stays in the context at inference.

Their strengths are cheap mixed-task batching and, for prompt tuning, better transfer under domain shift (Lester §5).

(IA)³

(IA)³ learns three vectors per layer, \(l_k\), \(l_v\), \(l_{ff}\), that rescale keys, values and the FFN's inner activations elementwise, starting at 1. Since \(l \odot (Wx) = (l \odot W)x\), a single-task (IA)³ folds into the weights, and mixed-task batches are cheap. In the T-Few recipe on T0 (11B) it stores 4.2 MB per task and reached 75.8% on RAFT against a 73.5% human baseline [Liu et al. 2022, Tables 1-2]. Its capacity is far below LoRA's, and the paper evaluates only classification and multiple-choice tasks, listing generative tasks as future work.

DoRA: magnitude and direction

DoRA writes each weight as a magnitude times a direction, initializes \(m = \|W_0\|_c\), keeps the direction frozen apart from a LoRA update, and trains \(m\):

\[ W' = m\,\frac{W_0 + BA}{\|W_0 + BA\|_c} \]

Motivation: on VL-BART query matrices the changes in magnitude and direction are negatively correlated in full finetuning (−0.62) and positively in LoRA (+0.83), which the authors read as LoRA tying the two together; DoRA is at −0.31 [Liu et al. 2024, §3.2, §4.1]. Commonsense-reasoning average: 74.7 for LoRA against 78.4 for DoRA on LLaMA-7B, 80.5 against 81.5 on LLaMA-13B, and 80.8 against 85.2 on LLaMA3-8B (+2.1 on LLaMA2-7B); at half the rank DoRA is still ahead on LLaMA-7B (77.5) [Table 1]. The weight norm of \(W_0 + \gamma BA\) must be recomputed every step, which materializes that matrix; treating the norm as a constant in the backward pass cuts training memory 24.4% (37.3 to 28.2 GB on LLaMA-7B) for 0.2 points of accuracy [Table 7]. It merges like LoRA. Caveats: the gains are the authors' runs on one benchmark suite, with LoRA baseline hyperparameters from earlier work; and the paper's \(\|\cdot\|_c\) is a column norm of \(W \in \R^{d \times k}\) while Hugging Face PEFT normalizes each row of the \((d_{\text{out}}, d_{\text{in}})\) weight, one magnitude per output unit.

LoRA+ and rsLoRA: optimization variants

LoRA+ sets \(\eta_B = \lambda\,\eta_A\) with \(\lambda \gg 1\). An infinite-width analysis says equal learning rates cannot learn features efficiently, while \(\eta_A = \Theta(n^{-1})\) and \(\eta_B = \Theta(1)\) can (Theorem 1, informal). The constant is not derived, so \(\lambda\) is tuned: \(2^4\) for RoBERTa with \(A\) zero and \(B\) random, \(2^2\) to \(2^3\) with the standard initialization, \(2^1\) to \(2^2\) for Llama-7B. Reported gains are 1 to 2% and up to 2× faster convergence, larger on harder tasks (MMLU +1.3% for Llama-7B on Flan-v2) and negligible on easy ones (Llama on MNLI) [Hayou et al. 2024, Abstract, §5]. rsLoRA uses \(\alpha/\sqrt{r}\) (see the scale α/r). Thinking Machines argue both are reparametrizations of the same two-dimensional hyperparameter space (raising \(\text{LR}_B\) equals raising \(\text{init}_A/\text{LR}_A\)) and report being "unable to improve on" plain LoRA hyperparameters in their sweeps [Schulman & Thinking Machines 2025]. Other variants: AdaLoRA [Zhang et al. 2023] gives each matrix its own rank by pruning the singular values of an SVD-form update, and PiSSA [Meng et al. 2024] initializes \(A, B\) from the top-\(r\) singular components of \(W_0\) and freezes the residual \(W_0 - BA\), so training starts on the principal directions instead of noise (quantizing the residual gives QPiSSA, reported to start with lower quantization error than QLoRA).

"Forgets less" is conditional

The forgetting in question is catastrophic forgetting 灾难性遗忘. Shuttleworth et al. find that LoRA updates introduce intruder dimensions, high-ranking singular vectors nearly orthogonal to every pretrained singular vector, which full finetuning does not produce. Scaling those singular values down after training lowers forgetting with little loss on the finetuning task, so they cause it. LoRA usually forgets less, but with \(\alpha = 8\) instead of \(2r\), or with large learning rates, it can forget more than full finetuning, and when RoBERTa-base is finetuned on six tasks in sequence LoRA accumulates intruder dimensions and degrades faster. Very high rank (\(r = 2048\)) behaves like full finetuning, with none [Shuttleworth et al. 2025, §3-5].

"LoRA Without Regret"

Thinking Machines (a 2025 blog post by John Schulman and colleagues, not peer reviewed) sweep rank from 1 to 512 and the learning rate for every condition, on Llama-3 and Qwen3 models with Tülu3 and OpenThoughts3 for supervised learning and math tasks for RL [Schulman & Thinking Machines 2025]:

  • On small to medium supervised datasets LoRA matches full finetuning in sample efficiency and final loss, provided it covers all layers (MLP essential) and is not capacity-limited. Beyond its capacity it underperforms: no hard floor, but less efficient training.
  • LoRA tolerates large batch sizes less well, independent of rank, which they attribute to the product parametrization \(BA\).
  • For RL, LoRA matches full finetuning "even with ranks as low as 1" (MATH and GSM8K on Llama-3.1-8B; DeepMath on Qwen3-8B-base, where learning curves, AIME scores and chain-of-thought lengths track full finetuning).

The RL argument is an information count. Supervised learning gives \(O(\text{tokens})\) bits per example; a policy-gradient episode gives one scalar advantage, so \(O(1)\) bits (their bound \(I(G;R) \le H(\text{Adv}) \lesssim \log B\) for \(B\) advantage bins). Their MATH run uses about 10,000 problems with 32 samples each: at one bit per completion, 320,000 bits. A rank-1 LoRA on all linear layers of Llama-3-8B has 2,621,440 parameters by my count (they say 3M), about 8 times more. DeepSeek-R1-Zero's 10,400 steps of 32 questions with 16 samples is 5.3M episodes, which they predict a low-rank LoRA could absorb; that is a prediction, not an experiment. Their caveats: the 1-bit-per-episode figure "may only apply narrowly to policy gradient algorithms", the 1 bit/token figure measures memorization and overestimates what generalizable learning needs, and how capacity interacts with parameters is an open question.

From your jet tagger

Count the same way for your tagger. A binary label is at most 1 bit per jet, so tens of millions of labeled jets supply at most about 107 to 108 bits. At 2 bits per parameter, a transformer with a few million parameters stores about 107 bits, so the labels can fill the model, which is why no parameter was spare. Policy-gradient RL on an 8B model is the opposite corner: about 3 × 105 bits of reward signal (the MATH run above) against a capacity near 1010 bits, so even a rank-1 adapter (2.6M parameters, about 5 × 106 bits) exceeds it. SFT and continued pretraining supply about 1 bit per token, which puts them in between and, at 20B tokens, beyond.

A back-of-envelope capacity check

Thinking Machines' rough constants are about 1 bit per training token and about 2 bits per parameter of capacity (from Allen-Zhu and Li). Llama-2-7B with all-linear LoRA has \(2{,}498{,}560\,r\) trainable parameters (per layer and unit of rank, \(4 \times 8192 + 3 \times 15{,}104 = 78{,}080\), times 32 layers): 80, 320 and 1,280 Mbit at \(r = 16, 64, 256\). Code instruction data at epoch 4 is about \(4 \times 72.5\)M = 0.29B tokens, roughly 290 Mbit, so rank 16 is capacity-limited, rank 64 borderline and rank 256 not, matching HumanEval 0.358, 0.417, 0.498. Twenty billion CPT tokens (about 20 Gbit) is about 16 times the capacity of rank 256 (20 / 1.28), and LoRA loses at every rank there. Both constants are rough, so this is a consistency check, not a prediction.

Serving many adapters

One merged copy per customer means one base-model copy per customer. Unmerged, a Llama-3-8B adapter at \(r = 16\) is 41,943,040 parameters, 83.9 MB in bf16, so 1,000 adapters are 83.9 GB, which fits in a server's host RAM next to one 16 GB base. Unmerged serving adds FLOPs (\(2 \times 41.9\)M against \(2 \times 8.03\)B per token, 0.52%), but decoding is memory-bound: each distinct adapter in a batch must be read separately while the base weights are read once. With 32 distinct adapters that is \(32 \times 83.9\) MB = 2.68 GB against 16.06 GB, 16.7% more bytes per step.

S-LoRA keeps all adapters in host memory, fetches those the running batch needs, and computes \(xW_0\) as one batched matmul plus \(xAB\) per adapter with custom kernels for mixed ranks and lengths. Unified Paging exploits that an adapter matrix of rank \(R\) has shape \((R, H)\) and a KV cache of length \(S\) has shape \((S, H)\), so both share one pool of pages. On Llama-7B (rank 8) it serves 2,000 adapters on one A100 80GB at 7.61 requests per second against 8.05 with 5 adapters, while vLLM with merged copies manages 5 adapters at 2.04 and runs out of memory at 100; the paper claims up to 4× vLLM's throughput (abstract) and up to 30× Hugging Face PEFT's (§1) [Sheng et al. 2023, Table 3, §1]. Punica contributes the SGMV kernel (segmented gather matrix-vector multiplication): requests are grouped by adapter and \(y \mathrel{+}= xAB\) runs as two launches, "shrink" to rank \(r\) and "expand" back. With every request on a different adapter it is a memory-bound matrix-vector product; the paper reports 12× the throughput of existing systems at +2 ms per token [Chen et al. 2023, Abstract, §4]. The LoRA paper itself notes that batching different tasks is not straightforward once adapters are merged [Hu et al. 2021, §4.2].

Common misconceptions

  • "LoRA makes finetuning use far less memory in every respect." It removes gradients, master weights and Adam states for frozen weights. The frozen weights (2 bytes per parameter) and the activations remain; for a 7B model at batch size 1 the LoRA input gradients (567 MB without gradient checkpointing) outweigh the adapter (26 MB).
  • "Initialize \(A\) and \(B\) both randomly (or both to zero)." Both random starts from a perturbed model; both zero has zero gradient everywhere. One is random, the other zero, and at step 0 only \(B\) receives gradient.
  • "\(\alpha\) is an independent knob from the learning rate." With Adam it is redundant with the learning rate and the initialization scale (two of the four hyperparameters are free). Its job in \(\alpha/r\) is to keep the best learning rate roughly rank-independent.
  • "Higher rank always means better." Rank buys capacity that matters only when data exceeds it. On small SFT sets ranks from 1 to 64 look alike, and with \(\gamma = \alpha/r\) and a fixed learning rate a higher rank may not even learn faster.
  • "NF4 is 4 bits per weight." It is 4.127 bits with double quantization and 4.5 without, and the embeddings and head usually stay in bf16.
  • "You can merge a QLoRA adapter into the 4-bit model for free." The merged weights fall off the NF4 grid; re-quantizing rounds away much of a small update.
  • "LoRA always matches full finetuning" or "LoRA never does." It matches on small to medium SFT and, per one report, on RL; it loses on continued pretraining and other capacity-limited settings.
  • "LoRA forgets less, so it is safe for sequential finetuning." Forgetting depends on \(\alpha\), learning rate and rank, and intruder dimensions accumulate across tasks.

Interview questions

Warm-up What does LoRA add to a frozen linear layer, and how many parameters is that for a 4096 × 4096 matrix at rank 16?

Two matrices, \(A \in \R^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}\) and \(B \in \R^{d_{\text{out}} \times r}\), in parallel with the frozen \(W_0\): \(h = W_0 x + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BAx\). The update has rank at most \(r\). Parameters: \(r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}}) = 16 \times 8192 = 131{,}072\), against \(16{,}777{,}216\) for the matrix, so 0.78%. \(B\) starts at 0 so the model begins at the pretrained function, and since the update is linear it can be merged into \(W_0\) for serving.

Warm-up Where does LoRA's memory saving come from, and what does it not reduce?

Full finetuning carries 16 bytes per parameter (bf16 weight and gradient, fp32 master copy, two fp32 Adam moments). LoRA needs the gradient, master copy and Adam states only for the adapter: for Llama-3-8B, 16.06 GB of frozen bf16 weights plus 0.67 GB of adapter states, 16.7 GB, against 128.5 GB.

It does not reduce the frozen weights (2 bytes per parameter), the activations (backpropagation still passes through all layers, so long sequences still need checkpointing), or most of the compute: the forward and input-gradient matmuls through \(W_0\) remain, so FLOPs drop to about two thirds, not to a few percent. The QLoRA paper measures 567 MB of LoRA input gradients (without gradient checkpointing) against 26 MB of adapter for a 7B model at batch size 1.

Warm-up What is NF4, and what does double quantization save?

NF4 is a 16-level code whose levels are quantiles of a standard normal, rescaled to \([-1, 1]\), with an exact zero (7 negative levels, 0, 8 positive). Weights are quantized in blocks of 64: divide by the block absmax, store the index of the nearest level, and store the absmax. Normal weights cluster near zero, so equal-probability bins beat a uniform grid; the paper measures perplexity 27.41 for NF4+DQ against 29.48 to 34.34 for FP4 and Int4.

The per-block FP32 scale costs \(32/64 = 0.5\) bits per weight. Double quantization stores the scales as 8-bit values in groups of 256 with one FP32 constant per group: \(8/64 + 32/(64 \cdot 256) = 0.127\) bits. Total 4.127 bits per weight instead of 4.5, saving 0.373 bits, about 3 GB at 65B.

Core Implement a LoRA linear layer. Why initialize \(B\) to zero?

Freeze the base layer; create lora_A of shape \((r, d_{\text{in}})\), uniform on \(\pm 1/\sqrt{d_{\text{in}}}\), and lora_B of shape \((d_{\text{out}}, r)\), zeros; scale \(\alpha/r\). Forward: base(x) + scale * (x @ A.T) @ B.T, two skinny matmuls, never forming \(BA\) (that would cost \(d_{\text{out}} d_{\text{in}} r\) and defeat the purpose). Merge: W += scale * B @ A; unmerge subtracts it.

Why \(B = 0\): (1) \(\Delta W = 0\) at step 0, so training starts at the pretrained function. (2) The gradients are \(\partial L/\partial B = \gamma g (Ax)^\top\) and \(\partial L/\partial A = \gamma B^\top g x^\top\), so with both zero nothing moves (a saddle); with \(A\) random and \(B = 0\), \(B\) gets a gradient immediately and \(A\) starts learning once \(B\) is nonzero. (3) Random \(A\) with variance \(\sim 1/d_{\text{in}}\) gives \(Ax\) entries of order 1 at any width. Zero \(A\) with random \(B\) also works (LoRA+ analyzes both); the convention is a choice, not a law.

Core What does \(\alpha/r\) do? What happens if you change \(r\) but not \(\alpha\)?

The adapter output is multiplied by \(\gamma = \alpha/r\). \(BA = \sum_{i=1}^r b_i a_i^\top\) sums \(r\) terms whose expected first update does not depend on \(r\), so dividing by \(r\) keeps the initial change in function independent of rank. With \(\alpha\) fixed, learning curves for different ranks coincide at the start and the best learning rate moves by less than 2× from \(r = 4\) to 512 (Thinking Machines). So raising \(r\) at fixed \(\alpha\) does not make the adapter learn faster; it adds capacity that pays off later, and your learning rate keeps working.

Changing \(\alpha\) at fixed \(r\) rescales the update, equivalent to a learning-rate change: with Adam (\(\epsilon = 0\)) training is invariant under \(\alpha \to \alpha/(pq)\), \(\text{init}_A \to p\,\text{init}_A\), \(\text{LR}_A \to p\,\text{LR}_A\), \(\text{LR}_B \to q\,\text{LR}_B\), leaving two effective hyperparameters. Alternatives: rsLoRA's \(\alpha/\sqrt{r}\) (stable as \(r \to \infty\)) and Biderman et al.'s \(\alpha = 2r\) (constant \(\gamma = 2\)). Unless \(\gamma \propto 1/r\), re-sweep the learning rate when changing \(r\).

Core How does QLoRA fit a 65B model on one 48 GB GPU?

Regular 16-bit finetuning needs over 780 GB (65.2B × 12 bytes). QLoRA changes three things. (1) The frozen base goes to NF4 with blocks of 64 and double-quantized scales, 4.127 bits per weight: about 33.4 GB for the linear layers plus 1.05 GB of bf16 embeddings and head, 34.5 GB. (2) Only LoRA adapters are trained (all linear layers, \(r = 64\), about 0.8B parameters at 12 bytes each, 9.6 GB), in bf16, with each weight dequantized on the fly. (3) Paged optimizers move optimizer state to CPU RAM when a long sequence under gradient checkpointing spikes memory. The sum is 44 GB by my arithmetic and 45.0 GB in the paper's Fig. 6 at batch size 1 and sequence length 512.

Caveats: 3 GB of headroom at batch 1; the paper shows parity with 16-bit LoRA, not full finetuning, at 33B and 65B; and dequantization adds work to every matmul.

Core Can you merge LoRA weights? What about with a quantized base?

Yes for a 16-bit base: \(W = W_0 + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BA\) has the shape of \(W_0\), so inference has no extra latency and unmerging is a subtraction. Add in fp32 and round once; bf16 rounding perturbs weights by up to about 0.2 to 0.4% of their size, a few percent of a typical update. Merging costs multi-adapter batching, since each merged model is its own weight set.

Not cleanly for an NF4 base. The merged weight is off the 4-bit grid, and re-quantizing with a smallest step of about \(0.2\sigma\) rounds away much of an update whose entries are a fraction of a step (in my toy simulation, at \(\|\Delta W\|/\|W\| = 5\%\) only 2.4% of codes change and the cosine between recovered and true update is 0.35). Options: keep the adapter separate, dequantize and merge into 16 bits, or merge then re-quantize with a calibrated method and re-evaluate. The adapter was also trained against the NF4 rounding error, so merging into the original unquantized weights changes the function slightly.

Core When does LoRA underperform full finetuning, and what would you change first?

When the data exceeds adapter capacity or the domain shift is large: continued pretraining, code and other domains far from pretraining. Biderman et al. (Llama-2-7B): code CPT at 20B tokens, HumanEval 0.263 for full finetuning against 0.224 for the best LoRA (\(r = 256\)); code instruction data at epoch 4, 0.358 and 0.417 for \(r = 16, 64\) against 0.497 for full finetuning at its epoch-8 peak. Full finetuning's \(\Delta W\) there needs rank 10 to 100 times typical LoRA ranks. Thinking Machines see the same capacity effect, plus a penalty at large batch sizes.

What to change, in order: (1) all linear layers, especially the MLP (attention-only underperforms even at matched parameters); (2) more rank if the data warrants it (rank 16 is about 80 Mbit of capacity at 2 bits per parameter against about 290 Mbit of code instruction data); (3) \(\alpha = 2r\) or a re-swept learning rate (about 10× the full-finetuning value); (4) a smaller batch; (5) if it is really continued pretraining, full finetuning.

Deep-dive Design question: a service hosts 1,000 customer-specific finetunes of Llama-3-8B. How do you train, store and serve them?

Training: LoRA on all linear layers at rank 16 to 64 (QLoRA if the training GPUs are small), learning rate about 10× the full-finetuning rate, swept per dataset type. Each run needs about 17 GB of states rather than 128 GB, so one GPU per job.

Storage: adapters only: 83.9 MB each in bf16 at \(r = 16\), 84 GB for 1,000, against 16 TB as full copies.

Serving: do not merge. Keep one base model resident and adapters in host RAM, fetch those in the running batch, and use a batched-adapter kernel (S-LoRA's unified paging and custom kernels, or Punica's SGMV). Cost: 0.52% extra FLOPs but 16.7% extra bytes per decode step with 32 distinct adapters in a batch, since each is read separately. Cluster requests by adapter where latency allows, and merge only for a customer whose traffic justifies a dedicated replica. Evaluate per customer: LoRA can lose on large or out-of-domain data.

Deep-dive Biderman et al. say LoRA learns less and forgets less; Thinking Machines say LoRA matches full finetuning in RL; Shuttleworth et al. say LoRA forgets in its own way. Reconcile these.

Capacity versus information. Biderman et al. train on up to 20B continued-pretraining tokens or hundreds of millions of instruction tokens. At about 1 bit per token, the 20 Gbit of CPT data exceeds even a rank-256 adapter (1.28 Gbit at 2 bits per parameter), and the roughly 290 Mbit of code instruction data at epoch 4 exceeds rank 16 (80 Mbit) and is about the size of rank 64 (320 Mbit), so LoRA "learns less" where rank is low or data is large. Thinking Machines' RL runs supply about 1 bit per episode: 320,000 bits for their MATH run, far below the 5.2 Mbit of a rank-1 adapter (2.6M parameters at 2 bits). One rule covers both: LoRA matches full finetuning until the information in the data exceeds adapter capacity.

Forgetting. Low rank keeps the model closer to the base, so LoRA forgets less, even at equal fit on the target task (Shuttleworth et al.). But high-ranking intruder dimensions, nearly orthogonal to the pretrained singular vectors, cause forgetting, and they appear more with \(\alpha = 8\) than \(2r\), with high learning rates, and across sequential tasks, where LoRA can degrade faster than full finetuning. Prescriptions: \(\alpha = 2r\), a moderate learning rate, higher rank, and separate rather than sequentially merged adapters.

Caveats: Thinking Machines is a blog post, RL only with policy gradient and scalar rewards, and the R1-Zero case is a prediction. Biderman et al. use Llama-2-7B and LionW and analyze the spectrum only for continued pretraining. Each finding is one setup.

Deep-dive DoRA, LoRA+ and rsLoRA each claim to improve on LoRA. What does each change, and how would you decide whether any is worth it?

DoRA changes the parametrization: \(W' = m(W_0 + BA)/\|W_0 + BA\|_c\), a trainable magnitude plus a LoRA direction, motivated by LoRA tying magnitude and direction changes together (correlation +0.83 against −0.62 for full finetuning). It reports +1.0 to +4.4 points on commonsense reasoning, depending on the model, at the cost of recomputing the weight norm each step. LoRA+ changes the optimization: \(\eta_B = \lambda \eta_A\) with \(\lambda \gg 1\), from an infinite-width argument; 1 to 2% and up to 2× faster on harder tasks. rsLoRA changes the scale: \(\alpha/\sqrt{r}\), so higher ranks learn at a fixed learning rate.

Decision: LoRA+ and rsLoRA are reparametrizations of the same two-dimensional space (\(\alpha\), init, \(\text{LR}_A\), \(\text{LR}_B\), with two redundancies), so first check whether a well-swept plain LoRA (learning rate, \(\alpha\), rank, all modules) closes the gap; Thinking Machines could not improve on it. DoRA changes the function class and is the one worth an ablation, but its evidence is the authors' runs on one suite with baseline hyperparameters from earlier work, and it costs memory. Run the same sweep on your data, compare at matched trainable parameters and tuning budget, and check forgetting as well as target accuracy.

Cheat sheet

  • LoRA: \(h = W_0 x + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BAx\), \(A \in \R^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}\) random (uniform \(\pm 1/\sqrt{d_{\text{in}}}\)), \(B \in \R^{d_{\text{out}} \times r}\) zero. Params \(r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}})\) per matrix. At step 0 \(\partial L/\partial A = 0\), only \(B\) moves.
  • Llama-3-8B, \(r = 16\), all 7 linear matrices: 1,310,720 per layer, 41,943,040 total (0.52%). \(q, v\) only: 6,815,744.
  • Training state: full finetuning 16 B/param (128.48 GB for 8B). LoRA: 16.06 GB frozen bf16 + 16 B per adapter param (0.67 GB) = 16.73 GB. QLoRA: 5.70 GB base + 0.67 = 6.37 GB. Activations extra, unchanged.
  • \(\alpha/r\): fixed \(\alpha\) keeps the best LR nearly rank-independent (Adam). \((\alpha, \text{init}_A, \text{LR}_A, \text{LR}_B)\) has two redundancies. rsLoRA \(\alpha/\sqrt{r}\); Biderman \(\alpha = 2r\); QLoRA \(r=64, \alpha=16\).
  • Recipe: all linear layers (MLP is 81% of linear params), LR about 10× full finetuning (\(5\times10^{-5}\) to \(5\times10^{-4}\)), rank 16 to 64 for SFT, 256 for hard domains, re-sweep LR when \(\gamma \not\propto 1/r\).
  • NF4: 16 normal-quantile levels, exact 0, blocks of 64, absmax scale; worst-case error \(0.1519 \times\) absmax. Double quantization: 8-bit scales in groups of 256 + FP32: \(4 + 8/64 + 32/(64\cdot 256) = 4.127\) bits vs 4.5.
  • 70B NF4+DQ: linears 68.45 G × 4.127 / 8 = 35.31 GB, + 4.21 GB bf16 embeddings/head = 39.5 GB (141.1 GB in bf16). 65B: about 34.5 GB base, 45.0 GB training total in the paper (48 GB card, batch 1).
  • Merge: exact in fp32 then round; free at inference; one copy per adapter. With NF4: off-grid, small updates rounded away; keep separate or merge in 16-bit and re-evaluate.
  • Limits: LoRA learns less (code CPT 0.224 vs 0.263 HumanEval; rank-16 code SFT 0.358 vs 0.497) and forgets less, conditionally (intruder dimensions; \(\alpha = 8\), high LR, sequential tasks). Rule: LoRA matches full finetuning until data information (about 1 bit/token) exceeds capacity (about 2 bits/param).
  • RL: about 1 bit per episode, so rank-1 LoRA matches full finetuning in Thinking Machines' policy-gradient runs (blog, not peer reviewed). LoRA tolerates large batches less well.
  • Variants: DoRA (magnitude + direction, +1.0 to +4.4 commonsense points, norm cost), LoRA+ (\(\eta_B = \lambda \eta_A\)), adapters (not mergeable, +20 to 30% latency), prefix/prompt tuning (need scale, fragile), (IA)³ (rescaling vectors).
  • Serving: adapter 83.9 MB at \(r=16\); unmerged batched kernels (S-LoRA, Punica SGMV); 32 distinct adapters per batch adds 16.7% bytes per decode step.
