Reward models & preference optimization (DPO family)

P0 ~65 min Prereqs: 19 SFT. Helpful: 22 RL foundations (not required)

After supervised fine-tuning, a model imitates good answers but has never been told which of two answers is better. This page covers how that signal is collected, how a Bradley-Terry reward model learns from it, why optimizing against a learned reward goes wrong, and how Direct Preference Optimization removes the reward model and the RL loop with one change of variables. The DPO derivation is the most frequently asked derivation in post-training interviews, so we do it line by line, then look at where it breaks and at the family of variants (IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO) built to repair it.

What interviewers probe
  • The DPO derivation, from a blank page: the KL-regularized objective, the closed-form optimum with its partition function \(Z(x)\), solving for the reward, why \(Z\) cancels in the Bradley-Terry difference, the loss, and its gradient with the \(\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)\) weight.
  • Assumptions, stated out loud: the optimum is over all policies, not the parametric family; the reference must put mass wherever the optimum does; preferences follow Bradley-Terry; pairs come from (or near) the reference.
  • Reward models: the pairwise loss, LM backbone plus scalar head on the last token, why one epoch, what accuracy is achievable given annotator disagreement, and reward over-optimization (Gao et al.).
  • Failure modes: likelihood displacement (the chosen response's probability can fall), length exploitation, off-policy data, \(\beta\) sensitivity, overfitting to deterministic preferences.
  • The zoo: what problem IPO, KTO, ORPO, SimPO, cDPO/rDPO and online DPO each address, with the exact objectives, and when online RL beats all of them.
  • Calculation: "given these four log-probabilities and \(\beta = 0.1\), what is the loss and the gradient weight?" and "what implicit-reward margin gives loss 0.1?"

Preference data: what is collected, and how noisy it is

Supervised fine-tuning (page 19) shows the model one good response per prompt. It cannot say that response A is better than response B, and it cannot penalize a fluent but wrong answer. Preference optimization 偏好优化 supplies that missing signal. The basic datum is a triple \((x, y_w, y_l)\): a prompt, a chosen (winning) response and a rejected (losing) response, meaning a labeler judged \(y_w \succ y_l\) given \(x\). Comparisons are used instead of absolute scores because people are inconsistent at scoring and much more consistent at ranking two things side by side [Christiano et al. 2017, §1, §3.3].

How pairs are collected

  • Human labelers, ranking. InstructGPT's roughly 40 contractors ranked \(K = 4\) to 9 responses per prompt, giving \(\binom{K}{2}\) (6 to 36) pairs; the reward-model set had 33k training prompts [Ouyang et al. 2022, §3.2, §3.4, §3.5].
  • Human labelers, binary with a strength. Llama 2 chose binary comparisons mainly to maximize prompt diversity, plus a four-level degree (significantly better, better, slightly better, negligibly better or unsure), and collected over 1 million comparisons (1,418,091 in its Meta sets) [Touvron et al. 2023, §3.2.1, Tab. 6]. Llama 3 kept the levels, added an edit step (edited \(\succ\) chosen \(\succ\) rejected), and trained only on pairs rated "significantly better" or "better" [§4.2.1].
  • AI feedback (RLAIF). A feedback model reads two responses and a written principle and picks the better one. Constitutional AI used the normalized log-probability of "(A)" versus "(B)" as a soft label and sampled one of 16 principles per comparison [Bai et al. 2022, §4.1]. UltraFeedback had GPT-4 rate 4 completions per instruction, from a pool of 17 models over 63,967 instructions, from 1 to 5 on four aspects [Cui et al. 2023, §2.2-2.4]. Tulu 3 does the same with a 22-model pool and GPT-4o: chosen = highest mean rating, rejected = a random response with a lower mean rating, 354,192 pairs in the full pool and 271,409 (8B) or 334,302 (70B) in the final DPO mixes [Lambert et al. 2024, §2.3, §5.2, Tab. 15].

The alternatives to human ranking are not free of error. On MT-bench, GPT-4 agreed with human experts 85% of the time on non-tied votes, against 81% agreement between two humans, but LLM judges show position bias (GPT-4 gave the same verdict after swapping the order of two near-identical answers only 65% of the time with the default prompt), verbosity bias and some self-preference [Zheng et al. 2023, §3.3, §4.2, Tab. 2 and 5].

Noise: what accuracy is even possible?

Human preferences are noisy. InstructGPT's training labelers agreed with each other on \(72.6 \pm 1.5\%\) of comparisons, held-out labelers on \(77.3 \pm 1.3\%\) [Ouyang et al. 2022, §3.4]. RewardBench notes that the Anthropic HH and OpenAI summarization test sets have accuracy ceilings of 60 to 70% from annotator disagreement [Lambert et al. 2024, §1]. Llama 2's Helpfulness RM scores 80.7% on "significantly better" pairs but 54.7% on "negligibly better or unsure" [Tab. 8].

Bradley-Terry makes this quantitative. If a pair's true reward gap is \(\Delta\), annotators pick the better response with probability \(\sigma(\Delta)\). Inverting, a pair with agreement \(p\) has gap \(\operatorname{logit}(p)\): \(p = 0.726\) gives \(0.97\) and \(p = 0.75\) gives \(1.10\). Typical gaps are about one nat, so a reward model cannot be much more than 70 to 80% accurate on such data, and a 95% accurate one is probably fitting an artifact.

Two systematic biases matter later. Length: always choosing the longer response agrees with the labels 55.7% of the time on WebGPT, 59.6% on Stack and 63.1% on RLCD [Singhal et al. 2023, Tab. 5]. Who wrote the pair: the model being trained (on-policy), earlier or other models (off-policy), or a pool. The delta between the two responses often matters more than the quality of either: OLMo 3 pairs Qwen3-32B (chosen) with Qwen3-0.6B (rejected), and preference tuning on them helps although supervised fine-tuning on the same chosen responses hurts [Olmo 3, §4.3, Tab. 21].

Problem
We need a training signal for "better", but absolute quality scores from different annotators are uncalibrated and unstable.
Idea
Show two responses to the same prompt and ask which is better. Model the latent scores with Bradley-Terry.
Why it works
A comparison cancels each annotator's private offset and scale, and it is easier and faster to give than a demonstration or a calibrated rating.

Christiano et al. found comparisons easier for humans to provide in some domains, and equally useful [§1]. InstructGPT, Llama 2/3 and Tulu 3 all train on them.

Every RLHF and DPO pipeline in this page. Scalar AI ratings (UltraFeedback, Tulu 3) are converted to pairs by binarizing.

Bradley-Terry reward models

A reward model 奖励模型 \(r_\phi(x, y) \in \R\) maps a prompt and a response to a scalar that should be higher for responses people prefer. The Bradley-Terry model 布拉德利-特里模型 (Bradley and Terry, Biometrika 39(3/4):324-345, 1952) says each response has a latent strength and the probability that \(y_1\) beats \(y_2\) depends only on the strength gap:

\[ P(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{e^{r(x,y_1)}}{e^{r(x,y_1)} + e^{r(x,y_2)}} = \sigma\big(r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)\big) \]

This is the Elo rating model up to a change of units (Elo uses base 10 and 400 points, so a gap of \(\Delta\) here corresponds to \(400\,\Delta/\ln 10\) Elo points): the better player wins with probability \(\sigma(\Delta)\) [Christiano et al. 2017, §2.2.3]. Fitting \(r_\phi\) by maximum likelihood on a dataset of pairs gives the pairwise logistic loss:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{RM}}(\phi) = -\,\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big] = \E\Big[\log\big(1 + e^{-(r_w - r_l)}\big)\Big] \]
Bradley-Terry reward-model loss (InstructGPT Eq. 1 averages this over the \(\binom K2\) pairs of a prompt)

This is logistic regression on the difference of two scalar features. Its gradient with respect to the gap \(\Delta = r_w - r_l\) is \(-\sigma(-\Delta)\): large when the model ranks the pair wrongly, small once it is confident and right. With equal rewards the loss is \(\log 2 = 0.693\) (chance). With \(r_w = 1.2\) and \(r_l = 0.4\), \(\Delta = 0.8\), the model assigns probability \(\sigma(0.8) = 0.690\) to the correct order and the loss is \(\log(1 + e^{-0.8}) = 0.371\).

Two consequences of "differences only" recur throughout the page. First, rewards are defined only up to a prompt-dependent shift: replacing \(r(x,y)\) by \(r(x,y) + f(x)\) leaves every probability unchanged. InstructGPT therefore adds a bias so that labeler demonstrations score 0 on average before RL [§3.5]. Second, rewards from different prompts are not comparable unless something anchors them, which is why the DPO implicit reward (below) is only meaningful within a prompt.

Architecture: LM backbone plus a scalar head

A reward model is usually a copy of a pretrained or SFT decoder with the unembedding \(d \to V\) removed and a linear head \(d \to 1\) added. InstructGPT describes starting from the SFT model with the unembedding layer removed [§3.5] (its App. C.2 adds that the final 6B RM was initialized from GPT-3 fine-tuned on public NLP datasets, with similar results from GPT-3 or SFT), and Llama 2 from the pretrained chat checkpoint, so that the reward model "knows what the chat model knows" and does not reward hallucinations the policy cannot detect [Touvron et al. 2023, §3.2.2]. The reward is read at the last token of the concatenated prompt and response. Under causal attention that position is the only one whose hidden state has attended to everything.

import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class RewardModel(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, backbone, d):              # backbone: decoder body returning hidden states (B, T, d)
        super().__init__()
        self.backbone = backbone
        self.head = nn.Linear(d, 1)               # replaces the (d -> V) unembedding

    def forward(self, input_ids, attention_mask):
        h = self.backbone(input_ids, attention_mask)             # (B, T, d), causal attention
        last = attention_mask.sum(-1) - 1                        # index of last real token (right padding)
        h_last = h[torch.arange(h.size(0)), last]                # (B, d): sees prompt and full response
        return self.head(h_last).squeeze(-1)                     # (B,) one scalar per (x, y)

def bt_loss(r_w, r_l, margin=0.0):
    return -F.logsigmoid(r_w - r_l - margin).mean()              # = softplus(-(r_w - r_l - margin))
From your jet tagger

Your tagger read a whole jet through a bidirectional encoder and pooled it into one classification logit, permutation-invariantly. A reward model is a classifier on a sequence, but built on a causal decoder, so the "pooling" is simply the final position: it is the one place where information from all earlier tokens has been aggregated, and its value depends on token order. The loss is also different in kind: not cross-entropy against a class label but a logistic loss on the difference of two forward passes.

Training recipes and tricks that matter

  • One epoch. Reward models overfit fast. Llama 2 trained one epoch (learning rate \(5\times10^{-6}\) for 70B, \(10^{-5}\) otherwise, 512 pairs per batch) [§3.2.2], InstructGPT one epoch at \(9\times10^{-6}\) [App. C.2], which found multiple epochs quickly overfit. Treat one epoch as the default, not a law: RewardBench 2 found that a second epoch helped some setups [Malik et al. 2025, §4].
  • All pairs of a prompt in one batch element. Pairs from one ranking task are strongly correlated; shuffling all \(\binom K2\) into one dataset reuses each completion \(K-1\) times and overfits within one pass. InstructGPT puts a prompt's pairs in one batch element, which also needs \(K\) forward passes instead of \(\binom K2\) [§3.5].
  • Reward margin. If labelers give a strength, subtract a margin, \(-\log\sigma(r_w - r_l - m(\text{rating}))\). In Llama 2, average Meta Helpfulness accuracy went from 62.5 (no margin) to 63.0 (small) and 62.9 (large), with the large margin better on separable pairs and worse on similar ones [App. A.3.3, Tab. 27-28]. Llama 3 dropped it "as we observe diminishing improvements after data scaling" [§4.1.2].
  • Separate models for separate objectives. Llama 2 trained a helpfulness RM and a safety RM because the two objectives trade off, and combined them with a safety threshold of 0.15 on the safety score [§3.2.2, §3.2.3].
  • Ensembles and label-noise robustness. Christiano et al. averaged an ensemble of normalized predictors and assumed a 10% chance the human answers uniformly at random, an adjustment "needed because human raters have a constant probability of making an error" [§2.2.3]. Conservative ensembles come next, label smoothing in the variants section.

Evaluating a reward model

The basic metric is pairwise accuracy on held-out pairs: the fraction with \(r(x,y_w) > r(x,y_l)\). Because of annotator noise it saturates at 70 to 80%, so it says little beyond a point. RewardBench builds about 3,000 prompt-chosen-rejected trios with subtle, verifiable differences (bugs, wrong facts, refusals) in four groups (Chat, Chat Hard, Safety, Reasoning), plus prior test sets at weight 0.5 [Lambert et al. 2024, §4, Tab. 1]. DPO-trained models can be scored through their implicit reward, but only with the right reference: a similar-but-wrong reference dropped accuracy to about chance [App. B]. RewardBench 2 (2025) has 1,865 cases in six subsets and scores about 20 points lower. Its score correlates strongly with best-of-n performance (Pearson 0.87 over 113 reward models), but it is only a rough guide for PPO: PPO results saturate for decent reward models from the policy's own lineage and drop when the reward model is off-policy [Malik et al. 2025, §5]. Tulu 3 likewise reports that strong reward-model benchmark scores do not necessarily translate into better PPO results [§5.4.1].

Reward over-optimization

A reward model is a proxy: it was fit on a finite, noisy set of comparisons and is only trustworthy near the distribution it saw. Optimize a policy hard against it and the policy finds responses the proxy scores highly and people do not. This is Goodhart's law 古德哈特定律 ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"), and in RLHF it is called reward over-optimization 奖励过优化. The classic symptom is a policy whose proxy score keeps rising while its real quality peaks and then declines, often with responses getting longer and more repetitive.

The measurement: Gao et al. (2022)

Real humans are expensive, so Gao et al. use a synthetic setup. A fixed 6B "gold" reward model (the InstructGPT RM) plays the labelers. It labels 100,000 synthetic comparisons (10% held out), always preferring the higher gold score, and these train proxy reward models of 3M to 3B parameters. A 1.2B policy (6B in one ablation) is optimized against a proxy by best-of-\(n\) or PPO, and the gold score is tracked against how far the policy moved [Gao et al. 2022, §2, §2.1]. Distance is \(d = \sqrt{\KL(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{init}})}\). For best-of-\(n\) it is analytic, \(\KL_{\text{bon}} = \log n - \tfrac{n-1}{n}\): \(n = 1000\) gives \(6.908 - 0.999 = 5.91\) nats and \(n = 60{,}000\) gives \(11.002 - 1.000 = 10.00\). The fitted forms are:

\[ R_{\text{bon}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\text{bon}} - \beta_{\text{bon}}\, d), \qquad R_{\text{RL}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\text{RL}} - \beta_{\text{RL}} \log d) \]
Gold reward against \(d = \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}\) (Gao et al. §1). The RL form has infinite slope at \(d = 0\), so it is not meant to hold near the origin.

Both curves rise, peak and fall. Setting the derivative to zero gives the peak location and height:

\[ R_{\text{bon}}' = \alpha - 2\beta d = 0 \Rightarrow d^\star = \frac{\alpha}{2\beta},\ R^\star = \frac{\alpha^2}{4\beta}; \qquad R_{\text{RL}}' = \alpha - \beta\log d - \beta = 0 \Rightarrow d^\star = e^{\alpha/\beta - 1},\ R^\star = \beta\, d^\star \]

Gao et al. use the same idea to predict peak gold scores (their Fig. 12); the closed forms above are our algebra on their fitted forms. A smaller \(\beta\) (a more robust reward model) pushes the peak out, and in KL terms the peak sits at \((d^\star)^2\).

What they found

  • Coefficients scale smoothly with reward-model size (roughly logarithmically; \(\alpha_{\text{RL}}\) is nearly constant), so bigger reward models over-optimize later [§1, §3.2]. Below about 2,000 comparisons the models were near chance [§3.3].
  • Policy size hardly matters. A 6B policy gains less than a 1.2B one, but its gold reward peaks at almost the same KL [§3.4].
  • RL spends KL less efficiently than best-of-\(n\). Best-of-\(n\) KL grows like \(\log n\), while RL's grows roughly quadratically with steps, so KL is a poor common yardstick across methods [§3.5, §4.1].
  • The KL penalty acts like early stopping. Varying it changed how soon a run converged but not the gold-versus-KL frontier (the authors caution this may depend on hyperparameters) [§3.6].

Gao et al. map the curves onto Goodhart's taxonomy. The proxy is roughly linear in \(\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}\), and the slope gap between it and the linear part of gold (the \(\alpha\) term) is regressional Goodhart: optimization power selects noise in the proxy. The downward bend (\(\beta\)) is mostly extremal Goodhart: the policy leaves the region where the reward model is accurate. Adversarial Goodhart is not captured because their models are too weak [§4.2]. Length is the standard real-world case: PPO with a reward that was only a length target reproduced most of the simulated-preference win rate of PPO with a learned reward (56% versus 58% on WebGPT) [Singhal et al. 2023, Tab. 2].

Mitigations

  • Bigger reward model, more and cleaner data, and keeping the policy near the RM's training distribution. Llama 2 collected fresh preference data each round because "reward model accuracy can quickly degrade if not exposed to this new sample distribution" [§3.2.1]. Gao et al. derive that with \(k\) rounds of fresh labels the gold reward rises by \(\beta_{\text{RL}}\, d \log k\), assuming constant coefficients and additive \(d\) across rounds, and only up to some maximum \(k\) [§4.3].
  • Ensembles with a conservative objective. Train several reward models and score with the minimum (worst case) or mean minus \(\lambda\) times variance. With five 44M proxies, a 7B gold model and 25% label noise, this raised best-of-\(n\) gold reward by about 75% over the average single model, and under PPO it removed over-optimization at a KL penalty of 0.01, where a single model needed 0.2 [Coste et al. 2023, §5.1, §5.2].
  • Early stopping on a held-out gold signal, length controls, and a KL penalty (below).
  • Direct methods over-optimize too. DPO, IPO and SLiC at seven values of \(\beta\) show the same hump: win rate against the reference rises then falls as KL grows, and wide-KL runs peak after about a quarter of an epoch. IPO was the least prone [Rafailov et al. 2024, §3.1-3.2]. Skipping the reward model removes the proxy, not the problem.
A KL penalty toward the reference policy
Problem
Maximizing a learned reward exploits it, and also erodes fluency and diversity, since the reward model has no opinion about text far from its training data.
Idea
Maximize \(\E[r] - \beta\,\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\), where \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the SFT model.
Why it works
It keeps the policy where the reward is informative, keeps entropy, and, crucially for the next two sections, has a closed-form optimum.
Evidence
Used in InstructGPT (\(\beta = 0.02\), [App. C.4]) and Llama 2 (\(\beta = 0.01\) for 7B and 13B, \(0.005\) for 34B and 70B, [§3.2.3]). But Gao et al. find it behaves like early stopping rather than a better frontier, and Coste et al. find it must be 20 times larger than with an ensemble.
Cost
One more hyperparameter, a reference model in memory (or cached log-probabilities), and the KL is measured over whole sequences in nats.
Used by
PPO-based RLHF and, implicitly through \(\beta\), DPO and its variants.

The KL-regularized objective and its closed-form optimum

Treat generation as a one-step decision at the sequence level: for prompt \(x\), the policy \(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\) is a distribution over complete responses \(y\), and \(\pi(y|x) = \prod_t \pi(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\). The standard RLHF objective is

\[ J(\pi) = \E_{x}\Big[\ \E_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)}\, r(x,y) \;-\; \beta\, \KL\big(\pi(\cdot|x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big)\Big], \qquad \beta > 0 \]
KL-regularized reward maximization (DPO paper Eq. 3). \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is usually the SFT model, and \(\pi\) is initialized from it.

We now derive its optimum exactly, with no calculus of variations. Every step below is one an interviewer will want to see.

  1. Decouple prompts. \(J\) is an average over \(x\) of terms that each depend only on \(\pi(\cdot|x)\). If the policy is unrestricted, maximize each prompt's term separately. Fix \(x\) and drop it from the notation.
  2. Write the KL as an expectation under \(\pi\). \(\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) = \E_{y\sim\pi}[\log \pi(y)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)]\), so \[ \E_\pi[r] - \beta\,\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\beta\,\E_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} - \frac{r(y)}{\beta}\Big]. \] Maximizing \(J\) is the same as minimizing \(F(\pi) = \E_\pi\big[\log \frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} - \frac r\beta\big]\).
  3. Define a normalized distribution that absorbs the reward. Let \[ Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\, e^{r(x,y)/\beta}, \qquad \pi^\star(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}. \] \(\pi^\star\) is nonnegative and sums to 1 by construction, so it is a valid distribution. \(Z(x)\) is the partition function 配分函数. Taking logs, \(\log\pi^\star = \log\pi_{\text{ref}} + r/\beta - \log Z\), hence \(\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} - \frac r\beta = \log\frac{\pi}{\pi^\star} - \log Z\).
  4. Substitute. \(F(\pi) = \E_\pi[\log \pi/\pi^\star] - \log Z = \KL(\pi\|\pi^\star) - \log Z\). Multiplying by \(-\beta\) gives an exact identity for every policy: \[ J(\pi) \;=\; \beta \log Z(x) \;-\; \beta\,\KL\big(\pi\,\|\,\pi^\star\big). \]
  5. Read off the optimum. \(\log Z\) does not depend on \(\pi\), and \(\KL \ge 0\) with equality if and only if \(\pi = \pi^\star\) (Gibbs' inequality). So the unique maximizer is \(\pi^\star\), the maximum value is \(\beta\log Z(x)\), and any other policy loses exactly \(\beta\,\KL(\pi\|\pi^\star)\) relative to it.
\[ \pi^\star(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big), \qquad Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Big) \]
Closed-form optimum (DPO paper Eq. 4, App. A.1): the reference policy, exponentially tilted by the reward

The Lagrangian route gives the same answer: differentiate \(\sum_y \pi(y)[r(y) - \beta\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}] + \lambda(\sum_y \pi(y) - 1)\) with respect to each \(\pi(y)\), get \(\pi(y) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}\), and let normalization define \(Z\). The argument above shows more: it proves optimality and prices every suboptimal policy. \(\beta\) is also the multiplier of "maximize \(\E[r]\) subject to \(\KL \le \epsilon\)".

Assumptions hidden in these five lines

(1) The maximization is over all distributions, not what a transformer can represent, so any trained \(\pi_\theta\) only approximates \(\pi^\star\). (2) Support: \(\pi^\star(y|x) = 0\) wherever \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) = 0\), and policies with mass outside that support have infinite KL. The inversion in the next section, \(r = \beta\log(\pi^\star/\pi_{\text{ref}}) + \beta\log Z\), is only defined on the reference's support, so rewards of responses it cannot produce are unidentified. A softmax LM gives every sequence positive probability, but the loss only touches ratios at dataset responses, so coverage is what matters. (3) \(Z(x)\) is finite (true for bounded \(r\)). (4) The KL is over whole sequences; by the chain rule it is the expected sum of per-token KLs, so \(\beta\) is a per-sequence price. (5) \(r\) is a fixed function; if learned, its errors are inherited.

Check the identity. At \(\beta = 1\): \(\E_{\pi^\star}[r] = 0.292 + 2(0.529) = 1.350\), \(\KL(\pi^\star\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) = 0.323\), so \(J(\pi^\star) = 1.350 - 0.323 = 1.0272\), and \(\beta \log Z = \log 2.7933 = 1.0272\). The reference policy itself scores \(J = 0.7\), and a plausible-looking rival \((0.2, 0.3, 0.5)\) scores \(1.0251\), just below the optimum, as the identity predicts. Smaller \(\beta\) concentrates mass on the highest-reward response (\(\beta = 0.5\) puts 0.80 on \(y_3\)), while larger \(\beta\) tilts less (at \(\beta = 2\) the mass of \(y_1\) has only fallen from 0.5 to 0.325, and as \(\beta\to\infty\) we recover \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)). Also, \(\beta\log Z(x) = \beta \log \E_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[e^{r/\beta}]\) is a soft maximum of the reward: \(\E_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[r]\) as \(\beta\to\infty\), \(\max_y r\) as \(\beta \to 0\).

Why not just use \(\pi^\star\) directly? Because \(Z(x)\) sums over every possible response: with \(V = 128{,}256\) tokens and 200-token responses, \(V^{200}\) terms, and a Monte Carlo estimate from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) has huge variance since \(e^{r/\beta}\) is heavy-tailed. DPO's trick removes exactly this constant.

Deriving Direct Preference Optimization

Direct Preference Optimization 直接偏好优化 (DPO) rests on two facts we already have. The Bradley-Terry model needs only differences of rewards. And the optimum of the KL-regularized problem is an explicit function of the reward. Solve the second for the reward and substitute it into the first: the intractable \(Z(x)\) sits in a term that differences cannot see. Figure 3 is the whole argument; the numbered steps below are the version to write on a whiteboard.

  1. Preference model (Bradley-Terry). \(p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\big(r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2)\big)\), where \(r^*\) is an unobserved latent reward (DPO paper Eq. 1).
  2. Optimal policy for a reward \(r\). \(\pi_r(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}\) (Eq. 4, derived in the previous section).
  3. Invert. Take the log of step 2: \(\log\pi_r = \log\pi_{\text{ref}} + r/\beta - \log Z\). Solve for \(r\): \[ r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta\log Z(x) \qquad \text{(Eq. 5)} \] This holds for every reward function, with \(\pi_r\) its own optimum. Check it on the three-response example of the last section: \(\log(\pi^\star/\pi_{\text{ref}}) = (-1.0272,\ -0.0272,\ +0.9728)\), and adding \(\log Z = 1.0272\) returns exactly \(r = (0, 1, 2)\). The constant \(\log Z\) is what makes the rewards come back.
  4. Substitute into Bradley-Terry; \(Z\) cancels. For the same prompt \(x\), \[ r^*(x,y_w) - r^*(x,y_l) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} + \underbrace{\beta\log Z(x) - \beta\log Z(x)}_{=\,0}, \] so \(p^*(y_w \succ y_l|x) = \sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\Big)\) (Eq. 6).
  5. Maximum likelihood over policies. Replace the unknown \(\pi^*\) by a parametric \(\pi_\theta\) and minimize the negative log-likelihood of the observed pairs (Eq. 7):
\[ \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\,\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\right)\right] \]
The DPO loss: Bradley-Terry with the implicit reward \(\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\)

Why \(Z\) really cancels, and why step 5 loses nothing

The algebraic reason is one line: \(\log Z(x)\) depends on the prompt only, and both responses answer the same prompt. The structural reason: Bradley-Terry is invariant to \(r \to r + f(x)\), and \(\beta\log Z(x)\) is exactly such an \(f(x)\). DPO calls two rewards equivalent when they differ by a prompt-only function (Def. 1) and proves that equivalent rewards give the same preference distribution (Lemma 1) and the same optimal policy (Lemma 2). So the one quantity Bradley-Terry cannot see is the intractable one, and the RL problem ignores it too.

Step 5 is legitimate because of Theorem 1: every reward class consistent with Bradley-Terry (or Plackett-Luce) contains a member of the form \(r = \beta\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\), namely the one with \(Z(x) = 1\). For it, \(\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}e^{\hat r/\beta} = \sum_y \pi_\theta = 1\), so the optimal policy of \(\hat r_\theta\) is \(\pi_\theta\) itself. Minimizing \(\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}\) over policies is thus fitting a Bradley-Terry reward model over all reward classes, with the KL-optimal policy attached for free: the policy is the reward model ("your language model is secretly a reward model"). This is a statement about the unrestricted optimum. Whether gradient descent on finite data finds such a policy is empirical, and the next section is about how it fails.

Rankings (Plackett-Luce). For \(K\) responses ranked \(y_{\tau(1)} \succ \dots \succ y_{\tau(K)}\), as in InstructGPT's labeling, Plackett-Luce gives \(p(\tau) = \prod_{k=1}^{K} \exp r(y_{\tau(k)}) \big/ \sum_{j \ge k} \exp r(y_{\tau(j)})\), which is Bradley-Terry at \(K=2\). Every factor is a softmax over responses to one prompt, so it is invariant to \(r \to r + f(x)\): the \(\beta\log Z(x)\) terms cancel exactly as before, and the loss is \(-\E\log\prod_{k}\exp\hat r_\theta(y_{\tau(k)}) \big/ \sum_{j\ge k}\exp\hat r_\theta(y_{\tau(j)})\) with the same implicit reward [Rafailov et al. 2023, App. A.3, Eq. 20]. Lemma 1 and Theorem 1 are stated for this case too. In practice most pipelines binarize rankings or ratings into pairs.

From sequence probabilities to code

\(\log\pi_\theta(y|x) = \sum_{t}\log\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\), summed over response tokens only. For plain DPO the prompt's terms are identical in the chosen and rejected sums and cancel, so masking them does not change the loss. It matters for per-response quantities (length normalization, an NLL term), and padding must always be masked. Llama 3 also masks formatting tokens (headers, end-of-turn) from both responses: they appear in both, so the contrastive loss asks the model to raise and lower them at once, which Meta observed can lead to tail repetition and abrupt terminations [Llama 3, §4.1.4].

def sequence_logprob(logits, labels, mask):
    # logits: (B, T, V) aligned so logits[:, t] predicts labels[:, t]; mask: (B, T), 1 on response tokens
    logp = torch.log_softmax(logits.float(), -1)                          # fp32 for stability
    tok = logp.gather(-1, labels.clamp(min=0).unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)  # (B, T), -100 labels are safe
    return (tok * mask).sum(-1)                                           # (B,)

def dpo_step(policy, ref, chosen_ids, rejected_ids, chosen_mask, rejected_mask, beta=0.1):
    ids = torch.cat([chosen_ids, rejected_ids])                           # (2B, T): one forward pass for both
    mask = torch.cat([chosen_mask, rejected_mask])[:, 1:]                 # shift: token t is predicted at t-1
    lp = sequence_logprob(policy(ids).logits[:, :-1], ids[:, 1:], mask)   # (2B,), gradients flow
    with torch.no_grad():                                                 # frozen reference; can be precomputed
        ref_lp = sequence_logprob(ref(ids).logits[:, :-1], ids[:, 1:], mask)
    pi_w, pi_l = lp.chunk(2); ref_w, ref_l = ref_lp.chunk(2)
    margin = beta * ((pi_w - ref_w) - (pi_l - ref_l))                     # implicit reward of chosen minus rejected
    return -F.logsigmoid(margin).mean(), (margin > 0).float().mean()      # loss, implicit-reward accuracy

The full drill, with the IPO, SimPO and ORPO variants and tests, is exercises/drills/dpo.py. Tulu 3 precomputes and caches the reference log-probabilities instead of keeping a reference model on the GPU [§5.4.2].

The gradient and what it does per example

Let \(u = \hat r_\theta(x,y_w) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\), so \(\mathcal L = -\log\sigma(u)\). Then \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u = -\sigma(-u)\), and \(\nabla_\theta u = \beta\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w|x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l|x)\big)\). Since \(\sigma(-u) = \sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)\):

\[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta\,\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[\underbrace{\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)}_{\text{higher weight when the implicit reward ranks the pair wrongly}}\Big(\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}_{\text{increase chosen}} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}_{\text{decrease rejected}}\Big)\Big] \]
DPO gradient (Rafailov et al. §4, "What does the DPO update do?")
  • Direction. The update points toward higher log-probability of \(y_w\) and lower of \(y_l\), with equal coefficients, the same for every token of a response. The \(y_l\) term is an unlikelihood update. The realized change of \(\log\pi_\theta(y_w)\) can still be negative when the two gradients overlap (see likelihood displacement).
  • Weight. \(\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w) \in (0,1)\). At initialization \(\pi_\theta = \pi_{\text{ref}}\), both implicit rewards are 0 and the weight is exactly \(0.5\). It approaches 1 for pairs the model ranks wrongly and decays toward 0 as the margin grows. This built-in curriculum matters: the DPO paper reports that a "naive" version without the weight can make the model degenerate [Rafailov et al. 2023, §4, App. Tab. 3].
  • \(\beta\). The gradient is \(\beta\) times the weight, and the weight is a function of the margin in reward units \(\beta\Delta\), where \(\Delta\) is the difference of log-ratios. A large \(\beta\) saturates the weight after a small log-ratio move, so the policy stays near the reference; a small \(\beta\) lets it travel far (the next section quantifies this).
  • No reward model, no sampling. The gradient uses only four sequence log-probabilities per pair. That is DPO's whole cost advantage and, as we will see, the source of its off-policy weakness.

The token-level view fits in a paragraph. Since \(\log\pi(y|x) = \sum_t \log\pi(y_t|x,y_{<t})\), the implicit reward is a sum of per-token log-ratios. Rafailov et al. (2024) re-derive DPO in the token-level MDP: the log-ratio is an optimal advantage for some reward, the shared prompt's value \(V^*(s_0)\) cancels as \(Z(x)\) did, and per-token implicit rewards qualitatively highlight the erroneous tokens of a summary [From r to Q*, §4-5.1].

Off-policy data

DPO's pairs were written by some policy \(\mu\) (the SFT model, a mix of models, a human), not by the current \(\pi_\theta\). Nothing in the loss looks at what \(\pi_\theta\) generates, so mass can move to responses no pair compared. Xu et al. prove that every policy PPO can find with a learned reward also minimizes the DPO loss, but not conversely. Their example: with \(\pi_{\text{ref}} = (0.5, 0.5, 0)\) and one pair \(y_1 \succ y_2\), the DPO loss is \(\log\big(1 + (\pi(y_2)/\pi(y_1))^\beta\big)\), minimized by \(\pi(y_2) \to 0\) whatever happens to \(y_3\), so \((0.1, 0, 0.9)\) is a global minimizer although the reference gives \(y_3\) probability zero. PPO cannot produce it, since \(\pi^\star \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\) [Xu et al. 2024, Thm. 4.1, Tab. 1]. DPO is also more sensitive than PPO to swapping the reference for one closer to or farther from the data [App. C.1].

Tang et al. compare online and offline algorithms at equal KL on four tasks and find online generally ahead, with a higher peak. Data coverage alone did not explain it: offline policies were better at pairwise classification but worse at generation. An offline algorithm on a repeatedly refreshed dataset is "effectively an online algorithm" [Tang et al. 2024, §1, §6, §8]. Tajwar et al. find on-policy sampling and a negative gradient (pushing rejected responses down) each help and are complementary [§5].

The reference model

\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) plays three roles: it anchors the KL, it defines the zero point of the implicit reward (\(\hat r = 0\) when \(\pi_\theta = \pi_{\text{ref}}\)), and it is assumed to be the distribution that generated the pairs. When the pairs come from other models, as in UltraFeedback, the third role is violated. The DPO paper's remedy is to first fine-tune on the chosen responses and use that as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) [§4]. The reference is not decoration (a wrong one drops implicit-reward accuracy to about chance, above). Its cost is one extra forward pass per sequence, avoided by caching reference log-probabilities (Tulu 3) or by reference-free objectives (SimPO, ORPO), which give up the anchor.

\(\beta\) and other hyperparameter sensitivity

DPO is often called tuning-free, but \(\beta\), the learning rate, the epochs and the data size interact. In Tulu 3's ablation (length-normalized DPO, learning rate \(5\times10^{-7}\), average over their suite) \(\beta = 2\) scored 46.8, \(\beta = 5\) scored 53.4 and \(\beta = 10\) scored 55.2 after 3 epochs, against 55.7 for the SFT start, while \(\beta = 5\) with one epoch scored 57.3 [Tab. 18]. The KTO paper warns that good DPO settings do not carry over even to analogous hyperparameters such as \(\beta\) [§4.2], and smaller \(\beta\) gives longer answers [Park et al. 2024, §4.2].

Length exploitation

Preference data lean long (55.7 to 63.1% of pairs favor the longer response), judges lean long, and the sum of token log-ratios grows with length. DPO amplifies this. In Singhal et al.'s runs DPO raised mean length from 59 to 68 tokens (RLCD), 203 to 248 (Stack) and 100 to 164 (WebGPT), and its implicit reward was no more accurate than a separate reward model (78% versus 80%, 62% versus 70%, 57% versus 62%) [App. C.1, Tab. 7]. Park et al. find DPO answers about twice as long as the data [§4.2]. Fixes act on the data (OLMo 3 Instruct caps the chosen-rejected length difference at 100 tokens in its chat and multi-turn pairs [§5.3.1]), on the loss (R-DPO, length-normalized DPO, SimPO), and on evaluation (length-controlled win rates). Normalization is a heuristic: it helps DPO mainly where the data are strongly length-biased (Mistral-Base) and not where lengths are similar (Instruct setting) [Meng et al. 2024, App. I].

Likelihood displacement: why the chosen likelihood can fall

The DPO loss depends only on the difference of the two log-ratios, so it can be lowered by pushing \(y_l\) down more than \(y_w\), even if \(y_w\) also goes down (case C in the table). Nothing in the loss ties \(\log\pi_\theta(y_w|x)\) to increase. Researchers call the resulting fall in the preferred response's probability likelihood displacement. Four complementary explanations, none contradicting the others:

  1. KL accounting (Rafailov et al. 2024). If \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) was fitted to the chosen responses by SFT, then \(\E_{y\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}[\beta\log\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}] = -\beta\,\KL(\pi_{\text{ref}}\|\pi) \le 0\). To the extent the chosen responses look like samples from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), their average implicit reward is expected to fall as the policy moves: "the likelihood of the chosen response should decrease" [From r to Q*, §5.4, Eq. 16]. Their companion study calls this not necessarily harmful, "even necessary for improvement" [§3.5].
  2. Shared tokens (Pal et al.). When chosen and rejected differ in a few tokens, the shared prefix's gradient cancels and later tokens' logits are pushed the wrong way. On MetaMath the mean log-probability of tokens after the first differing one was \(-0.37\) for the reference, \(-1.82\) after DPO, and \(-0.26\) with their fix DPOP [Pal et al. 2024, §3, §5].
  3. Embedding geometry (Razin et al.). Similar responses have similar hidden states, so pushing \(y_l\) down drags \(y_w\) along, and the freed mass goes to tokens whose unembeddings align with the update, possibly of the opposite meaning. With one prompt and one-token responses on OLMo-1B, training to prefer "No" over "Never" took \(\pi(\text{No})\) from 0.85 to 0.01 at its lowest (mean over ten prompts) while "Yes" gained. Training Llama-3-8B-Instruct to refuse unsafe prompts (over 70% of pairs had two refusals) cut its refusal rate on SORRY-Bench training prompts from 74.4% to 33.4% [Razin et al. 2024, §3, §6.2]. Filtering pairs by hidden-embedding similarity (the CHES score) prevented it.
  4. Data dependence (Tajwar et al.). With semantically distinct pairs (UltraFeedback, GPT-4 versus GPT-3.5 answers on Mistral-7B) the chosen likelihood rose while the rejected fell. With Pythia-1.4B on AlpacaFarm both fell [Tajwar et al. 2024, §5.2, Fig. 17].

Explanation 3 fits in a dozen lines. Give a one-token "model" a free hidden state \(h\) (6 tokens, fixed unembeddings \(W\)), make tokens 0 and 1 (chosen, rejected) nearly parallel, and align token 2 with \(W_0 - W_1\). The DPO gradient on \(h\) is exactly along \(W_0 - W_1\), so the logit of token \(k\) moves at rate \(\langle W_k, W_0 - W_1\rangle\):

W = torch.tensor([[2., .3, 0], [2., -.3, 0], [0, 3., 0], [-1, 0, .5], [0, -1, .5], [-.5, -.5, -.5]])  # tokens w, l, z, a, b, c
h0 = torch.tensor([.3, .2, 0.]); ref = torch.log_softmax(W @ h0, -1)
h = h0.clone().requires_grad_(); opt = torch.optim.SGD([h], lr=0.5)
for step in range(100):
    lp = torch.log_softmax(W @ h, -1)
    m = 0.1 * ((lp[0] - ref[0]) - (lp[1] - ref[1]))       # beta = 0.1; token 0 chosen, token 1 rejected
    opt.zero_grad(); torch.nn.functional.softplus(-m).backward(); opt.step()   # softplus(-m) = -log sigmoid(m)

Running this (we did): the loss falls monotonically from 0.693 to 0.650 and the log-ratio gap grows from 0 to 0.88 nats, yet \(\pi(w)\) falls from 0.248 to 0.020, \(\pi(l)\) from 0.220 to 0.007, and the unrelated token \(z\) absorbs the mass, rising from 0.233 to 0.965. The first-order change of \(\log\pi_w\) is \(c\,[\langle W_w, W_w - W_l\rangle - \E_\pi\langle W_k, W_w - W_l\rangle] = c\,[0.18 - 0.33] < 0\): the first term is small because \(W_w\) and \(W_l\) are nearly parallel, and the second is large because \(z\) carries probability and is aligned with the update. This is our construction in the "unconstrained features" setting of Razin et al., not a claim about a real LLM.

Mitigations. An NLL term on the chosen response (Llama 3: coefficient 0.2, "preventing the decrease of log probability of chosen responses" [§4.1.4]); DPOP's penalty \(\lambda\max(0, \log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}{\pi_\theta(y_w)})\) inside the sigmoid; filtering similar pairs (CHES; Razin et al. found this beat adding an SFT term); on-policy pairs, and fewer epochs.

Overfitting to deterministic preferences (the IPO argument)

If annotators always prefer \(y\) to \(y'\), Bradley-Terry needs \(r(y) - r(y') \to \infty\), so \(\pi^*(y')/\pi^*(y) \to 0\) for any \(\beta\): the more deterministic the preferences, the weaker the KL regularization. With finite data it is worse, since a true preference of 0.8 observed once or twice can look like 1. In Azar et al.'s two-action example (uniform reference, \(p^*(y_1 \succ y_2) = 1\)) DPO converges to \(\pi(y_1) = 1\) for every regularization strength, while IPO's optimum is \(\sigma(0.5/\tau)\): 0.622 for \(\tau = 1\), 0.993 for \(\tau = 0.1\) [Azar et al. 2023, §4.2, §5.3.1]. Their experiments are small bandit problems and IPO does not consistently beat DPO on LLM benchmarks (below), so read this as a theoretical warning, not proof of practical superiority.

IPO: a squared loss to a target margin

IPO (Identity Preference Optimization) regresses \(h_\theta\) onto \(1/(2\tau)\), so "the weaker the regularisation becomes, the higher would be the log-likelihood ratio" of \(y_w\) to \(y_l\) [Azar et al. 2023, Eq. 17]. It comes from taking the ΨPO mapping \(\Psi\) to be the identity, so preferences enter as probabilities, not Bradley-Terry log-odds, which avoids the unbounded target. \(\tau\) plays the role of \(\beta\). The behavioral difference is that the loss is two-sided. On pair A with \(\tau = 0.1\) the target is 5 nats and \(h = 8\), so the loss is \((8-5)^2 = 9\) and the gradient \(2(h - 5) = 6\) pulls the gap back down, while DPO's weight there is still 0.31 and keeps pushing up. IPO is less prone to over-optimization [Rafailov et al. 2024, §3.1], but the SimPO table has it below DPO in all four settings (AlpacaEval 2 length-controlled 11.8 versus 15.1 on Mistral-Base, 35.6 versus 40.3 on Llama-3-Instruct) [Meng et al. 2024, Tab. 4].

cDPO and rDPO: noisy labels

If each label is flipped with probability \(\varepsilon\), the labeled loss is a mixture. cDPO (conservative DPO, label smoothing) uses \((1-\varepsilon)\ell(h) + \varepsilon\ell(-h)\), which is biased. rDPO uses \(\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\ell(h) - \varepsilon\ell(-h)}{1-2\varepsilon}\), whose expectation over the flips equals the clean loss, at the price of knowing \(\varepsilon\) and a \(1/(1-2\varepsilon)\) factor in the guarantee [Chowdhury et al. 2024, Lemma 3.1, Thm. 4.2]. The gradient weights are DPO \(\sigma(-m)\), cDPO \(\sigma(-m) - \varepsilon\), rDPO \(\sigma(-m) + \frac{\varepsilon}{1-2\varepsilon}\) [Lemma 3.2]. At margin \(m = 0.8\), \(\varepsilon = 0.1\): \(0.310\), \(0.210\), \(0.435\). rDPO pushes harder on correct-looking pairs because it knows a fraction are flipped.

KTO: unpaired binary feedback

KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimization) needs only a binary label "desirable / undesirable" per output. Its value function is a logistic curve around a reference point \(z_0 = \KL(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\), estimated per microbatch by pairing each prompt with another example's response (\(\hat z_0 = \max(0, \tfrac1m\sum_i \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_j|x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j|x_i)})\), not backpropagated), with loss aversion set by \(\lambda_D, \lambda_U\) [Ethayarajh et al. 2024, §4.1]. The authors report matching or exceeding DPO from 1B to 30B, and matching it with up to 90% fewer desirable examples; they recommend a learning rate 2 to 10 times DPO's, \(\beta = 0.1\) and \(\lambda_D n_D / \lambda_U n_U \in [1, 3/2]\) [§1, §4.2, §4.3]. Caveats: these are the authors' own comparisons; KTO ignores examples with extreme implied reward, which protects against noise but can underfit hard data (their Prop. 4.1); and in SimPO's benchmark its length-controlled AlpacaEval 2 score is below DPO's in all four settings [Tab. 4].

ORPO: preference learning folded into SFT

ORPO (Odds Ratio Preference Optimization) trains once from a base model on \(\mathcal L_{\text{NLL}}(y_w) + \lambda\,\mathcal L_{\text{OR}}\), with \(\mathcal L_{\text{OR}} = -\log\sigma(\log\frac{\text{odds}_\theta(y_w|x)}{\text{odds}_\theta(y_l|x)})\): no reference model and no separate SFT stage. Here \(P_\theta(y|x)\) is the length-normalized probability \(\exp(\frac1m\sum_t\log P(y_t|\cdot))\) [Hong et al. 2024, Eq. 3-7]. Its gradient is \(\nabla\mathcal L_{\text{OR}} = -\delta(d)\cdot h(d)\) with \(\delta = (1 + \text{OR})^{-1}\) for the odds ratio \(\text{OR} = \text{odds}_\theta(y_w)/\text{odds}_\theta(y_l)\), and \(h = \frac{\nabla\log P_w}{1-P_w} - \frac{\nabla\log P_l}{1-P_l}\) (the paper's Eq. 8 drops the minus sign; its App. A differentiates \(\log\sigma\) rather than \(-\log\sigma\), and we checked the sign with autograd): like DPO, a weight that shrinks once the pair is ranked correctly, plus a contrast term amplified when a probability is low. The authors report Mistral-ORPO-\(\beta\) at 12.20% on AlpacaEval 2.0 [§4.3, §6.1, Tab. 1]. In SimPO's table its length-controlled score is below DPO's in all four settings (14.7 versus 15.1 on Mistral-Base, 28.5 versus 40.3 on Llama-3-Instruct) [Tab. 4]. On a pair with mean log-probs \(-0.65\) and \(-1.10\): \(P = 0.522, 0.333\), log odds ratio \(0.783\), \(\mathcal L_{\text{OR}} = 0.376\), \(\delta = 0.314\).

SimPO: a reference-free, length-normalized reward

SimPO argues that DPO's reward is not what generation optimizes, which is roughly the average log-likelihood. Among training pairs that DPO's reward ranks correctly, almost half (about 21.2k of 44.2k) have the chosen response at lower average log-likelihood [Meng et al. 2024, §4.4, Fig. 4b]. So it uses \(r(x,y) = \frac{\beta}{|y|}\log\pi_\theta(y|x)\), adds a target margin \(\gamma\) to Bradley-Terry, and drops the reference (Eq. 4-6; \(\beta \in [2.0, 2.5]\), \(\gamma \in [0.3, 1.6]\) searched). On Mistral-Base, removing length normalization drops the length-controlled AlpacaEval 2 win rate from 21.5 to 11.9 and \(\gamma = 0\) to 16.8, against 15.1 for DPO [Tab. 4-5]. Dropping the reference drops the KL anchor; the authors argue low learning rates and diverse data keep KL small. The evidence is mixed: Tulu 3 found SimPO (51.8 and 52.9 in two settings) worse than its SFT start (55.7) while length-normalized DPO helped [Tab. 18]. Worked example: chosen 120 tokens with total log-prob \(-78\), rejected 300 tokens with \(-190\). The total favors the chosen by 112 nats, but the per-token averages are \(-0.650\) and \(-0.633\), so with \(\beta = 2\), \(\gamma = 0.5\) the margin is \(-0.033 - 0.5 = -0.533\) and the loss is \(0.995\): the pair looked won only because the chosen response is shorter.

Online and iterative DPO

The cleanest fix for the off-policy weakness is to stop being off-policy: sample two or more responses from the current policy, label them with a reward model or LLM judge, train with the DPO loss, and repeat for a few rounds (Figure 1C). Xiong et al. show online exploration helps in theory and derive an iterative DPO that beat offline DPO in their experiments [Xiong et al. 2023]. Tajwar et al. find on-policy DPO converged faster and better than offline DPO and on-policy RL in their synthetic settings, though not always better than PPO [§5.3]. In production "iterative" often means rounds of new pairs: Llama 3 ran six rounds using mostly the latest preference batches from its best models, so "our training data conforms better to the distribution of the policy model" [§4.1.4, §4.1.6]. The cost is that the labeler becomes the bottleneck and the proxy problem of the over-optimization section returns.

Delta learning: pairs from a strong and a weak model

The delta-learning hypothesis [Geng et al. 2025] says a pair's value lies in the quality gap between its responses, not in either one: preferring \(y_w\) over \(y_l\) can help even when SFT on \(y_w\) would not, or would hurt [Olmo 3, §4.3]. Geng et al. paired a 3B model's outputs against a 1.5B model's to post-train an 8B model to Tulu 3's level on eleven benchmarks, and prove the effect for logistic regression [abstract].

Training is length-normalized DPO, \(\beta = 5\), one epoch, on 150K pairs (7B) or 200K (32B) [§4.3.1, §4.3.2, App. A.6.2, Tab. 48]. On a 7B SFT checkpoint (Tab. 21, average of ten benchmarks):

Why a pair carries signal (our reading, extending Geng et al.). The DPO update is the SFT update on \(y_w\) minus the SFT update on \(y_l\), scaled by \(\beta\,\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)\) (with Olmo 3's length normalization each term is divided by its own length). SFT on \(y_w\) alone pulls toward everything in it: the correct steps, but also Qwen3's phrasing, thinking style and errors. Two answers from one model family share most of those features, so their gradient components largely cancel in the difference, and what survives points from the weak answer's errors toward the strong one's. Geng et al. prove the clean version for logistic regression: each weak teacher's update alone can point away from the truth, but the difference of the two updates is positively aligned with it whenever the chosen teacher is better, and the same analysis predicts a training-length sweet spot, because training too long starts to fit the teachers' own errors [§6.2, Fig. 5]. Olmo 3 sees the sweet spot: AlpacaEval and ZebraLogic rose up to about 75K to 100K delta pairs and then flattened or fell, while AIME 2024 had not saturated [§5.5, Fig. 23]. Delta pairs are also deterministic (the strong model is always chosen), so the margin has no finite optimum (the IPO argument), and Olmo 3 tunes dataset size as a hyperparameter.

Risks. DPO learns whatever separates the sides, including length, style and format. In the Instruct data the 80th percentile of the chosen-minus-rejected length gap is 564 tokens for delta pairs, models learn the length bias along with the quality signal, and Olmo 3 caps the gap at 100 tokens for chat and multi-turn pairs, which lowered DPO-stage benchmarks but gave a better model after RL at 7B [§5.3.1, §5.5, Fig. 23]. The pairs are off-policy (above), and a gap that is too small may teach little: the paper speculates this is why its judge-based pairs from strong generators did not beat the OLMo 2 pairs (55.4 against 55.5 average) until weak models were forced into the response set (56.3) and the worst response became the rejected one (57.4) [§5.3.1, Tab. 32].

DPO versus online RL: the evidence, and when each wins

Cost and simplicity favor DPO. PPO needs sampling, a reward model, a critic and a reference (page 23); DPO needs two sequences through two models per pair, and the reference pass can be cached. In a controlled Tulu 3 comparison PPO reached a comparable but slightly lower average score and took about 28 hours on two nodes against about 4 hours on one for DPO [§5.4.1]. Llama 3 found DPO "required less compute for large-scale models and performed better, especially on instruction following benchmarks like IFEval" [§4.1.4].

Ceiling and generalization favor online RL, when it is tuned. Xu et al. compare the two on HH-RLHF, SafeRLHF and two code tasks. On HH-RLHF with an independent OpenAssistant reward model, DPO scored 0.611, iterative DPO 0.678 and PPO 0.718 (SFT: 0.532). On CodeContest with CodeLlama-34B (10@1k, test set), PPO reached 22.4%, above AlphaCode-41B with clustering at 16.4%, while DPO scored 0.0% and iterative DPO 3.2% [Tab. 4, 8]. Their PPO needed advantage normalization, a large batch and an exponential moving average for the reference: on APPS introductory problems (pass@5) plain PPO hurt (SFT 38.6% to 18.0%) and the three additions lifted it to 44.4% [Tab. 3]. With Tang et al. and Tajwar et al., the picture is that static off-policy DPO underperforms online methods using the same labeler, more so where the reward is verifiable and exploration matters.

Read the evidence critically

These comparisons are confounded by tuning effort. Xu et al. argue that earlier PPO baselines were under-tuned, they trained DPO at a fixed \(\beta = 0.1\) and learning rate \(10^{-6}\) (sweeping only the batch size, and one epoch for code), and Tulu 3 itself calls its PPO comparison a "non-tuned setup". The tasks also differ: open-ended chat preferences (where DPO is competitive) versus verifiable reasoning and code (where online RL dominates). No study here is a clean bake-off across both.

What recent open recipes do (as of September 2026, from the reports we checked). Most use both, in stages, the order is not settled, and some skip DPO entirely.

  • Tulu 3 (2024): SFT, then length-normalized DPO on on- and off-policy pairs, then RL with verifiable rewards (PPO-based). DPO was used for iteration speed; PPO was reserved for the verifiable stage [§5.4.1, §6].
  • OLMo 3 Think (2025): SFT, DPO on delta-learning pairs, then RLVR. DPO before RL beats going straight from SFT to RL: average 74.1 for SFT+DPO+RLVR versus 71.9 for SFT+RLVR on a subset of their evaluation suite at 7B (single run) [Tab. 22].

Common misconceptions

  • "DPO has no reward model, so it cannot over-optimize." The reward model is implicit, not absent. DPO, IPO and SLiC show the same hump-shaped win-rate-versus-KL curve as RLHF, often within the first quarter of an epoch, because the implicit reward is unconstrained off the data [Rafailov et al. 2024].
  • "DPO raises the chosen response's probability and lowers the rejected one's." It raises the gap between two log-ratios. Both probabilities can fall, and the freed mass can land on unrelated or opposite-meaning responses (case C in the worked numbers; Pal, Razin, Rafailov).
  • "A small \(\beta\) keeps the policy close to the reference." It is the opposite. \(\beta\) is the price of KL in the objective, so a larger \(\beta\) keeps \(\pi_\theta\) nearer \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). In the loss, a small \(\beta\) lets the log-ratio gap grow to \(\sim 22\) nats before the weight decays. And \(\beta\) values are not comparable across formulations (0.1 for plain DPO, 5 for Tulu 3's length-normalized loss, 2 to 2.5 for reference-free SimPO).
  • "DPO approximates or ignores the partition function." It cancels exactly, because Bradley-Terry sees reward differences and both responses share the prompt. The approximations in DPO are elsewhere: a parametric policy, finite off-policy data, and few gradient steps.
  • "The DPO optimum equals the RLHF optimum." Only for the unrestricted optimum, with Bradley-Terry preferences and adequate support. Xu et al. prove PPO's solutions are a proper subset of DPO's minimizers, and give a case where DPO puts 0.9 of its mass on a response the reference never produces.
  • "A more accurate reward model is a better RL signal." Accuracy on noisy labels caps near 70 to 80%, and higher benchmark scores do not reliably predict PPO outcomes (Tulu 3). Robustness to optimization pressure, not only pairwise accuracy, decides how far you can push.
  • "Length normalization fixes length bias." It changes the objective (it is no longer the KL-regularized optimum's Bradley-Terry fit), helps mainly where the data are strongly length-biased, and leaves other exploitable features untouched.
  • "Online RL makes DPO obsolete." Llama 3, Tulu 3 and OLMo 3 all use DPO, and OLMo 3 finds that DPO before RL beats SFT straight into RL. The comparisons are confounded by tuning effort, and DPO ran about 7 times faster in wall-clock time in Tulu 3's controlled test (4 hours on one node versus 28 hours on two).

Interview questions

Warm-up What is a Bradley-Terry reward model, and what loss trains it?

It assumes each response has a latent scalar reward and that a labeler prefers \(y_1\) to \(y_2\) with probability \(\sigma(r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2))\), which is the Elo model. Maximum likelihood on \((x, y_w, y_l)\) gives \(\mathcal L = -\E\log\sigma(r_w - r_l) = \E\log(1 + e^{-(r_w - r_l)})\): logistic regression on the reward gap. The gradient with respect to the gap is \(-\sigma(-\text{gap})\), so mistakes get large gradients. Rewards are identified only up to a prompt-dependent shift \(f(x)\). With \(r_w = 1.2\) and \(r_l = 0.4\) the loss is \(\log(1 + e^{-0.8}) = 0.371\), and with equal rewards it is \(\log 2 = 0.693\). Architecture: an LM backbone with the unembedding replaced by a linear head, read at the last token.

Warm-up What does the DPO loss equal when \(\pi_\theta = \pi_{\text{ref}}\), and what is the gradient weight? Why is it a useful check?

Both implicit rewards are zero, so the margin is 0, the loss is \(-\log\sigma(0) = \log 2 \approx 0.693\) for any \(\beta\) and any data, and the weight \(\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w) = 0.5\). The per-example gradient on \(\log\pi_\theta(y_w)\) is then \(-\beta/2\). It is the first thing to check in a new DPO implementation: if the initial loss is not \(\log 2\), the reference and the initial policy are not computing the same function (dropout left on in one model, a precision or tokenization mismatch, different weights, or a bug). It is also the loss you should see at step 0 in any log.

Warm-up What does \(\beta\) control in DPO?

It is the coefficient of \(\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\) in the RLHF objective that DPO solves, and it appears in the loss as the scale of the implicit reward, \(\hat r = \beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})\). Larger \(\beta\) means a stronger pull toward the reference: the closed-form optimum tilts \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) by \(e^{r/\beta}\), so \(\beta \to \infty\) recovers \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and \(\beta \to 0\) concentrates on the highest-reward response. In training, the weight \(\sigma(-\beta\Delta)\) depends on \(\beta\Delta\), so at \(\beta = 0.1\) a loss of 0.1 needs a 22.5-nat gap between log-ratios, whereas at \(\beta = 1\) it needs 2.25. Typical values are 0.1 for plain DPO and 5 for length-normalized DPO (Tulu 3). \(\beta\) is not transferable across objectives (KTO paper).

Warm-up How are preference pairs collected, and how noisy are they?

Human labelers rank several responses to a prompt (InstructGPT: 4 to 9 per prompt, giving \(\binom K2\) pairs) or choose between two with a strength label (Llama 2, over a million comparisons). Increasingly an LLM judge does it: Constitutional AI, UltraFeedback (GPT-4 scoring four aspects, then binarizing), Tulu 3 (GPT-4o over 22 candidate models, chosen = best, rejected = a random lower-rated response). Noise is large: InstructGPT labelers agreed 72.6% of the time (77.3% for held-out labelers), and public test sets have 60 to 70% accuracy ceilings. Under Bradley-Terry a pair with 73% agreement has a true reward gap of only about one nat. GPT-4 agrees with humans about as often as humans agree with each other (85% versus 81% on non-tied MT-bench votes) but has position and length biases. Data also lean long, so length is a built-in confound.

Core Derive DPO from the KL-regularized RL objective.

1. Objective. For each prompt, maximize \(\E_{y\sim\pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\) over all distributions \(\pi\).

2. Optimum. The objective equals \(-\beta\,\E_\pi[\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} - \frac r\beta]\). Define \(Z(x) = \sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)e^{r(x,y)/\beta}\) and \(\pi^\star = \pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}/Z\); then \(\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} - \frac r\beta = \log\frac{\pi}{\pi^\star} - \log Z\), so \(J(\pi) = \beta\log Z - \beta\,\KL(\pi\|\pi^\star)\), uniquely maximized at \(\pi^\star\) by Gibbs' inequality.

3. Invert. \(r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^\star(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta\log Z(x)\).

4. Bradley-Terry. \(p(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma(r_w - r_l)\). Substituting step 3, the two \(\beta\log Z(x)\) terms cancel, leaving \(\sigma\big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^\star(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi^\star(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}\big)\).

5. Loss. Replace \(\pi^\star\) with \(\pi_\theta\) and minimize the negative log-likelihood: \(\mathcal L = -\E\log\sigma\big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\big)\). This loses no generality: every Bradley-Terry reward class contains \(r = \beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})\) for some \(\pi\) (the member with \(Z = 1\)).

Assumptions to state: the optimum is over all policies, not the parametric family; \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) has support where \(\pi^\star\) does (and the reward is identified only there); \(Z\) finite; preferences are Bradley-Terry with a scalar reward; pairs come from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\); \(\beta > 0\); the KL is at sequence level.

Core What does the DPO gradient do, per example?

\(\nabla_\theta\mathcal L = -\beta\,\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)\,[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_w|x) - \nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_l|x)]\), with \(\hat r = \beta\log\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Descent raises the sequence log-probability of the chosen response and lowers that of the rejected one, with equal coefficients (each token in a response gets the same sequence-level coefficient). The weight \(\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)\) is 0.5 at initialization, tends to 1 for pairs the implicit reward ranks wrongly, and decays as \(e^{-\text{margin}}\) as it ranks them correctly, which acts as a curriculum on errors. The DPO paper reports that a "naive" version without the weight (an unlikelihood-style objective) can make the model degenerate. Derivation: \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u = -\sigma(-u)\) for \(u = \hat r_w - \hat r_l\), and \(\nabla u = \beta(\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_w) - \nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_l))\). Note that it never says to raise \(\log\pi(y_w)\) in absolute terms, only relative to \(y_l\).

Core Why doesn't the partition function \(Z(x)\) appear in the DPO loss?

Algebraically: solving the optimal-policy equation for the reward gives \(r = \beta\log(\pi^\star/\pi_{\text{ref}}) + \beta\log Z(x)\). The Bradley-Terry likelihood uses \(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l)\), and both responses answer the same prompt, so the two \(\beta\log Z(x)\) terms are identical and cancel. Structurally: Bradley-Terry is invariant to adding any function of the prompt to the reward, so rewards form equivalence classes, and \(\beta\log Z(x)\) is precisely such a prompt-only shift (Lemmas 1 and 2 in the DPO paper: equivalent rewards give the same preferences and the same optimal policy). So the one degree of freedom the data cannot identify is exactly the one the optimal policy ignores, and it is also the intractable one (a sum over all sequences). It would not cancel across different prompts, which is why rewards are only comparable within a prompt. In the token-level MDP the analogous cancelling term is the value \(V^*(s_0)\) of the shared prompt.

Core Calculate. With \(\log\pi(y_w) = -41\), \(\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w) = -44\), \(\log\pi(y_l) = -57\), \(\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l) = -52\) and \(\beta = 0.1\), find the DPO loss and the gradient weight. What log-ratio gap would give loss 0.1?

Log-ratios: chosen \(+3\), rejected \(-5\). Implicit rewards \(\hat r_w = +0.3\), \(\hat r_l = -0.5\), margin \(0.8\). Loss \(= \log(1 + e^{-0.8}) = 0.371\). Weight \(\sigma(-0.8) = 0.310\), so \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\log\pi(y_w) = -\beta\cdot 0.310 = -0.031\) and \(+0.031\) for the rejected response. (Compare a wrongly ranked pair with margin \(-0.7\): loss \(1.103\), weight \(0.668\).)

Loss \(0.1\) needs \(\sigma(m) = e^{-0.1} = 0.9048\), so \(m = \log(0.9048/0.0952) = 2.252\), a log-ratio gap \(\Delta = m/\beta = 22.5\) nats, at which the weight is \(1 - e^{-0.1} = 0.095\). That is a relative-odds change of \(e^{22.5}\approx 6\times10^9\), which over 200 tokens is only 0.11 nats per token.

Core How do you train and evaluate a reward model? What is reward over-optimization?

Training. Start from an SFT or chat LM, replace the unembedding with a scalar head at the last token, and minimize \(-\log\sigma(r_w - r_l)\) for one epoch at a low learning rate (multiple epochs usually overfit, although RewardBench 2 found a second epoch helpful in some setups). Put all pairs of a prompt in one batch element, since within-prompt pairs are correlated. Optionally add a rating-based margin (Llama 2; dropped in Llama 3), train separate helpfulness and safety models, and use ensembles. Evaluation. Held-out pairwise accuracy (capped near 70 to 80% by annotator noise, and better read per difficulty bucket), RewardBench-style structured sets (Chat, Chat Hard, Safety, Reasoning), and ultimately downstream best-of-\(n\) or RL results, since benchmark scores do not reliably predict them.

Over-optimization. The reward model is a proxy fitted on finite noisy data. As a policy is optimized against it (KL from the initial policy increasing), the proxy score keeps rising while the true (gold) reward rises, peaks and falls. Gao et al. fit \(R_{\text{bon}} = d(\alpha - \beta d)\) and \(R_{\text{RL}} = d(\alpha - \beta\log d)\) with \(d = \sqrt{\KL}\), and find the coefficients scale smoothly with reward-model size. Mitigations: bigger models and more data, fresh labels each round, ensembles with conservative scoring, early stopping on a held-out gold signal, and a KL penalty (which mostly acts as early stopping).

Core IPO, KTO, SimPO, ORPO: what problem does each address?

With \(h = \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}\):

  • IPO: loss \((h - \frac{1}{2\tau})^2\). Targets overfitting when preferences are (near) deterministic, where DPO's target gap is infinite and its KL control vanishes. A two-sided squared loss keeps the gap near a finite target. Mainly toy-bandit evidence; often behind DPO on LLM benchmarks.
  • KTO: \(\lambda_y - v(x,y)\) with a logistic value around a reference point \(z_0\) (a batch-estimated KL). Targets the need for paired data: it learns from unpaired desirable/undesirable labels and handles imbalance with \(\lambda_D, \lambda_U\).
  • SimPO: \(-\log\sigma(\frac{\beta}{|y_w|}\log\pi(y_w) - \frac{\beta}{|y_l|}\log\pi(y_l) - \gamma)\). Targets DPO's mismatch between the training reward and the average log-likelihood used at generation, length exploitation, and the reference model's memory; adds a target margin.
  • ORPO: SFT loss plus \(\lambda\,(-\log\sigma(\log\text{odds}_w - \log\text{odds}_l))\) with length-normalized probabilities. Targets pipeline complexity: one stage, no reference model, no separate SFT.

Also cDPO/rDPO (label noise), DPOP (chosen-likelihood collapse), and online or iterative DPO (off-policy data). Caveat to volunteer: most gains are reported by the method's authors on chat benchmarks, and independent tables often show the plain DPO baseline competitive (SimPO's table has IPO, KTO and ORPO all below DPO on AlpacaEval 2, while Tulu 3 found SimPO below its SFT baseline).

Core Design and trade-offs: when would you prefer DPO over PPO, and when the reverse?

DPO when you have a fixed preference dataset close to the policy's distribution, limited compute (Tulu 3: about 4 hours on one node versus 28 hours on two for PPO, with slightly lower PPO scores in an untuned comparison), and the goal is style, helpfulness or instruction following (Llama 3: DPO was cheaper and better on IFEval). It is stable, has few hyperparameters (\(\beta\), learning rate, epochs) and needs no reward model or generation loop.

Online RL when rewards are verifiable (math, code) or you need to discover behavior outside the data. It samples from the current policy, so it does not suffer the off-policy gap, and it queries a reward on exactly the responses being produced. Xu et al. (tuned PPO with advantage normalization, large batches and a reference EMA) get 0.718 versus DPO's 0.611 on HH-RLHF with an independent reward model, and 22.4% versus 0.0% on CodeContest. Tang et al. show online beats offline at equal KL. Costs: four models, sampling throughput, engineering, and sensitivity to reward-model quality.

In practice use both: DPO as a cheap stage before RL (OLMo 3: 74.1 versus 71.9 average), or online RL followed by a light DPO stage (Llama 4). Caveat: existing comparisons are confounded by tuning effort.

Core What is delta learning, and why can DPO on a strong-versus-weak pair help when SFT on the strong response hurts?

Hypothesis: a preference pair's value is the quality gap between its two responses, not the quality of either (Geng et al.). Olmo 3 built Think-DPO pairs with chosen from Qwen3 32B (thinking) and rejected from Qwen3 0.6B (thinking). On a 7B SFT checkpoint (average of ten benchmarks 70.3), more SFT on the chosen answers gave 64.5 (all ten fell, LiveCodeBench 67.0 to 55.1), while DPO on the pairs gave 72.9 (AIME 2024 71.0 to 75.7, IFEval 79.7 to 75.2). In Figure 20's plot (no values printed) the DPO model's pass@k on AIME is above the SFT model's over most of the plotted range (all \(k\) on AIME 2025, up to \(k = 16\) on AIME 2024), so the gain is not only pass@k converted into pass@1. As an RL start it beat SFT on average (74.1 against 71.9 after RLVR, one run), though not on every evaluation.

Why: the DPO update (the negative loss gradient) is \(\beta\,\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)[\nabla\log\pi(y_w) - \nabla\log\pi(y_l)]\), SFT's update on the chosen response minus its update on the rejected. SFT on \(y_w\) pulls toward all of it, including style and errors, and toward traces the paper conjectures are weaker than the SFT data already held. In the difference, features the two answers share (same model family, template, format) largely cancel and what remains points from error to correct reasoning (our reading). Geng et al. prove the logistic-regression version: the difference of two weak teachers' updates is aligned with the truth whenever one is better, even if each alone misleads, with a training-length sweet spot. Risks: anything else that separates the sides is learned too: length (an 80th-percentile gap of 564 tokens in Olmo 3's Instruct pairs, capped at 100 for chat pairs), style, format, identity; regressions (IFEval fell 4.5 points); off-policy pairs; over-training (some tasks peaked near 75K to 100K pairs); and a gap that is too small may teach little (the paper's speculation about its judged pairs). To check, compare SFT-on-chosen with DPO on the same pairs (Olmo 3's Table 21) and ablate the length gap (its 100-token cap for Instruct).

Deep-dive Why can the chosen response's likelihood decrease during DPO training?

Because the loss depends only on the gap between the two log-ratios. Pushing \(y_l\) down by 20 nats while \(y_w\) drops 1 nat lowers the loss (0.139 versus 0.371 in our worked example). Four mechanisms have been identified. (1) KL accounting: if \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) was fitted to the chosen responses, \(\E_{\pi_{\text{ref}}}[\beta\log\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}] = -\beta\,\KL(\pi_{\text{ref}}\|\pi) \le 0\), so the average implicit reward of chosen responses is expected to decrease as the policy moves (Rafailov et al.), and this is not necessarily harmful. (2) Shared structure: when \(y_w\) and \(y_l\) differ in few tokens, the shared prefix gradient cancels and the later tokens' logits are pushed the wrong way (Pal et al.). (3) Embedding geometry: similar responses have similar hidden states and unembeddings, so lowering \(y_l\) lowers \(y_w\), and the mass moves to tokens aligned with the update direction, possibly of opposite meaning (Razin et al.: Llama-3-8B-Instruct's refusal rate fell from 74.4% to 33.4% when trained to refuse). (4) Data dependence: distinct pairs (GPT-4 versus GPT-3.5) can raise both, similar pairs cannot (Tajwar et al.).

Mitigations: an NLL term on the chosen (Llama 3, coefficient 0.2), DPOP's penalty, filtering similar pairs by CHES, on-policy pairs and fewer epochs. A one-token toy model with a free hidden state reproduces it: the loss falls monotonically while \(\pi(y_w)\) drops from 0.25 to 0.02 and an unrelated token takes the mass.

Deep-dive State every assumption in the DPO derivation. What breaks if \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) has zero probability on a response?

Assumptions: (a) preferences follow Bradley-Terry with a scalar reward (fails for intransitive or deterministic preferences); (b) the maximization is over all distributions, not the model family, and is reached; (c) \(\beta > 0\) and \(Z(x) < \infty\); (d) \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) has support wherever \(\pi^\star\) should; (e) the KL is at sequence level; (f) the dataset pairs are samples from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), so the MLE is well posed; (g) labels are correct (else use cDPO/rDPO).

If \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) = 0\) then \(\pi^\star(y|x) = 0\) for any reward, since \(\pi^\star \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}\); the inversion \(r = \beta\log(\pi^\star/\pi_{\text{ref}}) + \beta\log Z\) is \(0/0\), so \(r(x,y)\) is unidentified; and any policy with mass on \(y\) has infinite KL, so it is infeasible for the true RL problem. DPO's loss, however, only evaluates ratios at dataset responses and does not penalize \(\pi_\theta\) for mass placed elsewhere. Xu et al.'s example: \(\pi_{\text{ref}} = (0.5, 0.5, 0)\), one pair \(y_1\succ y_2\): the loss is \(\log(1 + (\pi(y_2)/\pi(y_1))^\beta)\), minimized by \(\pi(y_2)\to 0\) with, for example, \(\pi = (0.1, 0, 0.9)\), which PPO can never reach. For a softmax LM every sequence has positive probability, so the failure is one of coverage, not literal zeros: the KL control exists only on the data. That is why DPO needs pairs near the reference distribution, or a refreshed on-policy dataset.

Deep-dive Gao et al.: write the functional forms, find where the gold reward peaks, and explain why a KL penalty does not fix over-optimization.

With \(d = \sqrt{\KL(\pi\|\pi_{\text{init}})}\): \(R_{\text{bon}}(d) = d(\alpha_{\text{bon}} - \beta_{\text{bon}}d)\) and \(R_{\text{RL}}(d) = d(\alpha_{\text{RL}} - \beta_{\text{RL}}\log d)\), with \(R(0) = 0\). Best-of-\(n\) has \(\KL = \log n - \frac{n-1}{n}\) (\(n = 1000\): 5.91 nats; \(n = 60{,}000\): 10.0 nats). Setting the derivative to zero: the BoN peak is at \(d^\star = \alpha/(2\beta)\) with height \(\alpha^2/(4\beta)\); the RL peak at \(d^\star = e^{\alpha/\beta - 1}\) with height \(\beta d^\star\). (Peaks in KL are \(d^{\star 2}\).) The \(\alpha\) term corresponds to regressional Goodhart (the proxy fits noise, a slope gap) and \(\beta\) to extremal Goodhart (leaving the reward model's support). Coefficients vary smoothly, roughly logarithmically, with reward-model size.

The KL penalty does not improve the gold-reward-versus-KL frontier in their experiments. It changes how far along the same curve training gets by the time it converges, which is early stopping in effect (with the caveat that this result may depend on hyperparameters). Better fixes change the curve: a larger or ensembled reward model with conservative scoring (Coste et al.: worst-case or uncertainty-weighted ensembles needed a KL penalty of only 0.01 where a single reward model needed 0.2), fresh preference labels each round (a gain of \(\beta_{\text{RL}}d\log k\) for \(k\) rounds under their simplifying assumptions), and stopping on a held-out gold signal.

Deep-dive Design question: you have 300k preference pairs written by a mix of stronger models, a budget for a few DPO runs on an 8B model, and a reward model of unknown quality. Lay out the recipe.

Reference and starting point. The pairs are off-policy, so first SFT on the chosen responses (or use an instruct model) and freeze that as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Data. Drop near-identical pairs (low margin, or high CHES similarity), keep only clear wins as Llama 3 does, cap the length difference between chosen and rejected (OLMo 3 Instruct: 100 tokens), and check the fraction of pairs where the chosen is longer. Objective. Start with length-normalized DPO or plain DPO plus an NLL term on the chosen (coefficient 0.2 as in Llama 3); sweep \(\beta\) on a log grid and the learning rate (order \(10^{-7}\) to \(10^{-5}\)); 1 to 2 epochs, since more can hurt (Tulu 3's 57.3 versus 53.4 at 1 versus 3 epochs). Cache the reference log-probabilities. Tulu 3's 8B DPO took about 10 hours on 8 H100s.

Monitoring. Held-out implicit-reward accuracy, chosen and rejected log-probabilities separately (watch displacement), KL from the reference, response length, and a length-controlled win rate against a fixed baseline, plus safety refusal rates if relevant. Second round. Sample from the DPO model, label with the judge or reward model, keep hard prompts, and run a short on-policy DPO round against the same reference. Check the reward model on a RewardBench-like set and against a swapped-order LLM judge first, and stop when a held-out gold signal (not the proxy) peaks. If the task turns out verifiable, move to online RL with a DPO stage in front.

Cheat sheet

  • Bradley-Terry: \(P(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma(r_w - r_l)\). RM loss \(-\log\sigma(r_w - r_l)\); equal rewards give \(\log 2\). Rewards live up to \(+f(x)\). RM = LM + linear head at the last token, 1 epoch, all pairs of a prompt in one batch element. Label noise caps accuracy near 70 to 80% (agreement 72.6% in InstructGPT; gap \(=\operatorname{logit}(p)\)).
  • Over-optimization (Gao): \(d = \sqrt{\KL}\); BoN \(R = d(\alpha - \beta d)\), RL \(R = d(\alpha - \beta\log d)\); \(\KL_{\text{bon}} = \log n - \frac{n-1}{n}\); peaks at \(d^\star = \alpha/2\beta\) (BoN) and \(e^{\alpha/\beta - 1}\) (RL). KL penalty behaves like early stopping. DAAs over-optimize too.
  • KL-regularized optimum: \(\pi^\star = \pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}/Z\), \(Z = \sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}\); \(J(\pi) = \beta\log Z - \beta\,\KL(\pi\|\pi^\star)\). Over all policies; needs support and finite \(Z\).
  • DPO chain: optimum \(\to\) \(r = \beta\log\frac{\pi^\star}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} + \beta\log Z\) \(\to\) plug into Bradley-Terry, \(Z\) cancels \(\to\) \(\mathcal L = -\log\sigma\big(\beta[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}]\big)\). Log-probs are sums over response tokens.
  • DPO gradient: \(-\beta\,\sigma(\hat r_l - \hat r_w)[\nabla\log\pi(y_w) - \nabla\log\pi(y_l)]\). Weight 0.5 at init, loss \(\log 2\). Loss 0.1 at \(\beta = 0.1\) needs margin 2.252 = 22.5 nats, weight 0.095.
  • Hand numbers (β = 0.1): chosen +3, rejected −5 nats gives margin 0.8, loss 0.371, weight 0.310. Wrongly ranked (margin −0.7): loss 1.103, weight 0.668.
  • Failure modes: off-policy data (Xu, Tang); likelihood displacement (gap only; NLL term 0.2 in Llama 3; DPOP); length exploitation (R-DPO, length-normalized, SimPO); deterministic labels (IPO); noisy labels (cDPO \(\sigma(-m) - \varepsilon\), rDPO \(\sigma(-m) + \frac{\varepsilon}{1-2\varepsilon}\)); \(\beta\)/epochs sensitivity.
  • Variants: IPO \((h - \frac1{2\tau})^2\). KTO unpaired, \(z_0\) = batch KL, lr about \(5\times10^{-6}\). ORPO NLL + \(\lambda\) odds-ratio, no reference. SimPO \(\frac{\beta}{|y|}\log\pi\) reward, \(\gamma\), \(\beta \in [2, 2.5]\), no reference.
  • Settings: DPO paper \(\beta = 0.1\), lr \(10^{-6}\); Llama 3 lr \(10^{-5}\), \(\beta = 0.1\), NLL 0.2, 6 rounds; Tulu 3 length-normalized, \(\beta = 5\), lr \(5\times10^{-7}\) (8B) / \(2\times10^{-7}\) (70B), 1 epoch.
  • DPO vs PPO: DPO about 4 h on one node vs PPO about 28 h on two (Tulu 3); tuned PPO wins on code and HH (Xu); labs combine them (OLMo 3: DPO before RLVR helps; Llama 4: online RL then light DPO).
  • Delta learning: a pair's value is the quality gap, not either response (Geng et al. 2025: 3B chosen against 1.5B rejected matched Tulu 3 from an 8B model). Olmo 3 Think DPO: Qwen3 32B thinking against Qwen3 0.6B thinking, 150K pairs (7B) or 200K (32B), length-normalized, \(\beta = 5\), 1 epoch. 7B average: SFT 70.3, SFT on chosen 64.5, DPO 72.9 (IFEval 79.7 to 75.2); as an RL start 74.1 against 71.9 on average (one run; SFT+RLVR ahead on IFEval and ZebraLogic). Why: the DPO update is the SFT update on chosen minus the SFT update on rejected, so shared features cancel. Risks: length (80th-percentile gap 564 tokens, 100-token cap), style, off-policy pairs, over-training (about 75K to 100K pairs).
