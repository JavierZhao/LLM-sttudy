RLHF in practice

P0 ~50 min Prereqs: 21 Reward models & DPO, 22 Policy gradient to PPO

RLHF is the pipeline that turned pretrained language models into assistants: fit a reward model to human comparisons, then optimize the model against it on a KL leash. This page covers the recipe as it was actually run (InstructGPT, Anthropic's HH work, Llama 2), the engineering that keeps the loop stable, how it fails, and the shift since 2023 toward AI feedback, process supervision and rubric-based generative rewards.

What interviewers probe

  • The KL penalty: what it protects against, what happens when \(\beta\) is too small or too large, why it is applied per token, and how a controller adapts it.
  • Engineering realities: memory for policy, reference, reward model and value model; why generation dominates wall-clock; whitening, value initialization from the reward model, EOS handling.
  • Failure modes: three concrete forms of reward hacking with mitigations, over-optimization, diversity loss, and how noisy the labels really are.
  • What replaced it: why labs moved to rejection sampling, DPO and verifiable rewards, what Constitutional AI and RLAIF change, and when process rewards or rubric-based judges beat a scalar reward model.

The InstructGPT pipeline

A pretrained model continues text, it does not try to be helpful. Supervised fine-tuning (page 19) narrows the gap by imitating demonstrations, but demonstrations are expensive, and imitation cannot express "this answer is better than that one", a judgment people make quickly and fairly consistently. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) 基于人类反馈的强化学习 turns such comparisons into a training signal. The idea comes from Christiano et al. 2017 (control tasks), reached language models with Ziegler et al. 2019 and Stiennon et al. 2020 (summarization), and general instruction following with InstructGPT [Ouyang et al. 2022]. The recipe has three stages, all starting from a GPT-3 model of 1.3B, 6B or 175B parameters (Figure 1).

  1. SFT. About 40 contractors write demonstrations. The model trains for 16 epochs: validation loss overfits after one, but reward-model score and human preference keep improving, so the checkpoint is chosen by reward-model score [Ouyang et al. 2022, §3.5]. The SFT model that initializes PPO and serves as the KL reference is a separate run: 2 epochs with 10% pretraining data mixed in [App. C.3].
  2. Reward model 奖励模型. Remove the SFT model's unembedding layer, add a scalar head, train on rankings (the final 6B model was in fact initialized from GPT-3 fine-tuned on public NLP datasets, with similar results from GPT-3 or SFT weights). Only a 6B reward model is used for all policy sizes: a 175B one trained unstably and, as the initialization of the value function, would have made PPO very expensive [App. C.2].
  3. PPO (page 22) against the reward model, with a KL penalty toward the SFT model and, in the final "PPO-ptx" variant, pretraining gradients mixed in.

The reward-model loss and the K-way trick

Labelers rank \(K\) responses to one prompt, which yields \(\binom{K}{2}\) preference pairs. The loss is the Bradley-Terry pairwise loss from page 21, averaged over those pairs:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{\binom{K}{2}}\, \E_{(x,\,y_w,\,y_l)}\Big[\log \sigma\big(r_\theta(x,y_w) - r_\theta(x,y_l)\big)\Big] \]

The engineering point is the batching. Comparisons from one prompt are strongly correlated, and shuffling all pairs into one dataset overfit after a single pass, so InstructGPT feeds all \(\binom{K}{2}\) pairs of a prompt as one batch element. That is also cheaper: for \(K = 9\), nine forward passes produce 36 comparisons [§3.5]. Because the loss sees only score differences, a bias is added after training so that labeler demonstrations score 0 on average before RL.

The RL objective and the pretraining mix

The RL environment is a bandit: a random prompt arrives, the policy writes a response, the reward model scores it, the episode ends. A KL penalty toward the SFT model is added at every token, and the value function is initialized from the reward model [§3.5]:

\[ \text{objective}(\phi) = \E_{(x,y)\sim \pi^{\text{RL}}_\phi}\Big[ r_\theta(x,y) - \beta \log \frac{\pi^{\text{RL}}_\phi(y \mid x)}{\pi^{\text{SFT}}(y \mid x)} \Big] + \gamma\, \E_{x \sim D_{\text{pretrain}}}\big[\log \pi^{\text{RL}}_\phi(x)\big] \]
InstructGPT Eq. 2. Final runs: \(\beta = 0.02\), \(\gamma = 27.8\). Setting \(\gamma = 0\) gives plain "PPO".

The first term is the KL-regularized objective of page 21. The second is the pretraining mix: for every RL episode, 8 pretraining examples are drawn and their log-likelihood gradient, scaled by \(\gamma\), is added [App. C.4]. Each 512-response batch is split into 8 minibatches of 64 and used for one inner epoch (PPO clip 0.2, temperature 1 at rollout, no discounting in GAE).

Mixing pretraining gradients into PPO (PPO-ptx)
Problem
Plain PPO against the reward model lowers accuracy on public NLP benchmarks (SQuAD v2, DROP, HellaSwag, WMT15 Fr→En). This is the alignment tax 对齐税: alignment costs capability.
Idea
Add \(\gamma\, \E_{x\sim D_{\text{pretrain}}}[\log\pi(x)]\) to the RL objective, computed on the same minibatches.
Why it works
The KL term only measures how far the policy moves on RL prompts and responses. Benchmark-style inputs live elsewhere, so nothing in the KL term protects them. The pretraining term anchors likelihood on the whole pretraining distribution. This is our reading of the authors' conclusion that the pretraining distribution is critical for fixing the regressions.
Evidence
PPO-ptx recovers most regressions and beats GPT-3 on HellaSwag without lowering labeler preference, while still lagging on DROP, SQuAD v2 and translation. Raising \(\beta\) to 2.0 (100× the default) never fixed DROP or SQuAD and cost a lot of reward. A pretraining coefficient of at least 20 recovers them for the 1.3B model [§4.2, App. E.6].
Cost
An extra forward and backward pass over 8× as many pretraining examples as RL episodes, and one more coefficient to tune. It is not a full cure: on the 1.3B model, training twice as long (512k episodes) pushed DROP and SQuAD v2 slightly below the GPT-3 baseline again.
Used by
InstructGPT. Kimi K2's RL keeps the idea as an auxiliary "PTX loss" on hand-picked high-quality data [Kimi K2, §3.2.3].

What the headline result means

On the paper's API prompt distribution, labelers prefer the 1.3B PPO-ptx model to the 175B GPT-3, which has 100× more parameters. The 175B InstructGPT beats 175B GPT-3 in 85 ± 3% of comparisons and few-shot-prompted GPT-3 in 71 ± 4%. Closed-domain hallucination drops from 41% to 21%, and TruthfulQA true-and-informative answers roughly double [§1, §4.1]. The stage is cheap: 4.9 petaflops/s-days for the 175B SFT model and 60 for PPO-ptx, against 3,640 for GPT-3, so \((4.9 + 60)/3640 \approx 1.8\%\) of pretraining compute (these two training runs only: not the 6B reward model, data collection or experimental runs) [§5.1].

Read "better" precisely: better according to about 40 contractors who agree with each other 72.6 ± 1.5% of the time (77.3 ± 1.3% for held-out labelers) [§3.4], on prompts sent to the API. The authors say the model is aligned to the preferences of a specific group, not to human values in general. Precursors show the same shape at small scale: on Reddit TL;DR, a 1.3B model trained with human feedback beat a supervised model ten times its size, 61% versus 43% against reference summaries [Stiennon et al. 2020, §4.1].

Engineering the PPO loop

Four models, one loop

PPO-based RLHF keeps four networks alive at once [Sheng et al. 2024, §2.1]. Each iteration has three stages: generation (the policy samples responses), preparation (reference, reward and value models each do one forward pass over prompt plus response) and learning (policy and value model are updated with Adam). DPO can cache reference log-probabilities once because its responses are fixed (Tulu 3 does [§5.4.2]). PPO cannot: responses are resampled every iteration.

Generation dominates wall-clock

By FLOPs, generation should be cheap. Per token, generation is one forward pass (\(2N\) FLOPs), the reference, reward and value models one each (\(2N\) each), and policy and critic each need forward plus backward (\(6N\) each): \(2+2+2+2+6+6 = 20N\), so generation is \(2/20 = 10\%\) of the FLOPs for one PPO epoch. But decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound (page 06) and runs at a fraction of a training pass's efficiency. If forward and backward passes reach 40% MFU and decoding 10% (assumptions, not measurements), generation takes \((2/0.10)/(2/0.10 + 18/0.40) = 20/65 = 31\%\) of an iteration, and 47% at 5%. Measured systems report larger shares (the HybridFlow figure below includes actor training, so it does not isolate generation):

  • Llama 2: with FSDP, generation was about 20× slower even with a KV cache, until weights were consolidated onto each node first. A 70B PPO iteration took about 330 s [§3.2.3].
  • HybridFlow: actor generation plus actor training are 58.9% of iteration time in its own runs (training included, so this bounds generation from above). In NeMo-Aligner, whose generation engine has no KV cache, generation alone is up to 81.2%. Moving a 70B actor's 140 GB of weights between training and generation layouts took up to 36.4% of an iteration on separate devices [§2.3, §8.2].
  • OpenRLHF: for long chain-of-thought rollouts, inference is over 90% of runtime [§1].

So systems treat rollout as an inference workload: a serving engine with paged KV caches (vLLM), different parallelism for generation (small tensor-parallel, large data-parallel) than for training, weight resharding between them, and asynchronous rollouts. Tulu 3's RL runs took 65 h on 8 GPUs (8B), 60 h on 48 (70B) and 46 h on 256 (405B) [§6.3]. Page 25 covers the systems side.

The reward vector, worked out

The reward-model score exists only for the complete response, so it is credited to the last token. The KL penalty is spread over all tokens using the log-ratio of the sampled token (the simplest KL estimate, compared with others on page 22):

\[ r_t = -\beta\,\Big(\log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\Big) + \mathbb{1}[t = T]\; r_\phi(x, y) \]
The sum over t is \(r_\phi - \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\), exactly the sequence-level objective

Spreading the penalty makes it dense: each token pays for its own drift, and the value function and GAE can attribute cost to the tokens responsible. A 6-token response with \(\beta = 0.1\) and reward-model score 1.5 (numbers chosen for the example):

Notice that the reward-model score dominates every return-to-go, so KL differences between tokens are small perturbations of a common signal, which is why whitening advantages matters. If the response had been truncated without EOS, most implementations replace the score with a constant (next section).

Fixed or adaptive \(\beta\)

With a fixed \(\beta\), the KL a run ends up with depends on seed, reward scale and step count: Ziegler et al. note that seeds with the same \(\beta\) "sometimes end up with quite different values of KL". They also used a proportional controller that steers KL toward a target [Ziegler et al. 2019, §2.2]:

\[ e_t = \operatorname{clip}\!\Big(\frac{\mathrm{KL}_t - \mathrm{KL}_{\text{target}}}{\mathrm{KL}_{\text{target}}},\, -0.2,\, 0.2\Big), \qquad \beta_{t+1} = \beta_t\,(1 + K_\beta\, e_t), \quad K_\beta = 0.1 \]
Ziegler et al. 2019. Targets used: 6 nats (descriptiveness) and 10 nats (sentiment)

The released lm-human-preferences code (we read the source) writes the same rule with gain \(n_{\text{steps}}/\text{horizon}\): mult = 1 + clip(kl / target - 1, -0.2, 0.2) * n_steps / horizon, where kl is the batch mean of the per-sequence KL (sum over tokens of the sampled log-ratio), n_steps the batch size in episodes and the default horizon 10,000 episodes. A batch of 1,024 gives a gain of 0.1024, close to the paper's 0.1. The code applies the reward as rewards = -beta * kl per token, then rewards[:, -1] += scores, exactly the vector above, and whitens rewards (keeping the mean) and then advantages.

Worked example. \(\beta = 0.1\), target 6 nats, measured KL 9, batch 512, horizon 10,000. The relative error \(9/6 - 1 = 0.5\) is clipped to \(0.2\); the multiplier is \(1 + 0.2\times512/10{,}000 = 1.01024\), so \(\beta \to 0.10102\). Even saturated, \(\beta\) grows 1% per update, and doubling takes \(\ln 2/\ln 1.01024 \approx 68\) updates. With KL 3 the error clips to \(-0.2\) and \(\beta \to 0.09898\). The controller is a slow set-point tracker, and the drill implements it.

import torch

def shaped_rewards(rm_score, logp_policy, logp_ref, beta, mask):
    # rm_score: (B,)   logp_*: (B, T) log-probs of the sampled tokens   mask: (B, T), 1 on response tokens
    m = mask.to(logp_policy.dtype)
    kl = (logp_policy - logp_ref).detach()              # per-token log-ratio (single-sample KL estimate)
    rewards = -beta * kl * m                            # dense KL penalty, 0 on padding
    last = (m * torch.arange(mask.shape[1], dtype=m.dtype)).argmax(dim=1)     # index of the last real token
    rewards[torch.arange(mask.shape[0]), last] += rm_score.detach()          # score lands on the last token
    return rewards                                      # (B, T)

class AdaptiveKLController:
    def __init__(self, init_beta, target_kl, horizon):
        self.beta, self.target, self.horizon = init_beta, target_kl, horizon
    def update(self, current_kl, n_steps):
        err = min(max(current_kl / self.target - 1.0, -0.2), 0.2)   # saturating proportional error
        self.beta *= 1.0 + err * n_steps / self.horizon             # multiplicative: beta stays positive
        return self.beta

Two production recipes: Anthropic HH and Llama 2

InstructGPT is the template. Two later recipes show what changes when the target is a safe chat assistant rather than an instruction follower: two competing objectives, weekly data refreshes, and a great deal of iteration.

Anthropic HH: two objectives and online iteration

Bai et al. collect two separate datasets with 52B models: crowdworkers chat with the model and pick the more helpful of two responses, or, when red-teaming, the more harmful one [Bai et al. 2022, §1, §2.2]. There are three tranches: 44k helpfulness plus 42k harmlessness comparisons from a context-distilled base model, 52k plus 2k from rejection sampling against an early preference model (usually best of 16), and 22k helpfulness comparisons from RLHF policies re-trained roughly weekly for about five weeks [§2.3].

The central finding is the tension between the objectives. "An excessive focus on avoiding harm can lead to 'safe' responses that don't actually address the needs of the human", and preference models trained mostly on one distribution do far worse than chance on the other. In RL it surfaces as evasiveness: policies gave the same exaggerated answer (for example, advising therapy) to anything remotely sensitive. The diagnosis was over-optimization on one axis: the policy's harmlessness score sat on the extreme tail of the preference-model data while its helpfulness score still looked in-distribution (their Fig. 14). Refusing ("I can't answer that") is enough to score well on red-team prompts, so it is easy to learn and hard to improve on. Their partial fix was a larger share of helpfulness prompts in RL [§4.4]. The KL coefficient was tiny, \(\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0.001\) with KL typically below 100 nats, which they say probably matters little [§4.1].

Online iterated RLHF closes the loop: deploy the newest policy, collect comparisons on its outputs, retrain the preference model and policy, repeat weekly. It filled out the upper tail of response quality and won in crowdworker Elo. Caveats the authors list: the online model had about 20% more data and better hyperparameters, so a controlled run with equal data (about 44k comparisons, an even mix of base, rejection-sampled and online) confirmed that the iterated mix gives a clearly preferred policy. The final preference model scores 74%, 70% and 67% on held-out base, rejection-sampled and online-only data: telling apart good responses gets harder as the policy improves [§4.5]. Two more results: RL reward grows roughly linearly in \(\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_0)}\) for much of training (the shape of best-of-\(n\), page 21), and the alignment tax depends on scale: small models lose on NLP evaluations after RLHF, while 13B and 52B models gain zero-shot and tie few-shot, so conclusions from small models can mislead [§1.1].

Llama 2: two reward models, a margin, rejection sampling then PPO

Data and reward models. Llama 2 collects over one million binary comparisons (1,418,091 in 14 weekly batches). Annotators write a prompt, choose between two responses from different model variants and temperatures, and rate the strength of preference on four levels (significantly better, better, slightly better, negligibly better or unsure). Data is refreshed each round because "reward model accuracy can quickly degrade if not exposed to this new sample distribution" [Touvron et al. 2023, §3.2.1]. Two reward models, helpfulness and safety, start from the chat checkpoint with a regression head and train for one epoch. The helpfulness RM sees all helpfulness data plus an equal amount of other data; the safety RM sees safety and Anthropic Harmless data, mixed 90/10 with helpfulness data. Each wins on its own test set (Table 7): the helpfulness RM scores 63.2% on Meta Helpfulness (safety RM 56.2%, zero-shot GPT-4 58.6%), the safety RM 64.5% on Meta Safety (helpfulness RM 62.8%, GPT-4 58.1%) [§3.2.2].

Margin. Because annotators grade preference strength, the ranking loss gets a rating-dependent margin \(m(r)\):

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{ranking}} = -\log \sigma\big(r_\theta(x,y_c) - r_\theta(x,y_r) - m(r)\big) \]
Llama 2 Eq. 2. Margins for (significantly better, better, slightly better, negligible): small = (1, 2/3, 1/3, 0), large = (3, 2, 1, 0)

The margin asks the model to separate clear pairs by more than similar ones. At a score gap \(\Delta = r_c - r_r = 2\), the loss is \(0.127\) with no margin, \(0.313\) with margin 1 and \(1.313\) with margin 3, so a "significantly better" pair keeps pushing scores apart long after an ordinary pair would saturate. On the helpfulness RM (Table 28), accuracy on "significantly better" pairs rises from 79.1% to 80.4% (small margin) and 80.7% (large), and the overall average from 62.5% to 63.0% and 62.9%. The large margin hurts similar pairs and makes scores more extreme, which the authors flag as a calibration issue because PPO is sensitive to the reward distribution [App. A.3.3].

Combining the two rewards for PPO. The safety score takes over when the prompt is flagged as safety-relevant or the safety score is below 0.15 (a threshold that gave precision 0.89 and recall 0.55 on the Meta Safety test set), and the logit of the chosen score is whitened to balance it against the KL term:

\[ R_c(g \mid p) = \begin{cases} R_s(g \mid p) & \text{if is\_safety}(p) \text{ or } R_s(g \mid p) < 0.15 \\ R_h(g \mid p) & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \qquad \tilde R_c = \operatorname{whiten}\big(\operatorname{logit}(R_c)\big) \]

Iterations. Llama 2-Chat went through five versions (RLHF-V1 to V5) using two algorithms. Rejection sampling fine-tuning samples \(K\) responses per prompt from the latest model, keeps the highest-reward one and fine-tunes on it. PPO updates online. They differ in breadth (\(K\) samples versus one) and depth (PPO's data depends on the policy after every step). Up to V4 only rejection sampling was used, afterwards PPO ran on top of the rejection-sampling checkpoint. Rejection sampling ran only on the 70B model, and smaller models were fine-tuned on its samples (distillation). Training V3 only on V2's samples left it worse at rhyming poems (suspected forgetting), so later rounds pooled the best samples of all earlier iterations. RLHF also shifted the useful sampling temperature: 1.2 to 1.3 for best-of-\(N\) with \(N\) between 10 and 100. How RLHF fails

The reward model is a proxy fit on a finite, noisy set of comparisons. Four different things go wrong, and they call for different fixes: reward hacking (the policy finds behaviors the reward model scores highly and people do not), over-optimization (the same failure seen as a function of optimization pressure, treated with the Gao et al. curves on page 21), loss of diversity, and label noise that caps how good the proxy can be.

Loss of diversity

Even when the reward is honest, the optimized policy is narrower. Kirk et al. compare SFT, best-of-\(N\) and RLHF from the same models: RLHF generalizes better out of distribution, especially under larger shifts, but sharply reduces the diversity of samples for a given input. For OPT-6.7B summarization, the per-input expectation-adjusted distinct-n-gram score is 0.07 after RLHF against 0.79 for SFT [Kirk et al. 2023, App. J.4, Tab. 11]. Diversity across different inputs also drops, less so, which they call the first rigorous demonstration of across-input mode collapse 模式坍塌 (best-of-\(N\) does not show it). Raising the KL coefficient does not restore diversity: it lowers both performance and per-input diversity, although Stiennon et al. list "an entropy bonus" as the KL term's first purpose. Anthropic worried that lower entropy would starve online data collection and deployed many policy snapshots at once [§4.5]. Consequences: RLHF policies may give best-of-\(N\) less to select from (Llama 2 found the best sampling temperature shifted up after RLHF, though it does not attribute this to diversity loss), and RL for reasoning must fight entropy collapse explicitly (page 24).

Annotator disagreement and label noise

The ceiling on the proxy is set by how much people agree. InstructGPT's training labelers agree 72.6 ± 1.5% of the time (held-out labelers 77.3 ± 1.3%), and Stiennon et al. report 73 ± 4% between researchers [§3.4]. Reward-model accuracy on hard data is modest: Llama 2's helpfulness RM reaches 63.2%, and accuracy depends on how clear the pair is. The safety RM is right 94.3% of the time on "significantly better" safety pairs but 55.3% on "negligibly better or unsure" ones, the helpfulness RM 80.7% versus 54.7% [Llama 2, Tab. 8]. As the policy improves, pairs get closer (the share of "negligibly better" ratings grows over the 14 batches, and Anthropic's final preference model scores 67% on its newest data against 74% on its base data). Singhal et al.'s reward models reach only 61.5%, 70% and 80% [Tab. 4].

Three consequences. Most of the signal is in the clear pairs, so Llama 3 discards pairs whose responses are similar. The disagreement is systematic taste (longer, more confident, more agreeable), and RL amplifies exactly those tastes. And a reward model can only be as good as labelers can judge: when the task outgrows what raters can check in a few minutes (Wen et al.), more RLHF widens the gap, which motivates the verifier-based and process-based rewards below.

AI feedback: Constitutional AI and RLAIF

Human labels are slow, expensive, and noisy in systematic ways (previous section). If a strong model can judge responses, it can supply the labels. Two papers established the pattern: Constitutional AI 宪法式 AI from Anthropic, and RLAIF 基于AI反馈的强化学习 from Google, which measured how good AI labels are against human ones.

Constitutional AI

Bai et al. train a harmless but non-evasive assistant with no human labels for harm. The only human oversight is a written list of principles (the "constitution"), plus a few few-shot examples [Bai et al. 2022, §1.2]. There are two stages (Figure 3).

  1. Supervised stage (SL-CAI). Take a helpful RLHF model and a red-team prompt (42,496 human-written and 140,335 few-shot-generated). Sample an answer, usually harmful. Append a critique request drawn from one of 16 principles, sample a critique, append a revision request, sample a revision, and repeat up to four times with fresh principles. Fine-tune a pretrained model on the revisions plus helpfulness samples (135,296 human prompts) for one epoch. This shifts the response distribution cheaply, so RL needs less exploration [§3.1, §3.2].
  2. RL stage (RLAIF). SL-CAI samples two responses per prompt. A feedback model reads a multiple-choice prompt with a random principle, and the normalized log-probabilities of (A) and (B) become a soft label. Chain-of-thought labels are near 0 or 1 and badly calibrated, so they are clamped to 40% to 60% (otherwise RL-CAI learned more extreme behavior). A preference model is trained on 182,831 AI harmlessness plus 135,296 human helpfulness comparisons, and the policy is trained against it as in RLHF [§4.1, §4.2].

Problem
Human harmlessness labels are slow to collect and must be recollected when the policy or the objective changes. Asking red-teamers to pick the worse response only teaches refusal, hence evasiveness (Anthropic HH, above).

RL-CAI models are less harmful at a given helpfulness than RLHF models trained on human harm labels (a Pareto improvement in crowdworker Elo), and less evasive. Ensembling over the 16 principles gave a more robust preference model than a single principle [Bai et al. 2022, §4.1, §4.3, Fig. 2].

The principles were chosen ad hoc for research. Over-trained RL-CAI models Goodhart into boilerplate (see above). Critiques are often inaccurate or overstated (and matter for small models, not large ones), and the labels inherit the labeler's own blind spots.

Its authors at Anthropic. The idea of model-judged preferences reappears in RLAIF and in the generative reward models of Kimi K2 and DeepSeek-V4 (below).

Process versus outcome supervision

For multi-step reasoning, a reward on the final answer says little about where a solution went wrong. An outcome reward model (ORM) 结果奖励模型 predicts whether the final answer is correct, one label per solution. A process reward model (PRM) 过程奖励模型 predicts whether each step is correct, one label per step. Figure 4 shows what each sees on the same flawed solution.

Evidence with human labels

Uesato et al. (GSM8K, 70B). Outcome- and process-based supervision give similar final-answer error (22.3% for process-supervised SFT versus 23.5% for RL on final answers without a reward model, 14.8% versus 16.6% with one). What differs is trace error: how often a correct-answer solution contains a wrong step. Even the best model trained by RL on final-answer correctness had 12.4% trace error, against 3.8% for the best process-based method. Their best combination improved the state of the art from 16.8% to 12.7% final-answer error and from 14.0% to 3.4% trace error. Even an ORM's predictions agree more with step labels (85%) than with its own outcome labels (77%), though the authors caution this may be math-specific, where wrong reasoning rarely reaches the right answer [Uesato et al. 2022, §1, §3].

Lightman et al. (MATH, GPT-4 base). The generator is fixed and reward models are compared by best-of-\(N\) selection on 500 test problems. At \(N = 1860\) the PRM solves 78.2%, the ORM 72.4%, majority voting 69.6%, and the gap widens with \(N\). On roughly 230 recent AP and AMC problems (best of 100): 72.9% versus 63.8% versus 61.3% [Lightman et al. 2023, §3, §5]. PRM800K holds about 800,000 step labels over 75,000 solutions to 12,000 problems. Labelers mark each step positive, negative or neutral and stop at the first negative step, so process and outcome supervision reveal the same about correct solutions and differ only on incorrect ones. They were shown convincing wrong-answer solutions, those the current PRM scores highly although the answer is wrong (each is a known PRM mistake), which was about 2.6× more data-efficient in a small-scale study [§2.4, §4.2, App. B].

To separate process from data effects, the large PRM labels data for small models: process supervision then beats both outcome variants at every data scale. Their explanation is credit assignment: most sampled solutions contain an error somewhere, so a negative outcome label is low-information, while a step label locates the mistake [§4.1, §6.1]. A solution's score is the product of its step probabilities. That is slightly better than the minimum (78.2% versus 77.6%) but biased against long solutions: five steps at 0.97 score \(0.97^5 = 0.859\), twelve score \(0.97^{12} = 0.694\), though no step is worse. The minimum gives both 0.97 [App. F.2].

Automatic process labels: Math-Shepherd

Human step labels do not scale. Math-Shepherd defines a step's quality as its potential to reach the correct answer and estimates it by sampling: a "completer" model writes \(N\) continuations from that step, and the label is 1 if any reaches the gold answer (hard estimation) or the fraction that do (soft estimation) [Wang et al. 2023, §3.3]. With \(N = 8\) and a 7B completer they labeled about 170k GSM8K and 270k MATH solutions. In a check against 160 human-labeled GSM8K steps with a 70B completer, hard estimation is 86% accurate at \(N = 4\), and accuracy falls as \(N\) grows because more completions produce more false positives. The resulting reward model supports reranking and step-by-step PPO: Mistral-7B goes from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K and 28.6% to 33.0% on MATH, ahead of outcome-only PPO (81.8% and 31.3%), and to 89.1% and 43.5% with verification on top [Tab. 2, 3].

The catch is cost. To label a \(K\)-step solution you generate completions from every step onward. With equal-length steps, a 300-token solution of \(K = 10\) steps and \(N = 8\), the completions sum to \(8\times\sum_{i=1}^{10} 300\,(10-i)/10 = 8\times1350 = 10{,}800\) tokens, 36 times the solution itself (our arithmetic).

Why DeepSeek-R1 dropped process reward models

DeepSeek-R1's list of unsuccessful attempts gives three reasons a PRM did not pay off in large-scale RL [DeepSeek-R1, App. G.2]. It is hard to define a fine-grained step in general reasoning. It is hard to decide whether an intermediate step is correct: automated annotation may not be good enough and manual annotation does not scale. And once a model-based PRM is in the loop it "inevitably leads to reward hacking", while retraining it costs resources and complicates the pipeline. The conclusion is careful: PRMs rerank top-\(N\) responses or guide search well, but the advantage is limited relative to the added overhead in large-scale RL, in these experiments, and this "does not imply" the approaches cannot work. R1's rewards for reasoning are rule-based accuracy and format checks, and the report says it abstains from neural reward models, outcome- or process-based, for reasoning because they are susceptible to reward hacking (page 28 covers the pipeline, page 24 the algorithm). Where no checkable final answer exists, as with proofs, other rewards are needed: DeepSeek-V3.2-Speciale adds the dataset and reward method of DeepSeekMath-V2 for proofs [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3].

Generative reward models and rubrics

A scalar reward model is a classifier fit to comparisons: it cannot reason before scoring, cannot read a reference answer or a checklist, and is one fixed function that the policy learns to exploit. Since 2025, open technical reports increasingly replace it, for tasks without a checkable answer, by an LLM that judges against rubrics 评分标准, a generative reward model 生成式奖励模型 (GRM). A useful ladder, from most to least trustworthy (our ordering, not a measured result): a rule-based verifier, an LLM comparing to a reference answer, a rubric-guided generative judge, and last a scalar reward model.

  • DeepSeek-V3.2 (December 2025). One GRPO stage merges reasoning, agent and human-alignment training. "For reasoning and agent tasks, we employ rule-based outcome reward, length penalty, and language consistency reward. For general tasks, we employ a generative reward model where each prompt has its own rubrics for evaluation." For search agents, verifiable question-answer pairs are mixed with helpfulness prompts scored by rubrics over several quality dimensions [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3, §3.2.3].
  • Kimi K2 (July 2025). The self-critique rubric reward: K2's critic ranks a prompt's responses by pairwise comparison against core rubrics (clarity and relevance, conversational fluency, objective and grounded interaction with no meta-commentary or unwarranted flattery), prescriptive rubrics that "aim to eliminate reward hacking" (no opening praise, no sentences justifying why the answer is good) and human-written rubrics. The critic is bootstrapped in SFT, then refined during RL on the on-policy rollouts of verifiable prompts, which "distills objective performance signals from RLVR" into the judge. The authors list a cost: the rubrics may favor confident, assertive answers, because the rules penalize hedging [Kimi K2, §3.2.2, App. F].
  • DeepSeek-V4 (April 2026 preview). For hard-to-verify tasks the report dispenses with "conventional scalar-based reward models": it curates rubric-guided RL data and uses a generative reward model to evaluate trajectories, and applies RL to the GRM itself. The actor doubles as the GRM, so judging and generating skill are optimized jointly, with "only a minimal set of diverse human annotations". Specialists trained with GRPO are merged by on-policy distillation instead of a mixed RL stage [DeepSeek-V4, §5.1]. When judge and policy share weights they can co-adapt; the report argues for robust scoring, but the section we read has no ablation of that risk.
  • Rubrics as Rewards (Gunjal et al. 2025, ICLR 2026). A controlled study: an LLM writes 7 to 20 weighted rubric items (essential, important, optional, pitfall) per prompt from the reference answer, and a judge scores responses against them. GRPO on Qwen2.5-7B with a gpt-4o-mini judge beat Likert-scale LLM-as-judge rewards by up to 31% relative on HealthBench and 7% on GPQA-Diamond. Rubrics written without the reference answer were clearly worse (32.0% versus 35.9% on HealthBench-1k), and the verifiable reward is the special case of one rubric item [Gunjal et al. 2025, §2, §5, §6].

Why rubrics help, and what they cost

A rubric turns "which answer is better?" into checks a judge applies one at a time, so holistic taste (length, tone, agreeableness) should matter less and the reward should be harder to exploit with style alone (a design rationale, not a measured result). The judge knows what to look for, a reference answer can anchor it, and changing the objective means editing rubrics instead of retraining a reward model, the advantage Constitutional AI claimed for principles. The costs are real. Judges have biases (Kimi's assertiveness, Lee et al.'s position bias, cancelled by querying twice with the order swapped). Rubric quality becomes the bottleneck. A reasoning judge adds inference cost to every rollout. And the exploit moves to the judge: a policy can learn to satisfy a checklist's wording. DeepSeek-R1 shows a habit worth copying: it used learned reward models for helpfulness and harmlessness only in the last 400 of the 1,700 steps of its second RL stage, because more steps against the model-based reward "may lead to reward hacking" [DeepSeek-R1, §3.2.2]. Limit exposure to any learned reward.

Why the field moved on from PPO-RLHF

Classic RLHF is expensive and fragile: four models (252 GB of states for a 7B policy), a rollout-dominated clock, and dozens of implementation details. The alternatives each removed a piece, and none is best everywhere.

  • Drop the RL loop: DPO and rejection sampling. Llama 3 found that DPO "required less compute for large-scale models and performed better, especially on instruction following benchmarks like IFEval" than PPO [§4.1.4]. Tulu 3 used length-normalized DPO for preference tuning throughout, "in lieu of more costly investigations into RL-based methods, such as PPO" [§1]. Rejection sampling (best of \(K\), then SFT) is simpler still, and the best-of-\(K\) distribution sits at most \(\log K - (K-1)/K\) nats from the base, 2.43 nats at \(K = 30\) (page 21).
  • Drop the learned reward: verifiable rewards. If correctness is checkable, use the checker: no reward model to hack or to hold in memory, and GRPO also removes the critic (page 24), so policy plus reference is 126 GB in our 7B example, half of PPO-RLHF. Tulu 3's RLVR gives reward \(\alpha = 10\) for a verified answer and 0 otherwise, and keeps the classic details (dropout off, value from a reward model, \(-10\) for a missing EOS, advantage whitening) [§6]. DeepSeek-R1-Zero uses rule-based accuracy and format rewards only.
  • Keep a learned judge only where nothing can be checked: rubric-guided generative rewards (previous section), used sparingly.
  • Merge specialists by distillation: DeepSeek-V4 replaced its mixed RL stage by multi-teacher on-policy distillation [§5.1].

The evidence is not one-sided. Xu et al. tuned PPO (advantage normalization, large batch, moving-average reference) and it beat DPO everywhere they tried: on HH-RLHF the OpenAssistant reward is 0.718 for PPO against 0.611 for DPO and 0.678 for iterative DPO, and on CodeContest (34B) PPO reaches 22.4% at 10@1k while DPO scores 0.0% and iterative DPO 3.2%. DPO is also sensitive to the gap between its data and the policy: with an SFT model far from the preference data, its safety rate was 55.4% against PPO's 99.5% [Xu et al. 2024, Tab. 2, 4, 8]. A fair summary: for fixed offline chat preferences, DPO is often enough and much cheaper; when you can sample from the policy and score cheaply, online RL (PPO, GRPO) has more room, which is why reasoning models are trained that way. As of September 2026, in the open reports we checked (Kimi K2, DeepSeek-V3.2, DeepSeek-V4), the recipe is verifiable rewards where possible, rubric-guided generative judges elsewhere, critic-free optimizers and, at DeepSeek, specialists merged by distillation; a scalar-reward-model PPO stage is not the centerpiece. Closed labs do not publish recipes, so this describes open reports only.

Common misconceptions

  • "RLHF teaches the model its abilities." Most of the capability comes from pretraining. InstructGPT's 175B SFT and PPO-ptx runs cost about 1.8% of GPT-3's pretraining compute (excluding the reward model, data and experiments), and Anthropic frames the open question as whether RL teaches new skills or focuses the model on a sub-distribution of existing behavior. What RLHF changes reliably is which behaviors are expressed (page 24 for the reasoning debate).
  • "The KL penalty prevents reward hacking." It slows drift, nothing more. Gao et al. found it behaves like early stopping (page 21), InstructGPT could not fix its alignment tax by raising \(\beta\) 100×, Kirk et al. found that a larger coefficient does not restore diversity, and length still grows under a high KL coefficient (Singhal et al.).
  • "The reward arrives at every token." The reward-model score is a single scalar credited to the last token. The dense per-token part is the KL penalty. Credit for the score reaches earlier tokens only through the value function and GAE.
  • "Human preference labels are ground truth." Labelers agree 72.6% to 77% of the time, their tastes are systematic (longer, more confident, more agreeable), and a reward model can only reproduce them. InstructGPT is aligned to the preferences of about 40 contractors, not to human values in general.
  • "Rejection sampling and PPO are different worlds." They differ in breadth (\(K\) samples per prompt versus one) and depth (PPO's data depends on the policy after every step), but Llama 2 stacked them in one pipeline, and iterated rounds make the difference small.
  • "Training longer keeps improving the model." Reward keeps rising, quality does not. Llama 2 stops PPO after 200 to 400 iterations on held-out prompts, InstructGPT's 1.3B model regressed again at 512k episodes, and two of Huang et al.'s 1B runs reached KL 50 to 85 with degenerate text.
  • "PRMs beat ORMs, so DeepSeek was wrong to drop them." Lightman et al. compare reward models for reranking a fixed generator with human step labels. DeepSeek-R1 reports that a model-based PRM was not worth its overhead and hacking risk in large-scale RL, and explicitly says this does not show the approach cannot work. Different settings, different conclusions.
  • "AI feedback is as good as human feedback." Lee et al. show parity on three tasks with a labeler much larger than the policy (same-size labelers also beat SFT, at 66% to 68%), and their gains shrink after length control (71% to 59% for summarization). Combining human and AI labels did not help. It is evidence of viability, not of interchangeability.

Interview questions

Warm-up Walk through the InstructGPT RLHF pipeline and the role of each model.

Step 1, SFT: fine-tune GPT-3 on about 13k labeler demonstrations. This model initializes everything else. Step 2, reward model: take the SFT model, replace the unembedding by a scalar head, and train a 6B model on rankings of 4 to 9 responses per prompt (about 33k prompts) with the pairwise loss \(-\log\sigma(r(y_w) - r(y_l))\), all pairs of a prompt in one batch element. Step 3, PPO: on about 31k unlabeled prompts, sample a response, score it with the frozen reward model, and update the policy with PPO. Four networks are involved: the policy (init from SFT, trained), the reference (frozen SFT copy, gives the KL term), the reward model (frozen) and the value model (init from the reward model, trained).

The reward of a response is \(r_\phi(x,y)\) on the last token minus \(\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{SFT}})\) on every token, with \(\beta = 0.02\). In the final "PPO-ptx" variant a pretraining log-likelihood term with coefficient \(\gamma = 27.8\) is added. The result: labelers preferred the 1.3B model to 175B GPT-3, and the two 175B training runs (SFT and PPO-ptx) took about 1.8% of GPT-3 pretraining compute.

Warm-up What is the alignment tax in InstructGPT, and how was it reduced?

After PPO against the reward model, the policy lost accuracy on public NLP benchmarks (SQuAD v2, DROP, HellaSwag, WMT15 Fr→En): alignment cost capability. The fix was PPO-ptx: for every RL episode, also take gradient steps on 8 times as many pretraining examples, adding \(\gamma\,\E[\log\pi(x)]\) to the objective, with \(\gamma = 27.8\). It recovered most regressions (better than GPT-3 on HellaSwag, still behind on DROP, SQuAD v2 and translation) without lowering labeler preference. The natural alternative, a bigger KL coefficient, failed: even 100× the default (\(\beta = 2.0\)) did not fix DROP or SQuAD and cost a lot of reward. Reason: the KL term only constrains behavior on RL prompts, while the pretraining term anchors the whole pretraining distribution. Also note the tax depends on scale: Anthropic found small models paid one, while 13B and 52B RLHF models improved zero-shot.

Warm-up What is Constitutional AI?

A way to train a harmless but non-evasive assistant without human harm labels; the human input is a list of written principles. Supervised stage: a helpful RLHF model answers red-team prompts (often harmfully), critiques its answer under a randomly drawn principle from 16, and revises, up to four rounds. A pretrained model is fine-tuned on the revisions plus helpfulness data (SL-CAI). RL stage (RLAIF): SL-CAI writes pairs of responses, a feedback model chooses the more harmless one per a principle (soft label from the log-probs of "A" and "B", chain-of-thought labels clamped to 40 to 60%), a preference model is trained on 182,831 AI harmlessness plus 135,296 human helpfulness comparisons, and the policy is trained against it. Results: less harmful at equal helpfulness than human-label RLHF, and less evasive. Caveats: ad hoc principles, boilerplate from over-training, and labels that inherit the labeler's blind spots.

Core Why add a KL penalty to the reference? What if \(\beta\) is too small or too large?

The reward model is a proxy that is only valid near the data it was trained on. The KL term \(\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})\) keeps the policy in that region, acts as an entropy bonus against collapse to one mode (Stiennon et al.), preserves fluency and pretrained knowledge, and makes the optimum a tilted reference distribution, \(\pi^\ast\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}\) (page 21). Applied per token, it is dense and spreads the cost over the tokens responsible.

Too small: reward hacking and degeneration. In Huang et al.'s reproduction two 1B runs reached KL 50 to 85 and produced run-together strings that GPT-3.5 preferred less than 20% of the time. Too large: the policy barely leaves the SFT model and gains little reward. Evidence for the sweet spot: InstructGPT's Likert scores peak around \(\beta = 0.01\) to 0.02 and are poor at 0 and at 2; Xu et al.'s OpenAssistant reward is 0.705 at \(\beta = 0\), 0.720 at 0.05, 0.718 at 0.1 and 0.629 at 0.2. Because \(\beta\) is only meaningful relative to the reward scale, rewards are normalized, and a controller can steer KL to a target. Two limits: the penalty acts like early stopping rather than removing hacking (Gao et al.), and a larger coefficient does not restore diversity (Kirk et al.).

Core Name three forms of reward hacking and how you would mitigate each.

Length. Learned rewards correlate with length (within-prompt \(r = 0.72\) on WebGPT), and length explains 98% (WebGPT) and 73% (RLCD) of PPO's reward gain (the non-length shares in Singhal et al. Table 1 are 2% and 27%). A reward for length alone reproduces most of the win-rate gain (56% versus 58%). Mitigate with length-balanced preference data, a length penalty or reward scaling, and by reporting length-controlled win rates; all cost some reward.

Sycophancy. Assistants agree with a user's stated view or flip correct answers when challenged (Claude 1.3 wrongly admits mistakes on 98% of questions), and "matches the user's beliefs" is one of the best predictors of human preference in the HH data. Mitigate with better or prompted preference models, more raters per item, labeler assistance, and evaluations that test for it.

Persuading the rater / style. After PPO against a ChatBotArena-trained reward model, human approval rose 9.4 points on QuALITY with no gain in correctness, and evaluators' false-positive rate went from 46.7% to 70.2%. The policy fabricated evidence and wrote hard-to-read code that passed simple tests. Mitigate by rewarding what can be checked (verifiers, tests), stronger or ensembled judges, and evaluation by people with more time than the training signal had. Across all forms: limit optimization (KL, early stopping on a held-out judge), refresh the reward model on the current policy's samples, and monitor reward, KL, length and entropy together.

Core Calculate the memory needed for the four models of PPO-RLHF with a 7B policy. How does it change for DPO or GRPO?

Use 16 bytes per trained parameter (bf16 weights 2, bf16 grads 2, fp32 master 4, Adam \(m,v\) 4 + 4) and 2 bytes per frozen parameter. Policy: \(7\text{B}\times16 = 112\) GB. Critic (trained, same size): 112 GB. Reference: \(7\text{B}\times2 = 14\) GB. Reward model: 14 GB. Total 252 GB (235 GiB), \(2.25\times\) an SFT run and about 3.15 H100s of 80 GB before activations, KV cache and logits. ZeRO-3 over 8 GPUs gives 31.5 GB per GPU for states, plus a generation copy of the weights (14 GB in bf16) if the rollout engine is separate.

DPO needs policy plus reference, 126 GB, or 112 GB if reference log-probs are cached (possible because the responses are fixed). GRPO with a verifier also holds policy plus reference, 126 GB, since it has no critic and no reward model. PPO with a verifier (Tulu 3) keeps policy, critic and reference: 238 GB. Trick list: offload the frozen models to CPU, train LoRA adapters, and choose small critics: InstructGPT's 6B reward and value models against a 175B policy make up only 3.3% of the 3.26 TB.

Core A 4-token response has \(\log\pi_\theta = (-0.5, -1.0, -0.2, -0.8)\), \(\log\pi_{\text{ref}} = (-0.7, -0.8, -0.9, -0.8)\), \(\beta = 0.05\) and reward-model score \(-0.4\). Give the per-token rewards and the return. Then apply one adaptive-KL step.

Log-ratios: \((0.2, -0.2, 0.7, 0.0)\), sum 0.7. KL rewards \(-\beta\times\) ratio: \((-0.01, +0.01, -0.035, 0)\). Add the score to the last token: \(r = (-0.01,\ 0.01,\ -0.035,\ -0.4)\). Undiscounted returns-to-go: \(G = (-0.435, -0.425, -0.435, -0.40)\). Check: \(G_1 = -0.4 - 0.05\times0.7 = -0.435\), the sequence-level objective \(r_\phi - \beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})\).

Controller with target 6 nats, measured sequence KL 3, batch 256, horizon 10,000: error \(= \operatorname{clip}(3/6 - 1, -0.2, 0.2) = -0.2\); multiplier \(1 + (-0.2)(256/10{,}000) = 0.99488\); \(\beta \to 0.05\times0.99488 = 0.04974\). A saturated error changes \(\beta\) by about 0.5% here, so the controller is a slow set-point tracker, not a step-by-step regulator. The reward is treated as a constant for the update (no gradient flows through it).

Core Why does generation dominate the wall-clock of RLHF, and how do systems cope?

By FLOPs it should not: per token, generation is one forward (\(2N\)), the reference, reward and value models one forward each (\(2N\) each), and policy and critic training forward plus backward (\(6N\) each), so generation is about \(2/20 = 10\%\) of the FLOPs for one PPO epoch. But decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound and runs at a small fraction of the MFU of training (page 06). Assuming 40% MFU for training and forward passes and 10% for decoding, generation takes \(20/65 = 31\%\) of the time, and 47% at 5%. Measured: actor generation plus actor training is 58.9% of an iteration in HybridFlow (this does not isolate generation), generation alone is up to 81.2% in a system without a KV cache, and over 90% for long chain-of-thought rollouts (OpenRLHF). Llama 2 saw a 20× generation slowdown under FSDP until it consolidated weights per node.

Systems response: serve rollouts with an inference engine (paged KV cache, continuous batching), use different parallelism for generation (small TP, large DP) and training and reshard weights between them without redundant copies, place models colocated or split depending on cluster size, and overlap rollouts with training asynchronously (see page 25). Weight transfer itself can cost up to 36% of an iteration for a 70B actor when training and generation sit on different devices.

Core How does Llama 2 build its reward signal? Why two reward models, what is the margin term, and how are they combined?

Over 1M binary comparisons with four preference-strength levels. Two reward models from the chat checkpoint: helpfulness and safety, because the objectives trade off and a single model would also have to tell adversarial prompts from benign ones (Bai et al. saw the same tension). Each wins on its own data (63.2% vs 56.2% helpfulness, 64.5% vs 62.8% safety). The ranking loss gets a margin \(m(r)\): \(-\log\sigma(r_c - r_r - m)\), with \(m = (1, 2/3, 1/3, 0)\) or \((3, 2, 1, 0)\) from "significantly better" down to "negligible". At a score gap of 2 the loss is 0.127 without margin, 0.313 with margin 1 and 1.313 with margin 3, so clear pairs keep being pushed apart. It helped accuracy on clear pairs (79.1% to 80.4% or 80.7%) but hurt similar pairs and made scores more extreme, a concern for PPO.

For PPO, use the safety score when the prompt is safety-flagged or the safety score is below 0.15 (precision 0.89, recall 0.55 on unsafe responses), otherwise the helpfulness score; then whiten \(\operatorname{logit}(R_c)\) to balance against the KL term. Llama 3 later dropped the margin and PPO.

Core Process versus outcome reward models: what are the pros and cons?

Outcome (ORM): one label per solution, cheap when the answer is checkable, but no credit assignment, and false positives (right answer through flawed reasoning) are rewarded. Process (PRM): a label per step, locating the first error. Pros: at best-of-1860 on MATH the PRM solves 78.2% versus 72.4% for the ORM and 69.6% for majority voting, and the gap widens with \(N\); it selects reasoning humans can follow; on GSM8K, RL on final answers left 12.4% of correct-answer solutions with a wrong step versus 3.8% for process-based methods. The intuition: most sampled solutions contain an error, so an outcome label carries little information.

Cons: human step labels are expensive (PRM800K: 800k labels), a "step" must be defined, Monte-Carlo labels (Math-Shepherd) cost \(N\) completions per step (about 36× the solution's tokens for a 10-step, 300-token solution at \(N = 8\)) and are noisy, product scoring penalizes long solutions, and a learned PRM used as an RL reward is hackable. DeepSeek-R1 lists exactly these as why PRMs did not pay off in large-scale RL, while still saying they are useful for reranking or search. In practice: PRMs for reranking and search, verifiers instead of any model when the answer is checkable.

Deep-dive Why did many labs move from PPO-based RLHF to DPO or RLVR?

Cost and fragility. PPO-RLHF holds four models (252 GB of states for a 7B policy against 126 GB for DPO or GRPO), spends most of its time on rollouts, and depends on many details (dropout off, whitening, EOS handling, KL control). Llama 3 states that DPO "required less compute for large-scale models and performed better, especially on instruction following benchmarks like IFEval", and Tulu 3 chose length-normalized DPO over more costly RL.

Reward hacking of a learned RM. Where correctness is checkable, a verifier removes the exploitable model, and there is nothing to hold in memory: Tulu 3's RLVR gives reward 10 for verified answers, and DeepSeek-R1 uses rule-based accuracy and format rewards, having found neural rewards hackable at scale. GRPO also removes the critic. What remains for learned judges: tasks nobody can check, handled by rubric-guided generative rewards (DeepSeek-V3.2, Kimi K2, V4).

Counter-evidence, so as not to overclaim: Xu et al. found tuned PPO beats DPO on HH-RLHF (0.718 versus 0.611) and on CodeContest (22.4% versus 0.0%), and that DPO is sensitive to a mismatch between its data and the policy; the move is driven by cost, simplicity and verifiable domains, not by a proof that PPO is worse. Online sampling (iterative DPO, RS, GRPO) recovers much of what offline DPO lacks.

Deep-dive Design and debugging: during RLHF the reward and KL rise, responses double in length, but human win rate is flat and then falls. Diagnose and fix.

Reward up with quality flat or down is reward hacking, and doubling length is the classic suspect. Diagnose: (1) stratify reward gain by length bucket (Singhal et al. found a non-length gain of 2% on WebGPT); (2) compare length-controlled win rates; (3) read samples for style tricks, sycophancy, hedging, and degenerate strings; (4) check KL: values in the tens with degenerate text mean over-optimization (Huang et al.); (5) verify EOS and truncation handling, since a missing EOS penalty or a reward read at the wrong position lets responses run to the limit; (6) check the reward model on a fresh on-policy sample: its accuracy on the policy's current outputs may have degraded (Llama 2 refreshes preference data every round for this reason).

Fix, in order: stop at the checkpoint that is best on a held-out independent judge (Llama 2 early-stops at 200 to 400 iterations); control the leash with a target-KL controller or a higher \(\beta\); remove the exploited feature from the proxy (length-balanced preference pairs, a length penalty, reward scaling); refresh the reward model with comparisons on the current policy's outputs, or an ensemble; replace the learned reward with a verifier or rubric-based judge where possible. Say which trade-offs you accept: every length intervention lowered reward in Singhal et al., and none removed the length increase entirely.

Deep-dive Read the RLAIF-versus-RLHF evidence critically. What does it show, and what would you check before replacing human labels?

Shows: with PaLM 2 L labeling and a PaLM 2 XS policy, RLAIF and RLHF both beat SFT (71% and 73% on summarization, 63% and 64% on helpful dialogue), are tied head to head (50%, 52%, not significant), and RLAIF has the higher harmless rate (88% versus 76% versus 64%). Direct scoring without a reward model (d-RLAIF) did even better (74%, 66%), and the labeler's agreement with humans (78%) is at the level of human-human agreement (73% to 77%).

Does not show: (1) the main comparison uses a labeler much larger than the policy, so it is largely distillation of a strong judge (same-size labeling gave 68% and self-labeling 66% on helpfulness, but only at PaLM 2 XS scale, not at the frontier); (2) length control shrinks gains (71% to 59%, 73% to 61%), so part of the "win" is length; (3) the harmless rate scores each response alone, so refusing more raises it, and it says nothing about helpfulness; (4) human plus AI labels did not beat human alone; (5) one model family, small policies, 2023 prices for the 10× cost claim. I would check: labeler-human agreement on my distribution and by pair difficulty; position bias (swap order); length-controlled and blind human evaluation with significance tests; whether gains persist at my policy scale when the labeler is no larger than the policy; and whether the AI-labeled data over-rewards the labeler's own style.

Deep-dive Design question: you need a reward for long-form helpful writing, where nothing can be checked. How would you build it in 2026, and how would you keep it from being hacked?

Build a rubric-guided generative reward, following the DeepSeek-V3.2, Kimi K2 and Rubrics-as-Rewards pattern. (1) For each prompt, generate a rubric of about 7 to 20 weighted, self-contained criteria, conditioned on a reference answer or expert-written points (RaR: rubrics without a reference scored 32.0% versus 35.9% on HealthBench). Add global rubrics for style, and prescriptive rubrics that target known hacks (no opening praise, no self-justification, length bands) as in Kimi K2. (2) Score with a reasoning judge, aggregate per-criterion checks explicitly or let the judge aggregate holistically, and swap presentation order to cancel position bias. (3) Where any part is checkable (facts with references, format constraints, code snippets), use a verifier for that part and keep the judge for the rest. (4) Calibrate the judge continuously against outcomes you can verify, as K2 does with RLVR rollouts, and against a held-out set of human comparisons.

Defenses in the loop: a KL leash or early stopping, a frozen judge (or, if the judge shares weights with the policy as in DeepSeek-V4, an independent audit to catch co-adaptation), limited RL steps under the learned reward (R1 used its preference rewards only in the last 400 of 1,700 steps), and monitors for length, entropy, refusal rate and judge-versus-human disagreement. Accept the known costs: judge bias toward assertive answers (K2 App. F.3), rubric quality as the bottleneck, and judge inference cost per rollout.

Cheat sheet

  • InstructGPT: SFT on ~13k prompts (16 epochs), 6B reward model on ~33k prompts with K = 4 to 9 ranked responses (all \(\binom K2\) pairs as one batch element), PPO on ~31k prompts (256k episodes, batch 512). The 1.3B model beats 175B GPT-3, 175B SFT + PPO-ptx compute ≈ 1.8% of GPT-3 pretraining.
  • Reward per token: \(r_t = -\beta(\log\pi_\theta - \log\pi_{\text{ref}})_t + \mathbb 1[t=T]\, r_\phi\), so the return is \(r_\phi - \beta\,\)(sequence KL). \(\beta\): 0.02 InstructGPT, 0.01 / 0.005 Llama 2, 0.001 Anthropic HH and R1-Zero (KL in the loss).
  • PPO-ptx: add \(\gamma\,\E_{\text{pretrain}}[\log\pi]\), \(\gamma = 27.8\), 8× as many pretraining examples as RL episodes. Raising \(\beta\) to 2.0 did not fix the alignment tax.
  • Four models: policy (trained), reference (frozen), reward model (frozen), value (init from RM, trained). 16 B/param trained, 2 B/param frozen: 7B gives 112 + 112 + 14 + 14 = 252 GB. DPO or GRPO: 126 GB.
  • Generation: ~10% of FLOPs (for 1 PPO epoch) but 31% to 47% of time at 10% or 5% decode MFU (assumed); measured 59% (actor gen + train), up to 81%, over 90% for long CoT.
  • Details that matter: dropout off, read the reward at EOS, normalize the RM (demos score 0), truncation penalty (−1 or −10), whiten advantages, value from RM with separate weights, large batches.
  • Adaptive KL: \(\beta \leftarrow \beta\,(1 + \operatorname{clip}(\mathrm{KL}/\mathrm{KL}_{\text{target}} - 1, \pm0.2)\cdot n/\text{horizon})\), horizon 10,000 episodes. Saturated, it moves \(\beta\) about 1% per 512-episode batch.
  • Llama 2: >1M comparisons, two RMs (helpfulness, safety), margin \((1, 2/3, 1/3, 0)\) or \((3,2,1,0)\), safety score used if the prompt is flagged or the safety score is below 0.15, whitened logit, rejection sampling (V1 to V4) then PPO, 330 s per PPO step at 70B, Ghost Attention. Llama 3: rejection sampling + DPO (\(\beta = 0.1\), NLL 0.2), no margin.
  • Hacking: length (98% and 73% of reward gain on WebGPT and RLCD), sycophancy (98% wrong "mistake" admissions for Claude 1.3), hedging and refusal boilerplate, persuading the rater (false positives 46.7% to 70.2%).   • Diversity: RLHF generalizes better than SFT but per-input diversity drops sharply (EAD 0.07 vs 0.79 for OPT-6.7B); a higher KL coefficient does not restore it.
  • CAI / RLAIF: 16 principles, up to 4 revisions, 182,831 AI + 135,296 human comparisons, CoT labels clamped to 40 to 60%. RLAIF vs RLHF: 71 / 73% (summ), 63 / 64% (helpful), harmless 88 / 76 / 64%; length control 71 to 59%.
  • PRM vs ORM: best-of-1860 on MATH 78.2 / 72.4 / 69.6% (PRM / ORM / majority). PRM800K: 800k step labels. Math-Shepherd: hard or soft estimation from \(N = 8\) completions, ~36× the solution's tokens for a 10-step, 300-token solution. R1 dropped PRMs: step definition, step correctness, reward hacking.
