RLVR & reasoning models: GRPO and successors

P0 ~80 min Prereqs: 22 Policy gradient to PPO, 23 RLHF in practice

Reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 are trained by sampling many long chains of thought, checking the final answers with a program, and reinforcing what passed. This page writes GRPO exactly as DeepSeekMath does, walks through R1-Zero and the R1 pipeline, treats Dr. GRPO, DAPO, GSPO and CISPO as answers to specific failures, and closes with the open debate on whether RL teaches models anything new, plus a recipe for a small run of your own.

  • The bias analysis: length bias from \(1/|o_i|\), difficulty bias from the group std, and how Dr. GRPO and DAPO's token-level loss remove them. Expect to compute per-token gradient weights for a 100-token and a 1,000-token response.
  • R1-Zero versus R1: what the reward was, what the cold start is for (readability, not capability), the four-stage pipeline, and what the "aha moment" does and does not show.

  • Debate literacy and practice: pass@k evidence that RL only sharpens, the counter-evidence, and how you would design and monitor a real RLVR run.

RLVR: reinforcement learning on rewards you can check

Page 23 covered RLHF: a reward model trained on human preferences scores each response and PPO maximizes that score under a KL leash. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards 可验证奖励强化学习 (RLVR) keeps the RL machinery and replaces the learned reward model with a program. The term comes from Tülu 3, which pays a constant \(\alpha=10\) when the answer extracted from a completion is verifiably correct and 0 otherwise, trained with PPO plus a KL penalty on GSM8K, MATH and instruction-following constraints [Lambert et al. 2024, §6]. DeepSeek-R1 then showed that the same idea at scale, on a strong base model, produces long chains of thought 思维链 with reflection and verification, without human-written reasoning traces [DeepSeek-R1, §2].

A serious math reward fits on one screen. The subtle parts are brace matching, normalization, and refusing credit when no boxed answer exists.

import re
from fractions import Fraction

def extract_boxed(text):
    """Content of the LAST \boxed{...} (brace-matched), or None."""
    i = text.rfind("\\boxed{")
    if i < 0:
        return None
    i += len("\\boxed{")
    depth, j = 1, i
    while j < len(text) and depth:
        depth += (text[j] == "{") - (text[j] == "}")
        j += 1
    return text[i:j - 1] if depth == 0 else None

def normalize(ans):
    ans = ans.strip().replace(" ", "").replace("\\!", "").replace("$", "").rstrip(".")
    ans = re.sub(r"\\d?frac\{(-?\d+)\}\{(\d+)\}", r"\1/\2", ans)   # \frac{1}{2} -> 1/2
    try:
        return str(Fraction(ans))          # numeric equivalence: "0.50", "1/2" and "\frac{1}{2}" agree
    except (ValueError, ZeroDivisionError):
        return ans

def accuracy_reward(response, gold):
    pred = extract_boxed(response)
    return float(pred is not None and normalize(pred) == normalize(gold))

def format_reward(response):               # R1-style structure check
    return float(re.fullmatch(r"(?s)\s*<think>.+?</think>\s*.+", response) is not None)

Why verifiable rewards resist hacking, and where they do not

A reward model is a function approximator fit to finitely many human judgments, and an optimizer that queries it millions of times finds its blind spots (page 23). R1 therefore abstains from neural reward models, "whether outcome-based or process-based", on reasoning tasks because they "are susceptible to reward hacking 奖励黑客 during large-scale reinforcement learning" [DeepSeek-R1, §2.2]. Its appendix shows the failure with the helpfulness reward model: reward keeps rising while Codeforces pass@1 falls (App. B.5, Fig. 6), which is why preference rewards are confined to the last 400 of 1,700 steps of the final stage (§3.2.2). "Resist" is not "immune". Four limits:

  1. The checker is the specification. Whatever it accepts is optimized: a parser that takes the first number, tests that a lookup table passes, numeric answers that a guess can hit. Yue et al. read the correct chains of thought of the hardest solved problems by hand to rule out lucky guesses [Yue et al. 2025, §3.1]. Rules also produce false negatives, so Qwen3's general RL adds an LLM judge that compares with a reference answer, "avoiding false negatives that can occur with purely rule-based rewards" [Qwen3, §4.4].
  2. Only checkable domains. R1 lists writing as a domain without a reliable reward [DeepSeek-R1, §6]. DeepSeek-V4 uses rubric-guided data and a generative reward model whose weights are the actor's own, with RL applied to the judging skill [DeepSeek-V4, §5.1.1].
  3. One bit per trajectory. A 20,000-token response earns a single 0 or 1, so credit assignment is left to sampling statistics. This is why group size and baseline design matter below.
  4. No signal on unsolvable prompts. If no sample ever passes, the gradient is zero. RLVR cannot teach what the model cannot sample (see the capability debate).
From your jet tagger

Your tagger had a differentiable loss on a label attached to every jet, so one forward pass gave an exact gradient. In RLVR the "label" is a scalar reward on a sequence the model sampled itself, and sampling is not differentiable. The gradient is a score-function estimate (page 22): unbiased but noisy. Everything below lowers that noise (group baselines), limits how far the policy moves from the sampler (clipping), or discards samples with no signal (dynamic sampling).

GRPO, exactly

Group Relative Policy Optimization 组相对策略优化 was introduced in DeepSeekMath to remove PPO's critic [Shao et al. 2024, §4.1]. The critic is a second model of the policy's size (a 7B policy under mixed-precision Adam holds about 16 bytes per parameter, so a 7B critic adds about \(7\times10^{9}\times16=112\) GB of training state, see page 15), and it must predict a completion's outcome reward from a partial response. R1 argues this is especially hard for long chains of thought, where "the content initially generated may later be revised or contradicted" [DeepSeek-R1, App. A.3]. GRPO instead samples \(G\) responses per prompt and uses the group's average reward as the baseline:

\[ \hat A_{i,t} \;=\; \hat A_i \;=\; \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(r_1,\dots,r_G)}{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\dots,r_G)} \qquad\text{for every token } t \text{ of } o_i \]

and maximizes the following (DeepSeekMath Eq. 3 with the KL estimator of Eq. 4; we write \(\rho\) for the probability ratio because \(r\) is the reward):

\[ \mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\E_{q,\ \{o_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac1{|o_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}\Big\{\min\!\big(\rho_{i,t}\,\hat A_{i},\ \clip(\rho_{i,t},\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,\hat A_{i}\big)\;-\;\beta\,\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}\big[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}\big]_{i,t}\Big\}\right] \] \[ \rho_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})},\qquad \mathbb D_{\text{KL}}\big[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}\big]_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}-1 \]
GRPO as written in DeepSeekMath. The last expression is the k3 estimator: with u = log π_ref − log π_θ it equals eu − u − 1 ≥ 0

Read it in four pieces, because every successor changes exactly one:

  1. The advantage is shared by all tokens of a response. Nothing says which token deserved the credit; a correct response pushes up every one of its tokens, and statistics over many samples average the noise out. This is REINFORCE with a per-prompt baseline.
  2. Two averages and a std. \(1/|o_i|\) averages tokens within a response, \(1/G\) averages responses, and the std rescales each group. These are the three normalizers dissected in the section on fixes.
  3. The KL is in the loss, not the reward. DeepSeekMath's stated reason is "avoiding complicating the calculation of \(\hat A_{i,t}\)" [Shao et al. 2024, §4.1.1]. It sits inside the token sum, so it shares the \(1/|o_i|\) factor.
  4. The clip is against \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), not \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). It is a per-rollout trust region (page 22). DeepSeekMath makes a single update per exploration stage, so \(\rho=1\) and the clip never fires; the gradient reduces to [Shao et al. 2024, Eq. 20] \[ \E\!\left[\frac1G\sum_{i}\frac1{|o_i|}\sum_{t}\Big(\hat A_{i,t}+\beta\Big(\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}-1\Big)\Big)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})\right]. \]

A note on notation. The R1 paper writes the same objective at the sequence level, with \(\rho_i=\pi_\theta(o_i\mid q)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_i\mid q)\) and no token sum or \(1/|o_i|\) [DeepSeek-R1, Eq. 1], while DeepSeek-V3.2 writes the token form but with \(\hat A=R_i-\operatorname{mean}(R)\), no std [DeepSeek-V3.2, Eq. 5]. Which normalization the R1 run itself used is not confirmed. "GRPO" in an interview means DeepSeekMath's Eq. 3.

Why is losing token-level credit acceptable? With a final-only reward, GAE at \(\gamma=\lambda=1\) is "return minus a learned baseline", so the real difference is how the baseline is estimated. In DeepSeek's comparison on MATH with DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite (16B MoE, 2.4B active), PPO with the common \(\lambda=0.95\) was "considerably worse than GRPO", and with \(\lambda=1.0\) it came "nearing" it [DeepSeek-R1, App. A.3, Fig. 4]. R1 also argues that a KL penalty in the reward penalizes cumulative KL and "may implicitly penalize the length of the response", which conflicts with wanting longer thinking; we know of no controlled ablation isolating that effect. The critic is not dead, though: VAPO keeps a value model and repairs it with value pretraining, decoupled GAE (\(\lambda=1.0\) for the critic, a separate \(\lambda\) for the policy) and a length-adaptive \(\lambda_{\text{policy}}=1-1/(\alpha l)\) with \(\alpha=0.05\). On Qwen2.5-32B it reports 60.4 on AIME 2024 (avg@32) against 50 for DAPO, while vanilla PPO collapses to 5, and argues that value-based methods have "a higher performance ceiling" if the value model can be trained [VAPO, §1, Table 1]. That is one lab's result; the critic-free family remains the default.

Why the group mean is a legitimate baseline. A baseline must not depend on the sample it baselines. The group mean includes \(r_i\), but only through a constant. With \(\bar r_{-i}\) the mean of the other \(G-1\) rewards (independent of \(o_i\)), \(r_i-\bar r=\frac{G-1}{G}(r_i-\bar r_{-i})\): the group-mean advantage is the leave-one-out advantage of RLOO [Ahmadian et al. 2024, §2.3] times \((G-1)/G\), so its expected gradient is unbiased up to scale. The std division is what is not a constant.

def grpo_loss(logp_new, logp_old, logp_ref, adv, mask, eps_low=0.2, eps_high=0.2, beta=0.0):
    # logp_*: (B, T) log-prob of the SAMPLED tokens; adv: (B,) one scalar per response; mask: (B, T)
    ratio = (logp_new - logp_old).exp()                                  # rho_{i,t}
    a = adv[:, None]                                                     # shared by every token of a response
    surr = torch.minimum(ratio * a, ratio.clamp(1 - eps_low, 1 + eps_high) * a)
    u = logp_ref - logp_new
    k3 = u.exp() - u - 1                                                 # >= 0; DeepSeekMath Eq. 4
    tok = (surr - beta * k3) * mask                                      # KL enters the loss, not the reward
    per_seq = tok.sum(-1) / mask.sum(-1)                                 # (1/|o_i|) sum_t
    return -per_seq.mean()                                               # (1/G) sum_i; negate to minimize

DeepSeek-R1-Zero and R1

R1-Zero: RL on a base model, with nothing else

DeepSeek-R1-Zero starts from DeepSeek-V3-Base (671B total, 37B activated parameters) and applies GRPO with no SFT, on the hypothesis that "human-defined reasoning patterns may limit model exploration" [DeepSeek-R1, §1]. The prompt only asks for reasoning inside <think> tags and the answer inside <answer> tags: "we intentionally limit our constraints to this structural format" (§2.3). The reward is rule-based accuracy plus a format reward "with the same weight" (§2.2). RL prompts are about 26K math questions (reference match, reward 1 or 0, proofs excluded), 17K algorithm problems and 8K bug fixes (hidden tests), 22K multiple-choice STEM questions and 15K logic problems (App. B.3.1). Hyperparameters are in the table above; the length cap doubled from 32,768 to 65,536 tokens at step 8.2K.

AIME 2024 pass@1 rises from 15.6% to 77.9%, and to 86.7% with self-consistency (cons@16 in the figure). These are the January 2026 (v2) numbers; the January 2025 version reported 71.0% pass@1 and 86.7% for majority voting over 64 samples, so quote the version you mean. The paper reports "a steady increase in thinking time" through training (Fig. 1b), read as the model learning "to solve reasoning tasks with more thinking time". But the paper also says both performance and length "exhibit a significant jump at the 8.2k step", where the cap doubled, so part of the curve is a budget effect.

The "aha moment": what is claimed and what is shown

As the paper states it, R1-Zero "exhibits an 'aha moment' (Table 2), characterized by a sudden increase in the use of the word 'wait' during reflections", which "marks a distinct change in reasoning patterns". The evidence (App. C.2): a list of reflective words chosen by three human experts (wait, mistake, however, but, retry, error, verify, wrong, evaluate, check) rises 5- to 7-fold, and "wait" was "nearly absent during early training, showed occasional usage between steps 4000-7000, and then exhibited significant spikes after step 8000". Table 2 shows an intermediate checkpoint writing "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here." on a nested-radical equation.

That establishes a change in the frequency of certain tokens during RL. It does not establish that RL created reflection, or that reflection causes the accuracy gain:

  • Dr. GRPO hosted DeepSeek-V3-Base and found that under the R1 template it "generates a decent amount of self-reflections", including "Aha" and "wait", before any RL [Liu et al. 2025, §2.3]. Within R1-Zero, in nearly half the cases responses with self-reflection were not more accurate than those without, although reflection may still help exploration during training, which they did not test (App. F).
  • MiniMax observed the mechanism from the other side: reflective tokens (However, Recheck, Wait, Aha) are rare and low-probability under the base model, so PPO-style clipping removes them after the first update [MiniMax-M1, §3.1]. This motivates CISPO below.

A defensible position: RL selects and amplifies latent behaviors, some of which look like reflection; the token statistics are real; the causal story is open.

From R1-Zero to R1: four stages

R1-Zero "faces challenges such as poor readability and language mixing" and its RL was "narrowly focused on reasoning tasks" (§1). R1 fixes both with four stages (§3, Fig. 2):

  1. Cold-start 冷启动 SFT of V3-Base on "thousands" of long chains of thought in a conversational, first-person style. The data come from R1-Zero outputs filtered for correctness and readability, rewritten by DeepSeek-V3 (including "Translate the thinking process to the same language as the question") and checked by human annotators. The stated motivation is "primarily product-driven" (App. B.3.2).
  2. Reasoning RL, the R1-Zero recipe plus a language-consistency reward: the fraction of target-language words in the chain of thought, added to the reward. The ablation shows "a slight degradation" and better readability, and they keep it (§3.2.1, App. B.6).
  3. Rejection sampling 拒绝采样 and SFT. Sample from the stage-2 checkpoint, keep correct responses, filter mixed-language, long-paragraph and code-block chains (prompts without a rule-based check are judged by DeepSeek-V3 against the reference). Result: about 600K reasoning plus about 200K non-reasoning samples, which fine-tune DeepSeek-V3-Base again for 2 to 3 epochs (App. B.3.3, B.4.2). The RL checkpoint is only a data generator.
  4. RL for all scenarios. Rule-based rewards for reasoning prompts, reward models for general prompts (helpfulness on the final summary, harmlessness on the whole response), plus the language reward. Temperature falls to 0.7, and of 1,700 steps the preference data are used only in the last 400 (§3.2.2).

Why did R1 need a cold start? Not for reasoning strength: R1-Zero has AIME 77.9 with no SFT, and the cold-start checkpoint is worse (59.0), which the authors attribute to "the limited size of the cold-start dataset". It buys readable, instruction-following, language-consistent behavior (IF-Eval 46.6 to 71.7, Arena-Hard 53.6 to 77.0) and a better-behaved starting policy for stage 2; v1 also frames it as avoiding "the early unstable cold start phase of RL training from the base model". The final stage barely moves AIME (78.1 to 79.8) and mostly lifts preference benchmarks (Arena-Hard 75.6 to 92.3). Yet R1 is not just R1-Zero made readable: on math and GPQA the two are close, but on code the later stages matter (Codeforces rating 1444 to 2029, LiveCodeBench 50.0 to 65.9).

What it cost. R1-Zero trained on 512 H800 GPUs for about 198 hours: \(512\times198=101{,}376\approx101\)K GPU-hours. R1 took about 80 hours (\(512\times80\approx41\)K GPU-hours) and the SFT data 5K, for 147K in total [DeepSeek-R1, App. B.4.4, Table 7], which at the paper's assumed $2 per GPU-hour is about $294K. DeepSeek-V3's pre-training used 2.664M H800 GPU-hours [DeepSeek-V3, §1], so R1's RL was \(142\text{K}/2664\text{K}\approx5.3\%\) of it. DeepSeek-V3.2 later reports an RL budget above 10% (see test-time scaling).

What did not work: PRMs and MCTS

R1 reports two failed directions, "but this does not imply that these approaches are incapable of developing effective reasoning models" [DeepSeek-R1, App. G.2]. Process reward models: (1) fine-grained steps are hard to define in general reasoning; (2) judging intermediate correctness is itself hard, since automatic annotation is unreliable and manual annotation does not scale; (3) a model-based PRM invites reward hacking and needs retraining. A PRM is useful for reranking or guided search, but its benefit is limited against its overhead in large-scale RL. MCTS with a value model: (1) token generation has an exponentially larger search space than chess, and capping node expansion risks local optima; (2) the value model steers every search step and a fine-grained one is hard to train, so the self-improvement loop stalls. This is the same obstacle as GRPO's critic problem, a token-level value function for open-ended text, appearing in three places. Page 23 covers PRMs versus outcome reward models.

Fixes and successors

Each method below changes one piece of the GRPO objective: (1) how advantages are normalized (the std), (2) how the loss is averaged over tokens and responses (\(1/|o_i|\), \(1/G\)), (3) the unit and shape of the trust region (token ratio, clip bounds), (4) the KL term, (5) which samples are used. A summary table closes the section.

The two biases in GRPO's normalization (Dr. GRPO)

In the on-policy limit, token \(t\) of response \(i\) receives weight \(w_{i}=\frac1G\cdot\frac{\hat A_i}{|o_i|}\) times \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\). Two normalizers hide in that weight, and each is a bias [Liu et al. 2025, §3.1]:

  • Length bias, from \(1/|o_i|\). A correct response (\(\hat A_i\gt0\)) gets a larger per-token push when it is shorter, so the policy learns brevity when right. A wrong response (\(\hat A_i\lt0\)) gets a smaller per-token penalty when it is longer, so verbosity is cheap when wrong. Net effect: wrong answers drift longer without reasoning benefit. The authors add that popular open-source PPO implementations (TRL, OpenRLHF, verl, SimpleRL-Zero, Open-Reasoner-Zero) length-normalize the loss too (their Table 2, as of March 2025).
  • Difficulty bias, from the std. Prompts with a small reward spread (almost all right or almost all wrong) are divided by a small number and so weigh more. Normalizing advantages is common in RL, but usually over the whole batch, not per question.

DAPO: four changes that make long-CoT RL work

ByteDance Seed found that naive GRPO on Qwen2.5-32B reached only 30 on AIME 2024 (DeepSeek's R1-Zero-Qwen-32B: 47), with "entropy collapse, reward noise, and training instability" [DAPO, §1]. DAPO (Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization) writes:

\[ \mathcal J_{\text{DAPO}}(\theta)=\E\!\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{G}|o_i|}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}\min\!\big(\rho_{i,t}\hat A_{i,t},\ \clip(\rho_{i,t},\,1-\varepsilon_{\text{low}},\,1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}})\hat A_{i,t}\big)\right]\quad\text{s.t. }\ 0\lt\big|\{o_i:\ o_i\ \text{correct}\}\big|\lt G \]
DAPO, Eq. 8: token-level normalization, decoupled clip, no KL term, only mixed-outcome groups. The advantage keeps the group std.
  1. Clip-higher (against entropy collapse). The upper clip bounds how far one update can raise a token's probability, and that bound is far tighter for rare tokens than likely ones: with \(\varepsilon=0.2\), a token at \(\pi_{\text{old}}=0.01\) can rise to at most 0.012, while one at 0.9 could rise to 1.08, that is, to certainty. The authors measured the mean probability of up-clipped tokens below 0.2. They raise \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}\) to 0.28 and keep \(\varepsilon_{\text{low}}=0.2\), "because increasing it will suppress the probability of these tokens to 0, resulting in the collapse of the sampling space" (§3.1). See Figure 3.
  2. Dynamic sampling (against zero-advantage groups). A group with all \(G\) responses correct (or all wrong) has zero advantage everywhere and no gradient, yet occupies batch slots; the share of accuracy-1 prompts keeps rising (their Fig. 3b), shrinking the effective batch. Over-sample, drop groups with accuracy 0 or 1, and keep sampling until the batch is full. It reached the same accuracy in fewer steps despite the extra sampling (Fig. 6).
  3. Token-level loss: the \(1/\sum_i|o_i|\) normalization of the table above. A small accuracy gain but healthier entropy and length dynamics (Fig. 4).
  4. Overlong reward shaping (against truncation noise). Truncated responses normally get a punitive reward even if the reasoning was sound. First mask the loss of truncated samples ("overlong filtering"), then add a soft length penalty to the correctness reward: \[ R_{\text{length}}(y)=\begin{cases}0,& |y|\le L_{\max}-L_{\text{cache}}\\[2pt] \dfrac{(L_{\max}-L_{\text{cache}})-|y|}{L_{\text{cache}}},& L_{\max}-L_{\text{cache}}\lt|y|\le L_{\max}\\[6pt] -1,& L_{\max}\lt|y|\end{cases} \] with \(L_{\max}=16{,}384\) and \(L_{\text{cache}}=4{,}096\) (20,480 tokens in total). A 12,288-token response pays 0, a 14,336-token one pays \((12{,}288-14{,}336)/4096=-0.5\), a 16,384-token one pays \(-1\).

DAPO also drops the KL ("the model distribution can diverge significantly from the initial model", so the restriction "is not necessary") and uses a plain \(\pm1\) rule reward on DAPO-Math-17K, 17K prompts whose answers were rewritten as integers (§2.3, §2.4, §3.5).

GSPO: make the importance ratio match the reward

Qwen's Group Sequence Policy Optimization starts from a statistical objection. Importance sampling 重要性采样 corrects a distribution mismatch by averaging over many samples, but GRPO applies \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\) at each token position using one sampled token, so it cannot do the correction and instead "introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients, which accumulates over long sequences and is exacerbated by the clipping mechanism" [Zheng et al. 2025, §3]. Their principle: "the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward". The reward belongs to the whole sequence, so ratio and clip should too:

\[ s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}=\exp\!\Big(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\rho_{i,t}\Big),\qquad \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}=\E\!\left[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\!\big(s_i\hat A_i,\ \clip(s_i,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat A_i\big)\right] \]
GSPO: the ratio is the geometric mean of the token ratios; clipping removes whole responses from the gradient

The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) keeps \(s_i\) in one numerical range across lengths. Because \(s_i\) is a geometric mean, its natural clip range is tiny: \(3\times10^{-4}\) (left) and \(4\times10^{-4}\) (right), against 0.2 and 0.27 for their GRPO baseline. GSPO clips about two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO (average clipped fraction 0.15 versus 0.0013, their Fig. 2) and is still more sample-efficient, which they read as evidence that token-level gradients are noisy. The gradients differ too: GSPO weights every token of a response equally by \(s_i\hat A_i/|y_i|\), while GRPO weights each token by its own \(\rho_{i,t}\), ranging in \((0,1+\varepsilon]\) for \(\hat A\gt0\) and \([1-\varepsilon,\infty)\) for \(\hat A\lt0\) (§4.2).

The MoE motivation. A gradient step changes which experts fire. For the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, "roughly 10% of the experts activated under the new policy \(\pi_\theta\) are different from those under the old policy" for the same response after each update, so token ratios fluctuate wildly. Qwen's earlier fix was Routing Replay: cache the experts activated under \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) and replay them under \(\pi_\theta\), costing memory and communication and capping model capacity. GSPO needs no replay because "the MoE model always maintains its language modeling capability", so the sequence likelihood stays stable even when individual routes change (§5.3). DeepSeek chose the other route: Keep Routing in V3.2 (below) is essentially Routing Replay, while GSPO changes the objective (routing itself: page 08). A last claim: sequence likelihoods tolerate the numerical mismatch between training and inference engines, so the inference engine's log-probabilities can be used without recomputation (§5.4), which matters for partial rollouts and multi-turn RL (page 25). Later, the same team proposed SAPO, which replaces hard clipping with a smooth gate \(f(\rho)=\tfrac4\tau\sigma(\tau(\rho-1))\) with a larger temperature for negative-advantage tokens (\(\tau_{\text{neg}}=1.05\gt\tau_{\text{pos}}=1.0\)) [Gao et al. 2025].

CISPO: clip the weight, not the update

MiniMax-M1 hit a different failure. In zero-RL runs GRPO "failed to effectively promote the emergence of long CoT reasoning behaviors". Ablations traced it to clipping: tokens that start reflection (However, Recheck, Wait, Aha), which "often serve as 'forks' in reasoning paths", are rare and low-probability in the base model, so their ratio is high after one update and they are clipped out of all later off-policy updates. With 16 off-policy update rounds per generation batch, DAPO's larger \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}\) was "less effective in our setup" [MiniMax-M1, §3.1]. CISPO starts from REINFORCE with a stop-gradient importance weight and clips that weight:

\[ \mathcal J_{\text{CISPO}}(\theta)=\E\!\left[\frac1{\sum_i|o_i|}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}\sg\big(\hat\rho_{i,t}\big)\,\hat A_{i,t}\,\log\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})\right],\qquad \hat\rho_{i,t}=\clip\big(\rho_{i,t},\,1-\varepsilon^{IS}_{\text{low}},\,1+\varepsilon^{IS}_{\text{high}}\big) \]
CISPO: the clipped importance weight is a constant multiplying a plain policy-gradient term, so every token keeps a gradient

Without clipping this is the standard policy gradient. In practice the lower bound was removed and only \(\varepsilon^{IS}_{\text{high}}\) tuned. The gradient is slightly biased but "preserves gradient contributions from all tokens, especially in long responses". CISPO also uses the token-level loss, group advantages, dynamic sampling and a length penalty from DAPO, and no KL. On Qwen2.5-32B zero-RL with DAPO's data it matched DAPO's AIME 2024 score with 50% of the steps (a "2x speedup"); MiniMax-M1's whole RL run took three weeks on 512 H800 GPUs, about $0.53M (§1). RLOO, REINFORCE++ and Kimi: baselines without a group std

RLOO baselines sample \(i\) with the mean reward of the other \(k-1\) samples: \(\frac1k\sum_i\big[R(y_i)-\frac1{k-1}\sum_{j\ne i}R(y_j)\big]\nabla\log\pi(y_i\mid x)\). It is unbiased, needs no critic or std, and was proposed for RLHF, where plain REINFORCE also beat PPO by 3.2% to 20.3% in win-rate [Ahmadian et al. 2024, §1, §2.3]. REINFORCE++ normalizes across the global batch: its \(k=1\) form puts a token-level k1 KL penalty in the reward and normalizes advantages over the batch; its "w/ Baseline" form subtracts the group mean, divides by the batch std, and adds a separate k2 KL loss [Hu et al., v9, §3]. Its claims about local std are the authors' own, and the paper has nine versions, so cite the one you mean. Kimi k1.5 uses online policy mirror descent with the group's mean reward as baseline, an \(\ell_2\)-style regularizer to the current policy, and no value network. Its case against a critic is worth quoting: if the model can continue from a partial chain with a correct step or with a wrong one it later recovers from, an oracle value function would penalize the wrong step, yet "exploring \(z'_{t+1}\) is extremely valuable for training the model to generate long CoT" [Kimi k1.5, §2.3.2].

What DeepSeek changed when it scaled GRPO (V3.2)

DeepSeek-V3.2 still uses GRPO and lists its stabilizers [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3.1]:

  • Advantage without the std: \(\hat A_{i,t}=R_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{R})\).
  • Unbiased KL estimate. Samples come from \(\pi_{\text{old}}\), so the k3 term is multiplied by \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\). Otherwise, "when the sampled tokens have substantially lower probabilities under the current policy than the reference policy", the k3 gradient gives "disproportionately large, unbounded weights" to raising them. For math, "a relatively weak KL penalty or even omitting it entirely can yield improved performance".
  • Off-policy sequence masking. Drop sequences with negative advantage whose mean log-ratio \(\frac1{|o_i|}\sum_t\log\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}}{\pi_\theta}\) exceeds a threshold \(\delta\): "the model benefits the most by learning from its own mistakes, whereas highly off-policy negative samples can be detrimental". \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) is the inference engine's probability, so the measured divergence includes train-inference mismatch.
  • Keep Routing (MoE): force the expert routes used at sampling time in training, "crucial for RL training stability of MoE models", used since DeepSeek-V3-0324. Keep Sampling Mask: apply the top-p and top-k truncation of \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) to \(\pi_\theta\) too, which "effectively preserves language consistency".
  • Rewards: rule-based outcome reward, a length penalty and a language-consistency reward for reasoning and agent tasks; a generative reward model with per-prompt rubrics for general tasks.
Which KL does the k3 term actually regularize?

k3 is unbiased for the value of \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\), but the loss is differentiated with the samples held fixed. Then \(\nabla_\theta\text{k3}=-\big(\tfrac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}-1\big)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\), whose on-policy expectation is \(-\sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y)=\nabla_\theta\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{ref}}\|\pi_\theta)\): the gradient of \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{ref}}\|\pi_\theta)\), not of \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\), which is the quantity the estimate is unbiased for. Forward and reverse naming varies between papers, so always write the arguments. Multiplying by \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\) and differentiating through the ratio, as V3.2 does, restores the gradient of \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\), and so does the k2 estimator \(\tfrac12(\log\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})^2\) that REINFORCE++ uses. We checked all three numerically on a six-way categorical distribution (our derivation, not a claim from those papers). Page 22 derives the same identities (with a three-token check) and covers the estimator family; Tang and Munos analyze the pitfall in general [Tang and Munos 2025].

Training dynamics: entropy, length and what carries signal

Entropy collapse and its covariance mechanism

Let \(H\) be the policy's mean per-token entropy on its own samples. In RLVR runs without intervention \(H\) falls fast and the policy becomes nearly deterministic: the \(G\) samples of a prompt become near-copies, groups become uniform (zero advantage), and reward plateaus. DAPO saw this with naive GRPO (their Fig. 2b), and Cui et al. made it quantitative: without entropy intervention, performance follows \(R=-a\,e^{H}+b\) across runs, so the ceiling at exhausted entropy (\(H=0\), \(R=-a+b\)) is predictable, and over 95% of the entropy drop and performance gain occur early [Cui et al. 2025, Fig. 1] (entropy collapse 熵坍缩). Why does entropy fall? To first order, for a softmax policy with logits \(z\):

\[ H(\pi^{k+1})-H(\pi^{k})\;\approx\;-\operatorname{Cov}_{a\sim\pi^{k}(\cdot\mid s)}\!\Big(\log\pi^{k}(a\mid s),\ z^{k+1}_{s,a}-z^{k}_{s,a}\Big),\qquad \Delta z=\eta\,\pi A\ (\text{PG})\ \text{or}\ \eta A\ (\text{NPG}) \] \[ \Rightarrow\quad \Delta H\approx-\eta\operatorname{Cov}\big(\log\pi,\ \pi A\big)\ \ (\text{PG}),\qquad \Delta H\approx-\eta\operatorname{Cov}\big(\log\pi,\ A\big)\ \ (\text{NPG}) \]
Entropy dynamics (Cui et al., Lemma 1, Proposition 1, Theorems 1 and 2; tabular softmax policy)

An already-likely action with positive advantage lowers entropy (the update sharpens the distribution); a rare action with positive advantage raises it. Cui et al. verify on Qwen2.5-7B that the covariance term tracks the measured entropy change and stays mostly positive, which explains the monotone decline: after pretraining, confidence is roughly calibrated, so high-probability tokens tend to be the good ones. RLVR is a machine for the first row: correct chains are mostly the likely ones. The remedies:

  • Clip-higher (DAPO) and CISPO let rare positive tokens keep growing: the second row.
  • Clip-Cov and KL-Cov (Cui et al.) act on the cause: take the small fraction of tokens with the largest covariance and either remove them from the gradient (Clip-Cov, clip ratio \(2\times10^{-4}\)) or KL-penalize them (KL-Cov, top-\(k\) fraction \(2\times10^{-3}\) for 7B and \(2\times10^{-4}\) for 32B, \(\beta=1\)). On Qwen2.5 with DAPO-Math data they report +2.0% (7B) and +6.4% (32B) average over GRPO and more than 10 times higher entropy than the plateaued baseline. They also report that clip-higher raises entropy early but later destabilizes, and that a naive entropy bonus is ineffective (§4).
  • Temperature and a KL leash. ProRL finds a higher rollout temperature "only delays the onset of entropy collapse", while a KL penalty with periodic hard resets of the reference (and optimizer) sustains entropy beyond 2,000 steps from an already-capable start [ProRL, §2]. Qwen3 controls entropy "to increase steadily or remain stable" [Qwen3, §4.2]. Too high is also bad (DAPO): monitor it against validation accuracy.

Response length: growth, overthinking and length control

Length growth is the most-watched curve and the most misread. Four things push it up, only the first being "reasoning": (1) longer chains genuinely help on hard problems; (2) the \(1/|o_i|\) bias lets wrong answers grow for free; (3) a raised length cap (R1-Zero at step 8.2K); (4) unproductive loops. DAPO's advice is to read length only together with validation accuracy, since it "does not always maintain a continuous upward trend" [DAPO, §4.3].

Cost and overthinking 过度思考 follow. Kimi k1.5 saw length "significantly increases during RL training", helping accuracy but "often not preferred by humans", and added a length reward within each group: with \(\lambda=0.5-\frac{\text{len}(i)-\text{min\_len}}{\text{max\_len}-\text{min\_len}}\), the reward is \(\lambda\) for a correct response, \(\min(0,\lambda)\) for a wrong one, and 0 for all if lengths are equal [Kimi k1.5, §2.3.3]. For correct answers of 1,000, 2,000 and 4,000 tokens it pays \(+0.5\), \(+0.167\), \(-0.5\); a wrong 4,000-token answer pays \(-0.5\), a wrong 1,000-token one 0. Short-and-correct wins and short-and-wrong is never rewarded. Because it slows early learning, they train without it first and then hold it constant. DeepSeek treats length as a dial: V3.2 uses a length penalty, and its reported scores are "constrained by a length constraint reward model; upon removal of the restriction, we observe further improvement", while the experimental V3.2-Speciale used a reduced penalty [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3, §4.1]. V4 trains one specialist per reasoning-effort mode with "distinct length penalties and context windows" (see below). R1 itself lists overthinking on simple questions as a limitation [DeepSeek-R1, §6].

Does RL add capability? An open debate

The question decides whether extra compute goes into pretraining, RL or distillation. The literature has not converged (as of September 2026 the debate is still active), so the evidence is laid out on both sides.

The sharpening view

The first hint is in DeepSeekMath: after GRPO, Maj@K improved but Pass@K did not, which the authors read as RL "rendering the output distribution more robust", boosting the correct response from the top-K rather than improving fundamental capability [Shao et al. 2024, §5.2.2]. Yue et al. made it the central experiment [Yue et al. 2025]: compare base and RL models by pass@k for large \(k\) (up to 256 or more) on math, code and visual tasks. The RL models are zero-RL GRPO checkpoints from SimpleRL-Zoo (Qwen2.5-7B, 14B, 32B and Llama-3.1-8B, trained on GSM8K and MATH with correctness-only reward), plus Oat-Zero-7B and DAPO-32B. Findings:

  • RL wins at \(k=1\); as \(k\) grows the curves cross and the base overtakes (on Minerva with the 32B model, the base is about 9 points ahead at \(k=128\)). Oat-Zero and DAPO start about 30 points above their bases at \(k=1\) and are eventually passed.
  • Coverage and perplexity analyses suggest RL models' reasoning paths already lie inside the base model's sampling distribution: RL mostly re-weights them.
  • Six algorithms (PPO, GRPO, REINFORCE++, RLOO, ReMax, DAPO) behave alike. The gap between the base's pass@256 and the RL model's pass@1 stays above 40 points on their in-domain set (43.9 for GRPO, 42.6 for RLOO).
  • Distillation differs: R1-Distill-Qwen-7B's pass@k curve sits consistently above its base's.

A lab-side data point agrees. On Qwen3-8B after off-policy distillation, further RL raised AIME'24 pass@1 from 55.0 to 67.6 and AIME'25 from 42.8 to 55.5 but left pass@64 unchanged (90.0 and 83.3), while on-policy distillation from a stronger teacher reached 74.4 and 65.5 with pass@64 of 93.3 and 86.7, at 1,800 GPU-hours instead of 17,920 [Qwen3, §4.7, Table 21]. AIME has 30 problems, so one problem is 3.3 points: suggestive, not conclusive. A September 2026 study (Qwen3-8B teacher, Qwen3-1.7B-Base student) finds OPD followed by RL beats pure OPD, pure RL and joint schemes, explaining that OPD expands coverage of teacher-supported solutions and RL sharpens within that support [Li et al. 2026].

Counter-evidence: prolonged RL

ProRL argues the negative results reflect method limits: they concentrate on math, which is heavily trained already, and stop RL after "no more than hundreds of steps" [Liu et al. 2025, §1]. Their recipe trains DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (a distilled checkpoint, not a raw base) for over 2,000 steps on 136K verifiable problems in five domains, with decoupled clipping (\(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}=0.4\)), dynamic sampling, temperature 1.2, a KL penalty and periodic reference resets, about 16K GPU-hours. They report large gains in every domain (for example +54.8% on Reasoning Gym logic puzzles), success where the base fails at every \(k\), and a higher Creativity Index (less overlap with the pretraining corpus). The pass@128 gain correlates negatively with the base's own pass@128: "the weaker the start, the stronger the gain". Benchmarks fall in three regimes: diminish (breadth narrows, mostly math), plateau, and sustained gains (some code and puzzle tasks). So ProRL's own data contain the Yue et al. effect on math.

Spurious rewards: what RLVR on Qwen actually optimizes

A third strand asks how much the reward matters. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, GRPO for about 300 steps raised MATH-500 by +29.1 points with ground-truth rewards, +24.1 with a reward for matching majority-vote answers that are incorrect, and +21.4 with rewards drawn at random (probability 0.5) [Shao et al. 2025, §2]. On AIME 2025, whose problems postdate the models, the spurious gains largely vanish, and Llama-3 and OLMo-2 gain little or nothing. Their mechanism is a clipping bias: with random rewards the expected gradient is zero without clipping, but with clipping the expected update raises the probability of tokens the model already favors, amplifying high-prior behaviors. In Qwen2.5-Math one is "code reasoning" (Python-style reasoning without execution), whose frequency rises from 65% to over 90% under any of the rewards and correlates with accuracy (answers using it are correct 60.9% of the time). The rewards are analysis tools, not a recipe; the recommendation is to validate on several model families and to use spurious rewards as a baseline. Dr. GRPO agrees in spirit: Qwen2.5 models answer best with no chat template, and the authors hypothesize Qwen2.5-Math was pretrained on concatenated question-answer text [Liu et al. 2025, §2.2].

How to hold the debate in an interview

What everyone accepts. At typical open-source scale RLVR raises pass@1 chiefly by making reachable solutions more probable. That matters: users see pass@1, and majority voting or best-of-\(k\) costs \(k\) times more at inference, so RLVR distills the group's best behavior into one sample. R1 shows the gap it closes: AIME 2024 pass@1 79.8, majority vote over 64 samples 86.7, pass@64 90.0 [DeepSeek-R1, App. E.4].

What is contested is whether long, diverse RL creates paths with negligible base probability, and whether pass@\(k\) can even see that. A solution the base produces with probability \(q\) per sample is found by pass@256 with probability \(1-(1-q)^{256}\): 92% for \(q=10^{-2}\), 2.5% for \(q=10^{-4}\), 0.026% for \(q=10^{-6}\). Problems whose only successful chains have \(q\lesssim10^{-4}\) look "unsolvable" at \(k=256\), yet RL can raise such a \(q\) by orders of magnitude, a real gain that a \(k\le256\) test cannot tell from zero. Conversely, on numeric-answer tasks some large-\(k\) "successes" are lucky guesses, inflating the base curve. Add pretraining confounds (Qwen's data), short, narrow RL runs, and contamination. What would settle it: tasks with verifiably novel skills, RL far past hundreds of steps on broad data, and per-problem probability estimates beyond \(k=256\). Until then the defensible answer is "both": RL sharpens reliably, expands under some conditions, and distillation expands more cheaply when a teacher exists.

Test-time scaling, thinking modes and where post-training is going

Two ways to spend inference compute

Test-time scaling 测试时扩展 buys accuracy with inference tokens, along two axes. Sequential scaling lengthens one chain of thought (more exploration, verification, backtracking). Parallel scaling draws independent responses and aggregates them by majority vote or a verifier (page 06 for sampling, page 18 for pass@k). They complement each other. R1 notes that non-reasoning models "lack the ability to backtrack or self-correct", so more samples from them just re-draw potentially wrong answers: majority voting over 64 samples lifts GPT-4o on AIME 2024 only from 9.3% to 13.4%. Its own long chains "still sometimes fail to be thorough or become trapped in incorrect logic paths", so sampling several helps: R1 has pass@1 79.8, majority vote over 64 86.7, pass@64 90.0 [DeepSeek-R1, App. E.4]. The same appendix shows R1 spending fewer than 7,000 thinking tokens on easy competition problems and more than 18,000 on the hardest, so sequential compute already adapts to difficulty.

Budget forcing (s1). Muennighoff et al. fine-tune Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on only 1,000 curated reasoning traces (distilled from Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking; 26 minutes on 16 H100 GPUs). At decode time, to stop early they append the end-of-thinking delimiter; to think longer they suppress it and append "Wait", which often makes the model re-check and fix an error. On AIME 2024 s1-32B scores 50.0 without forcing and 56.7 with it (MATH-500 92.6 to 93.0, GPQA Diamond 56.6 to 59.6). Gains flatten after about six forced continuations, more "Wait"s cause repetitive loops, and majority voting on the base model could not catch up with sequential scaling [Muennighoff et al. 2025, §3, §4.2, Table 1]. The lesson for RL: the format of long reasoning and its length control can be elicited with a tiny SFT set; RL is what teaches a model to use extra tokens productively without an injected "Wait".

Thinking budgets and hybrid modes

Products need a fast answer and a deep one from the same weights, with a cost dial. Three documented designs:

  • Qwen3: fusion by SFT. After reasoning RL, continue SFT on thinking data (rejection-sampled from the reasoning-RL model on the cold-start queries) mixed with non-thinking data, with /think and /no_think flags and an empty think block for non-thinking samples. A thinking budget then comes free: at a user-set length, insert "Considering the limited time by the user, I have to give the solution based on the thinking directly now.\n</think>" and let the model answer. The authors say this "is not explicitly trained but emerges naturally", and show smooth accuracy growth with budget up to 32K tokens for Qwen3-235B-A22B [Qwen3, §4.3, §4.7].
  • DeepSeek-V3.1: one model, two chat templates: "One model supports both thinking mode and non-thinking mode by changing the chat template" [DeepSeek-V3.1 model card], with thinking-mode quality comparable to R1-0528 at lower latency, per the card.
  • DeepSeek-V4: effort baked into RL. Modes Non-think, Think High and Think Max. "For each mode, we apply distinct length penalties and context windows during RL training", Think Max also prepends a system-prompt instruction, and evaluation windows are 8K, 128K and 384K tokens [DeepSeek-V4, §5.1.1, §5.3.1]. See page 30 and page 28.

Why this works: training on both modes teaches the model to continue from partial reasoning, so truncating or extending a chain stays in-distribution; V4 goes further and shapes the length distribution itself. The costs are a more complex pipeline and truncated thinking that can be worse than a model trained for the shorter budget.

RL compute as a scaling axis

DeepSeek-V3.2 states that its RL budget "exceeds 10% of the pre-training cost" and that it has seen "consistent performance improvements correlating with extended RL training budget" [DeepSeek-V3.2, §1, §4.1]. For scale, R1's RL was about 5.3% of V3's pretraining GPU-hours (computed above), and MiniMax-M1's run was three weeks on 512 H800 GPUs: \(512\times24\times21=258{,}048\) GPU-hours, which at their $0.53M implies about $2.05 per GPU-hour, consistent with R1's $2 assumption. RL compute is spent on more steps (ProRL: over 2,000), longer sequences (Kimi k1.5 scaled the RL context to 128K; MiniMax staged 40K to 80K), more prompts and domains, and more samples per prompt. Rollouts, not gradient steps, tend to dominate wall-clock because long-tail samples stall synchronous batches (DAPO §3.2, MiniMax §3.2); page 25 covers the systems side.

Post-training trends as of September 2026

  • Specialists, then on-policy distillation instead of mixed RL. DeepSeek-V3.2 trained per-domain specialists with large-scale RL, distilled their data into the main model, then ran one mixed RL stage over reasoning, agent and alignment data. DeepSeek-V4 keeps the specialists (SFT then GRPO) but replaces the mixed RL stage entirely with multi-teacher on-policy distillation: the unified student samples its own responses and minimizes \(\sum_i w_i\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{E_i})\) against more than ten experts, to avoid "the performance degradation often encountered in traditional weight-merging or mixed RL techniques". It uses full-vocabulary reverse KL rather than the common shortcut of \(\operatorname{sg}[\log\pi_E-\log\pi_\theta]\) at the sampled token as a per-token advantage, which has high gradient variance [DeepSeek-V4, §5.1].
  • OPD is RL with a dense reward. As a policy gradient it uses the per-token advantage \(\log\pi_{\text{teacher}}-\log\pi_\theta\) instead of one outcome reward per response, which is why it plugs into RL infrastructure and is cheaper per unit of signal (Qwen3: 1,800 versus 17,920 GPU-hours for the 8B model; Qwen3 already used it for its small models). Order matters: OPD then RL beat fusing the signals in one step in the September 2026 study above [Li et al. 2026].
  • GRPO survives, with patches: V3.2's list, DAPO-style shaping, GSPO-style stability for MoE, not a replacement algorithm.
  • Generative reward models for what rules cannot check (V4: rubric-guided data, the actor as its own judge), and reasoning persisting across tool calls (V3.2 keeps thinking across tool results; V4 keeps it across user turns when tools are in play; page 25).

Common misconceptions

  • "GRPO has no baseline." It has one: the mean reward of the group. Algebraically the group-mean advantage equals the leave-one-out (RLOO) advantage times \((G-1)/G\). What GRPO lacks is a learned per-token value function, and with it any token-level credit assignment.
  • "The KL term goes into the reward, as in RLHF." In GRPO it is subtracted in the loss, inside the token sum, as the k3 estimator. Also remember which KL it regularizes: differentiated on-policy with samples held fixed, k3's expected gradient is that of \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{ref}}\|\pi_\theta)\), the other direction, unless corrected by the importance ratio as in DeepSeek-V3.2.
  • "The advantage says which tokens were good." Every token of a response shares one scalar. Nothing in the objective distinguishes the decisive token from filler; the model learns which is which only because the statistics differ across many samples.
  • "Clipping keeps the policy close to the reference." The clip acts relative to \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) within one rollout. Closeness to the reference is the KL term's job. With one update per rollout, as in DeepSeekMath, \(\rho=1\) and the clip never fires.
  • "Zero-advantage groups are harmless." They add no gradient but occupy batch slots and, under token-mean normalization, still enter the denominator. Their share grows as the policy improves (for \(p=0.9\), \(G=8\): 43% of groups), which is why DAPO filters them.
  • "A growing response length means better reasoning." Length also grows from the \(1/|o_i|\) bias (wrong answers get cheap verbosity), from a raised length cap (R1-Zero's jump at step 8.2K), and from loops. Only length together with validation accuracy is informative.
  • "The aha moment proves RL invents reflection." The frequency change of "wait" is measured, but base models, including DeepSeek-V3-Base, already produce "wait" and "aha", and in R1-Zero reflective responses were often no more accurate. RL amplifies; the causal story is open.
  • "Higher pass@1 means the model learned to solve new problems." Usually it means it solves the same problems more reliably. Check pass@\(k\) at large \(k\), and remember that random rewards lift Qwen2.5-Math-7B's MATH-500 accuracy by 21 points.

Interview questions

Warm-up What is RLVR, and how does it differ from RLHF?

RLHF optimizes a learned reward model trained on human preference pairs. RLVR (named in Tülu 3) replaces it with a program: extract and normalize the final answer and compare it with a reference, run unit tests, check a format constraint. The reward is usually binary. Consequences: no reward model to train or exploit (R1 avoids neural reward models on reasoning tasks because they get hacked at scale); the reward is exact but exists only for checkable domains; it is sparse, one bit per trajectory of thousands of tokens; and many samples per prompt are the natural way to get a baseline. It is not immune to hacking: the checker is the specification, so parser false positives, weak tests and lucky guesses get optimized. Some recipes drop the KL leash because a rule-based reward cannot drift like a learned proxy and long-CoT training is expected to move far from the initial model.

Warm-up Why is there no value model in GRPO, and what replaces it?

PPO's critic is a second model the size of the policy that must predict the expected final reward token by token. With an outcome-only reward and long, self-revising chains this is hard to learn (R1: early content "may later be revised or contradicted") and costly (a 7B critic is roughly 112 GB of training state). GRPO samples \(G\) responses per prompt and uses their mean reward as the baseline: \(\hat A_i=(r_i-\text{mean})/\text{std}\), the same for every token of response \(i\). It is a valid baseline because \(r_i-\bar r=\frac{G-1}{G}(r_i-\bar r_{-i})\) and \(\bar r_{-i}\) does not depend on \(o_i\), so the gradient is unbiased up to a constant. Costs: \(G\) samples per prompt and no per-token credit. Kimi adds that a value function would penalize a wrong but recoverable step, and exploring such steps is what long-CoT training needs. The counterpoint is VAPO, a carefully engineered critic-based recipe that reports 60.4 on AIME 2024 versus 50 for DAPO, so critic-free is a practical default, not a theorem.

Warm-up Why do groups where all samples are correct, or all wrong, contribute nothing? How often does that happen?

If all rewards are equal, \(r_i-\text{mean}=0\) for every \(i\), so every advantage is 0 and the surrogate gradient vanishes (a KL term, if present, is the only gradient left). The std is 0 too, hence the \(\epsilon\) in the denominator. Dr. GRPO and RLOO share the property, so it is not caused by the std. A prompt with pass rate \(p\) gives a useless group with probability \(p^G+(1-p)^G\): for \(p=0.9\), 43% at \(G=8\), 18.5% at \(G=16\), 0.1% at \(G=64\). As the policy improves, more prompts drift to \(p\to1\) and the fraction rises (DAPO Fig. 3b). Consequences: wasted rollouts, a smaller effective batch, noisier gradients. Remedies: dynamic sampling, offline difficulty filtering, larger \(G\), and partial-credit rewards that avoid exact ties.

Core Write the GRPO objective. How does it differ from PPO?

For each prompt \(q\), sample \(\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim\pi_{\text{old}}\), compute \(\hat A_i=(r_i-\text{mean}(r))/\text{std}(r)\), and maximize \[ \E\Big[\tfrac1G\sum_i\tfrac1{|o_i|}\sum_t\Big\{\min\big(\rho_{i,t}\hat A_i,\ \clip(\rho_{i,t},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat A_i\big)-\beta\,\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}]_{i,t}\Big\}\Big],\quad \rho_{i,t}=\tfrac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})}, \] with \(\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}=\tfrac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}-\log\tfrac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}-1\) (k3). Versus PPO: (1) the baseline is the group mean, not a learned \(V(s_t)\), so no critic; (2) one advantage per response shared by all tokens, no GAE; (3) the KL is a loss term, not a per-token reward penalty; (4) \(G\) samples per prompt; (5) ratio, clip and \(1/|o|\) are inherited from PPO. In DeepSeek's comparison, PPO with \(\lambda=0.95\) was much worse than GRPO on MATH and with \(\lambda=1\) came close, so the baseline estimator is the main difference.

Core Where does the KL term go in GRPO, why the k3 estimator, and which KL does its gradient regularize?

It is subtracted per token in the loss, inside the token sum, sharing the \(1/|o_i|\) normalizer, instead of being folded into the reward (where it would enter the advantage and penalize cumulative KL). With \(u=\log\pi_{\text{ref}}-\log\pi_\theta\) at the sampled token, k3 \(=e^u-u-1\): non-negative, low-variance, and for tokens sampled from \(\pi_\theta\) its expectation is \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\). The subtlety is the gradient: differentiating with samples fixed gives \(-(\pi_{\text{ref}}/\pi_\theta-1)\nabla\log\pi_\theta\), whose expectation is the gradient of \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{ref}}\|\pi_\theta)\), the other direction from the one k3 estimates. DeepSeek-V3.2 multiplies k3 by \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\), restoring the gradient of \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\); k2 does too. In practice many reasoning recipes drop the KL (DAPO, Dr. GRPO, CISPO); R1 used \(\beta=0.001\) and replaced the reference every 400 steps; V3.2 notes a weak or absent penalty works better for math. \(\beta=0\) also removes the reference model's memory and forward pass.

Core What biases does GRPO's normalization introduce, and how do Dr. GRPO and DAPO fix them?

Two. Length bias from the per-response \(1/|o_i|\): token weight \(\hat A_i/(G|o_i|)\), so among correct answers shorter ones get bigger per-token rewards and among wrong answers longer ones get smaller per-token penalties; wrong answers drift longer for free. Difficulty bias from the group std: prompts with rare successes or rare failures are up-weighted, by about 1.5 times relative to 50/50 prompts at \(G=8\). Dr. GRPO removes both: a constant normalizer (for example the maximum generation length) replaces \(|o_i|\), and there is no std. DAPO removes the length term with token-mean over the batch but keeps the std; DeepSeek-V3.2 drops the std. Dr. GRPO reports that incorrect-response length stops inflating and reaches 43.3% AIME 2024 with a 7B model. DAPO's argument is complementary: under sequence-mean, tokens of long responses are under-weighted, so good long reasoning is learned slowly and long gibberish is penalized too little.

Core What happened in DeepSeek-R1-Zero, and why did R1 need a cold start?

R1-Zero applied GRPO directly to DeepSeek-V3-Base with a rule-based reward (accuracy plus a format reward for <think> tags), no SFT, no neural reward model. AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% (86.7% with self-consistency), response length grew, and reflective behavior ("wait", verification, alternatives) became more frequent, with the "aha moment" example. Problems: poor readability, language mixing, weak general ability (IF-Eval 46.6). R1's four stages: cold-start SFT on thousands of readable first-person long-CoT examples; reasoning RL with a language-consistency reward; rejection sampling from that model, about 600K reasoning plus 200K other samples, SFT again from V3-Base; a final RL stage with rules for reasoning and reward models for helpfulness and harmlessness. The cold start is not for reasoning strength: the cold-start checkpoint scores lower on AIME (59.0) than R1-Zero (77.9). It provides a readable, instruction-following starting policy (IF-Eval 71.7) that RL then upgrades. The aha moment shows frequency changes of reflective tokens, not that RL created reflection: base models already produce them.

Core What is entropy collapse in RLVR and how do you prevent it?

Policy entropy falls to near zero within a small fraction of training: samples per prompt become near-identical, groups become uniform (zero advantage), exploration and reward plateau. Cui et al. fit \(R=-ae^{H}+b\), so exhausting entropy sets a predictable ceiling. The cause: to first order \(\Delta H\approx-\eta\operatorname{Cov}(\log\pi(a),A(a))\) (natural-gradient case; \(\operatorname{Cov}(\log\pi,\pi A)\) for vanilla PG), so reinforcing high-probability, high-advantage actions sharpens the distribution, and RLVR does this constantly since correct chains are usually the likely ones. Prevention: (1) clip-higher (\(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}=0.28\) versus \(\varepsilon_{\text{low}}=0.2\)) or CISPO so rare positive tokens keep growing; (2) restrict high-covariance tokens (Clip-Cov, KL-Cov); (3) dynamic sampling and diverse prompts; (4) a KL to a periodically reset reference plus high temperature from an SFT start (ProRL); (5) monitor entropy against validation accuracy, since too high is also bad. A plain entropy bonus was reported ineffective.

Core Explain clip-higher with numbers, then compute the extra sampling dynamic sampling needs for prompts with pass rate 0.9 and \(G=16\).

With \(\varepsilon=0.2\) and \(A\gt0\), a token's probability can rise by at most a factor 1.2 per rollout: a rare token at \(\pi_{\text{old}}=0.01\) reaches at most 0.012, while one at 0.9 could reach 1.08, certainty. The clip limits rare tokens and barely limits likely ones (DAPO measured the mean probability of up-clipped tokens below 0.2). \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}=0.28\) lets the rare token reach 0.0128 and keeps more of them inside the trust region; \(\varepsilon_{\text{low}}\) stays 0.2, since loosening it would let negative advantages push rare tokens to probability 0. Dynamic sampling: a group is useless with probability \(0.9^{16}+0.1^{16}\approx0.185\), so 81.5% carry signal, and filling a batch of 512 informative groups takes about \(512/0.815\approx628\) prompts, 1.23 times the rollouts (real prompts vary in \(p\), so measure). At \(G=8\) the useless share is 43% and the multiplier 1.76, but a group costs half the samples: cost per informative group is \(G/(1-\text{useless})\), 14.0 samples at \(G=8\) versus 19.6 at \(G=16\).

Core What do GSPO and CISPO each change, and what problem motivated each?

GSPO (Qwen) replaces token ratios by the length-normalized sequence ratio \(s_i=(\pi_\theta(y_i)/\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i))^{1/|y_i|}\) and clips whole sequences (\(\varepsilon\approx3\text{-}4\times10^{-4}\)). Motivation: a token-level ratio is a single-sample importance weight, high-variance noise that accumulates with length; in MoE models about 10% of activated experts change after one update (Qwen3-30B-A3B), making token ratios unreliable. GRPO needed Routing Replay; GSPO does not, since sequence likelihood is stable, and it tolerates train-inference numerical mismatch. CISPO (MiniMax-M1) keeps token-level updates but clips the importance weight (stop-gradient) instead of zeroing the update: \(\sg(\clip(\rho))\hat A\log\pi_\theta\). Motivation: rare fork tokens ("Wait", "Recheck") get a high ratio after the first update and PPO clipping removes them from later off-policy updates, so long reflection is never reinforced. CISPO keeps every token's gradient at the cost of a small bias and matched DAPO's AIME score in half the steps. DeepSeek's alternative for MoE: keep the sampling-time routes and mask far-off-policy negative sequences.

Core Compute the per-token gradient weights of a 100-token and a 1,000-token response under sequence-mean, token-mean and a constant normalizer.

Take \(G=8\), four responses of 100 tokens and four of 1,000 (4,400 tokens), all with \(|\hat A|=1\). The weight on each token's \(\nabla\log\pi\): sequence-mean \(1/(G|o_i|)\): \(1/800=1.25\times10^{-3}\) for the short response, \(1/8000=1.25\times10^{-4}\) for the long one, a 10\(\times\) per-token difference with equal total weight 0.125 each. Token-mean \(1/4400=2.27\times10^{-4}\) for every token, so the long response has 10\(\times\) the total weight (0.227 versus 0.0227). Constant with \(L_{\max}=1000\): \(1/(8\cdot1000)=1.25\times10^{-4}\) for every token. Sequence-mean equalizes responses and makes short responses' tokens count more (length bias); the other two equalize tokens, and only the constant is independent of batch composition. A two-response group of 100 and 1,000 tokens under sequence-mean gives the same 10:1.

Deep-dive Does RL teach models new reasoning abilities? Argue both sides with evidence.

No, it sharpens. DeepSeekMath: Maj@K up, Pass@K not. Yue et al.: RL wins at \(k=1\) but the base overtakes at large \(k\) (about 9 points ahead on Minerva at \(k=128\), 32B); RL paths have high likelihood under the base; six algorithms leave a sampling-efficiency gap above 40 points; distillation extends the boundary. Qwen3-8B: RL raised AIME'24 pass@1 by 12.6 points with pass@64 unchanged; on-policy distillation raised pass@64. Random rewards lift Qwen2.5-Math-7B's MATH-500 by 21.4 points versus 29.1 for ground truth, via clipping bias amplifying priors. Dr. GRPO: base models already show "aha". Yes, under the right conditions. ProRL trains a distilled 1.5B model over 2,000 steps on 136K problems in five domains with KL resets and clip-higher, and finds success on tasks where the base fails at every \(k\), higher novelty scores, and gains largest where the start is weakest, while math still narrows. Reconciling. Pass@256 cannot see a path of per-sample probability \(10^{-6}\) (found with probability 0.026%) that RL could amplify enormously; numeric answers inflate pass@\(k\) via guesses; published RL runs are short and math-heavy; Qwen is atypical. My answer: RL reliably converts latent ability into pass@1, may expand with long, broad, entropy-preserving training, and distillation expands most cheaply when a teacher exists.

Deep-dive Design an RLVR run to improve a 7B model's math accuracy. What would you monitor?

Setup. A 7B model whose pass@8 on my prompts is well inside (0, 1); a second family for checking. Data: 10 to 20K problems with parseable answers (DAPO-Math-17K, or MATH plus GSM8K train), decontaminated, pre-filtered to pass rates about 0.1 to 0.9. Reward: binary normalized answer match, no length reward at first, truncated samples masked. Algorithm: GRPO with token-mean or constant normalization, \(\varepsilon_{\text{low}}=0.2\), \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}=0.28\), dynamic sampling, no KL (or \(\beta=0.001\) with resets from an SFT start), \(G=8\) to 16, 256 to 512 prompts per step, constant \(10^{-6}\) with a short warm-up, 1 to 4 minibatches per rollout, temperature 1.0, a 4K to 8K cap extended in stages. Evaluate on held-out prompts (avg@16-32), pass@\(k\) to 256, an out-of-domain set, and a distillation baseline if a teacher exists. Monitor: validation versus training reward, zero-advantage fraction, mean and p99 length and truncation rate, entropy, clipped fraction and train-sampler log-prob mismatch, gradient norm and KL, and read samples for hacks and language mixing. Responses: entropy collapse means widen \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}\) or filter better; length explosion means check normalization and add overlong shaping; validation falling while reward rises means stop and fix the verifier or data. Budget: 256 \(\times\) 8 \(\times\) 3,000 tokens is 6.1M tokens and about \(2.6\times10^{17}\) training FLOPs per step; rollout throughput sets the wall-clock.

Deep-dive Your training reward keeps rising, but held-out accuracy has fallen and response length has exploded. How do you diagnose it?

Separate three hypotheses and test each cheaply. (1) Checker exploitation or overfitting: read 50 high-reward samples for answer-first outputs without reasoning, several boxed answers where the parser takes the wrong one, multiple-choice guessing, or copied numbers. Fix the verifier and add a held-out check on another distribution. DAPO found training reward poorly correlated with validation accuracy, and R1 saw reward rise while Codeforces performance fell with a learned reward model. (2) Length bias or loops: under per-response normalization wrong answers grow for free; compare the mean length of correct versus incorrect samples (Dr. GRPO's diagnostic), check the truncation rate and repetition, and whether the cap was raised. Switch to token-mean or a constant normalizer, add overlong shaping and repetition-based early stopping. (3) Off-policy or numerical instability: check the clipped fraction, the sampler-trainer log-prob mismatch at the first minibatch, KL, gradient-norm and entropy spikes; reduce minibatches per rollout or the learning rate, mask far-off-policy negatives, fix precision (MiniMax's FP32 LM head), and for MoE keep the sampling routes. Also check that entropy has not collapsed and the zero-advantage fraction is not rising. A checkpoint sweep of held-out accuracy locates the break; roll back to before it with the fix applied.

Deep-dive Compare the compute of R1's RL with V3's pretraining, say how that changed in V3.2, and explain when you would distill instead of running RL.

R1-Zero used 512 H800 GPUs for about 198 hours, \(512\times198\approx101\)K GPU-hours; R1 about 41K more; SFT data 5K; total 147K, about $294K at $2 per GPU-hour. V3's pretraining used 2.664M, so R1's RL was \(142\text{K}/2664\text{K}\approx5.3\%\). DeepSeek-V3.2 reports an RL budget above 10% of pretraining cost and steady gains with more RL, so RL compute is now a first-class scaling axis. Distill when a stronger teacher exists and you want a small model fast: R1's 800K-sample SFT of Qwen2.5-32B reached 72.6 AIME pass@1 versus 47.0 for RL alone over more than 10K steps, and Qwen3's on-policy distillation of the 8B model beat RL on every benchmark at 1,800 versus 17,920 GPU-hours (74.4 versus 67.6 on AIME'24). Distillation can also extend pass@\(k\) coverage, while RL mostly sharpens. Run RL when there is no teacher (you are the frontier), the base model is big enough to explore (R1's 7B and 16B pure-RL attempts failed), the domain is verifiable, and you want to exceed the teacher's ceiling. The strongest recent pattern combines them: RL specialists merged by on-policy distillation (V4), or OPD followed by RL (a September 2026 study). Caveats: distillation needs traces RL had to produce first, and the RL baselines above were early recipes; DAPO-style fixes narrowed but did not close the gap on AIME (50 versus 72.6, different protocols).

Cheat sheet

  • RLVR: reward from a program (answer match, unit tests, format), usually binary; term from Tülu 3. No reward model to hack, but the checker is the spec. Only checkable domains; one bit per trajectory.
  • GRPO (DeepSeekMath Eq. 3): \(\hat A_i=(r_i-\text{mean})/\text{std}\), shared by all tokens; \(\frac1G\sum_i\frac1{|o_i|}\sum_t\{\min(\rho\hat A,\clip(\rho,1\pm\varepsilon)\hat A)-\beta\,\text{k3}\}\); k3 \(=e^u-u-1\), \(u=\log\pi_{\text{ref}}-\log\pi_\theta\). KL in the loss, not the reward. No critic. Group-mean advantage \(=\frac{G-1}{G}\times\) RLOO.
  • Worked case (\(G=8\), 3 correct): GRPO \(+1.208/-0.725\) (sample std 0.5175), Dr. GRPO \(+0.625/-0.375\), RLOO \(+0.714/-0.429\). All-correct or all-wrong: 0.
  • Biases: \(1/|o_i|\) gives short responses' tokens 10\(\times\) the weight at 100 vs 1,000 tokens (1/800 vs 1/8000 at \(G=8\)); std up-weights extreme prompts about 1.5\(\times\). Dr. GRPO: constant normalizer, no std. DAPO: token-mean, keeps std. V3.2: no std.
  • Useless-group probability: \(p^G+(1-p)^G\); \(p=0.9\): 43% at \(G=8\), 18.5% at \(G=16\). Cost per informative group \(G/(1-p^G-(1-p)^G)\).
  • DAPO: \(\varepsilon_{\text{low}}=0.2\), \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}=0.28\) (clip-higher); dynamic sampling; token-level loss; overlong shaping (\(L_{\max}=16{,}384\) + 4,096 buffer); no KL. AIME'24 30 to 50 (Qwen2.5-32B).
  • GSPO: \(s_i=(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}})^{1/|y_i|}\), clip \(3\text{e-}4/4\text{e-}4\); about 10% of experts change per update in Qwen3-30B-A3B, so GRPO needed Routing Replay. CISPO: \(\sg(\clip(\rho))\hat A\log\pi\), all tokens keep gradients, matches DAPO in 50% of steps.
  • V3.2 GRPO: \(\hat A=R-\text{mean}\); k3 times \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\); mask negative sequences that drift; Keep Routing; Keep Sampling Mask; length penalty; RL budget above 10% of pretraining cost.
  • R1: R1-Zero AIME'24 15.6 to 77.9 (v1: 71.0), 86.7 with voting; four stages (cold-start SFT, reasoning RL + language reward, ~600K + ~200K rejection-sampled SFT from V3-Base, final RL).
