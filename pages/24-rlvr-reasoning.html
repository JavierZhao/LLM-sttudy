RLVR & reasoning models: GRPO and successors

P0 ~80 min Prereqs: 22 Policy gradient to PPO, 23 RLHF in practice

Reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 are trained by sampling many long chains of thought, checking the final answers with a program, and reinforcing what passed. This page writes GRPO exactly as DeepSeekMath does, walks through R1-Zero and the R1 pipeline, treats Dr. GRPO, DAPO, GSPO and CISPO as answers to specific failures, and closes with the open debate on whether RL teaches models anything new, plus a recipe for a small run of your own.

What interviewers probe
  • Write the GRPO objective from memory with its exact normalizers (\(1/G\), \(1/|o_i|\)), where the KL term sits (in the loss, as the k3 estimator), and what is shared across the tokens of a response. Then say how it differs from PPO and why there is no critic.
  • The bias analysis: length bias from \(1/|o_i|\), difficulty bias from the group std, and how Dr. GRPO and DAPO's token-level loss remove them. Expect to compute per-token gradient weights for a 100-token and a 1,000-token response.
  • R1-Zero versus R1: what the reward was, what the cold start is for (readability, not capability), the four-stage pipeline, and what the "aha moment" does and does not show.
  • Each fix and the failure behind it: clip-higher against entropy collapse, dynamic sampling against zero-advantage groups, GSPO against token-level importance noise in MoE models, CISPO against clipped-out rare tokens.
  • Debate literacy and practice: pass@k evidence that RL only sharpens, the counter-evidence, and how you would design and monitor a real RLVR run.

RLVR: reinforcement learning on rewards you can check

Page 23 covered RLHF: a reward model trained on human preferences scores each response, and PPO maximizes that score under a KL leash. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards 可验证奖励强化学习 (RLVR) keeps the RL machinery and replaces the learned reward model with a program. The term comes from Tülu 3, which sets the reward to a constant \(\alpha\) (they use \(\alpha = 10\)) when the answer extracted from the completion is verifiably correct and to 0 otherwise, and trains with PPO plus a KL penalty on GSM8K, MATH and instruction-following constraints [Lambert et al. 2024, §6]. DeepSeek-R1 then showed that the same idea, run at scale on a strong base model, yields long chains of thought 思维链 with reflection and verification, without a single human-written reasoning trace [DeepSeek-R1, §2].

The reward function of a serious math recipe fits on one screen. The subtle parts are brace matching, normalization, and refusing credit when no boxed answer exists.

import re
from fractions import Fraction

def extract_boxed(text):
    """Content of the LAST \boxed{...} (brace-matched), or None."""
    i = text.rfind("\\boxed{")
    if i < 0:
        return None
    i += len("\\boxed{")
    depth, j = 1, i
    while j < len(text) and depth:
        depth += (text[j] == "{") - (text[j] == "}")
        j += 1
    return text[i:j - 1] if depth == 0 else None

def normalize(ans):
    ans = ans.strip().replace(" ", "").replace("\\!", "").replace("$", "").rstrip(".")
    ans = re.sub(r"\\d?frac\{(-?\d+)\}\{(\d+)\}", r"\1/\2", ans)   # \frac{1}{2} -> 1/2
    try:
        return str(Fraction(ans))          # numeric equivalence: "0.50", "1/2" and "\frac{1}{2}" agree
    except (ValueError, ZeroDivisionError):
        return ans

def accuracy_reward(response, gold):
    pred = extract_boxed(response)
    return float(pred is not None and normalize(pred) == normalize(gold))

def format_reward(response):               # R1-style structure check, added with equal weight in R1
    return float(re.fullmatch(r"(?s)\s*<think>.+?</think>\s*.+", response) is not None)

Why verifiable rewards resist hacking, and where they do not

A reward model is a function approximator fit to a finite set of human judgments, and an optimizer that queries it millions of times finds its blind spots (the over-optimization curves of page 23). DeepSeek-R1 therefore says it abstains from applying neural reward models, "whether outcome-based or process-based", to reasoning tasks, because they "are susceptible to reward hacking 奖励黑客 during large-scale reinforcement learning", and because retraining them costs compute and complicates the pipeline [DeepSeek-R1, §2.2]. Their appendix shows the failure with the helpfulness reward model: the reward keeps rising while Codeforces pass@1 falls (App. B.5, Fig. 6), and the paper confines preference-based rewards to the last 400 of the 1,700 steps of the final RL stage for that reason (§3.2.2).

"Resist" does not mean "immune". Four limits to state in an interview:

  1. The checker is the specification. Whatever it accepts gets optimized: a parser that accepts the first number in the response, a test suite that a hard-coded lookup table passes, a numeric-answer problem where 1 in 5 or 1 in 100 guesses succeed. Yue et al. manually read the correct chains of thought of the hardest solved problems to rule out lucky guesses for exactly this reason [Yue et al. 2025, §3.1].
  2. Rules produce false negatives too. Qwen3's general RL therefore adds a reward from an LLM that compares the response with a reference answer, "avoiding false negatives that can occur with purely rule-based rewards" [Qwen3, §4.4]. R1 finds LLM-versus-reference judging robust for short answers and poorly generalizing to open-ended writing [DeepSeek-R1, App. G.1].
  3. Only checkable domains. R1 lists writing as a task where a reliable reward is hard to build and falls back on human-annotated SFT data there [DeepSeek-R1, §6]. DeepSeek-V4 addresses hard-to-verify tasks with rubric-guided data and a generative reward model, and applies RL to the judge itself, so the actor doubles as the grader [DeepSeek-V4, §5.1.1].
  4. One bit per trajectory. A 20,000-token response earns a single 0 or 1. Credit assignment to individual tokens is left entirely to sampling statistics, which is why the group size \(G\) and the baseline design matter so much in what follows.
From your jet tagger

Your tagger had a differentiable loss on a label attached to every jet, so one forward pass gave an exact gradient. In RLVR the "label" is a scalar reward on a sequence the model sampled itself, and sampling is not differentiable. The gradient is a score-function estimate (page 22): unbiased but noisy. Every algorithm on this page is a way to lower that noise (group baselines), keep updates near the sampling policy (clipping), or discard samples that carry no signal (dynamic sampling).

GRPO, exactly

Group Relative Policy Optimization 组相对策略优化 was introduced in DeepSeekMath to remove PPO's critic [Shao et al. 2024, §4.1]. The critic is a problem for two reasons. It is a second model of the policy's size (a 7B policy under mixed-precision Adam holds about 16 bytes per parameter, so a 7B critic adds roughly \(7\times10^{9}\times16 = 112\) GB of training state, see page 15). And it must predict, from a partial response, the outcome reward of a completion that has not happened yet. R1 argues this is especially hard for long chains of thought: because the model reflects and revises, "the content initially generated may later be revised or contradicted, which makes it even less feasible to predict the final reward based on a partial response" [DeepSeek-R1, App. A.3]. GRPO instead samples \(G\) responses to the same prompt and uses the group's average reward as the baseline.

For each prompt \(q\), GRPO samples \(\{o_1,\dots,o_G\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid q)\), scores them with rewards \(r_1,\dots,r_G\), and forms one advantage per response:

\[ \hat A_{i,t} \;=\; \hat A_i \;=\; \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(r_1,\dots,r_G)}{\operatorname{std}(r_1,\dots,r_G)} \qquad\text{for every token } t \text{ of } o_i \]

Then it maximizes the following objective (DeepSeekMath Eq. 3 with the KL estimator of Eq. 4; we write \(\rho_{i,t}\) for the probability ratio because \(r\) is already the reward):

\[ \mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\E_{q,\ \{o_i\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac1{|o_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}\Big\{\min\!\big(\rho_{i,t}\,\hat A_{i},\ \clip(\rho_{i,t},\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,\hat A_{i}\big)\;-\;\beta\,\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}\big[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}\big]_{i,t}\Big\}\right] \] \[ \rho_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})},\qquad \mathbb D_{\text{KL}}\big[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}\big]_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}-1 \]
GRPO as written in DeepSeekMath. The last expression is the k3 estimator, with u = log π_ref − log π_θ it equals eu − u − 1 ≥ 0

Read the formula in four pieces, because every successor on this page changes exactly one of them:

  1. The advantage is shared by all tokens of a response. Nothing in GRPO says which token deserved the credit: a correct response pushes up every one of its tokens, sensible or not, and statistics over many samples average the noise out. This is REINFORCE with a per-prompt baseline.
  2. Two averages and one standard deviation. \(1/|o_i|\) averages tokens within a response, \(1/G\) averages responses within the group, and the std rescales each group. These are the three normalizers analyzed in the section on fixes.
  3. The KL is in the loss, not the reward. DeepSeekMath states the design goal plainly: instead of adding a KL penalty to the reward, GRPO "regularizes by directly adding the KL divergence between the trained policy and the reference policy to the loss, avoiding complicating the calculation of \(\hat A_{i,t}\)" [Shao et al. 2024, §4.1.1]. It sits inside the token sum, so it shares the \(1/|o_i|\) factor.
  4. The clip is against \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), not \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). It is a per-rollout trust region (page 22). DeepSeekMath does a single update per exploration stage, so \(\pi_\theta=\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), every \(\rho=1\), and the clip never activates. The gradient then reduces to [Shao et al. 2024, Eq. 20]:
\[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}=\E\!\left[\frac1G\sum_{i}\frac1{|o_i|}\sum_{t}\Big(\hat A_{i,t}+\beta\Big(\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)}-1\Big)\Big)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})\right] \]

A note on notation. The R1 paper writes the same objective at the sequence level, with \(\rho_i=\pi_\theta(o_i\mid q)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_i\mid q)\) and no token sum or \(1/|o_i|\) [DeepSeek-R1, Eq. 1]. DAPO, Dr. GRPO, GSPO and DeepSeek-V3.2 all write the token-level form above, and V3.2's statement of GRPO uses \(\hat A=R_i-\operatorname{mean}(R)\) without the std [DeepSeek-V3.2, Eq. 5]. Which normalization the R1 run itself used is not confirmed by the paper. When an interviewer says "GRPO", the safe reading is DeepSeekMath's Eq. 3.

Two pieces of evidence explain why the loss of token-level credit is acceptable here. First, when the only reward arrives at the end, GAE with \(\gamma=\lambda=1\) is just "return minus a learned baseline", so the real difference is how the baseline is estimated. DeepSeek's own comparison on the MATH task with DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite (a 16B MoE with 2.4B active parameters) found PPO with the common \(\lambda=0.95\) "considerably worse than GRPO", and with \(\lambda\) tuned to 1.0 "nearing" it [DeepSeek-R1, App. A.3, Fig. 4]. Second, R1 argues that a KL penalty in the reward penalizes the cumulative KL and "may implicitly penalize the length of the response", which they did not want in a setting where longer thinking is the point (same appendix). Those are the authors' arguments; we are not aware of a controlled ablation isolating the effect.

Why the group mean is a legitimate baseline

A baseline must not depend on the action it baselines, or the gradient estimate changes. The group mean includes \(r_i\) itself, so it does depend on \(o_i\), but only through a constant factor. Write \(\bar r_{-i}\) for the mean of the other \(G-1\) rewards, which is independent of \(o_i\). Then

\[ r_i-\bar r \;=\; \frac{G-1}{G}\,\big(r_i-\bar r_{-i}\big), \]

so the group-mean advantage equals the leave-one-out advantage (RLOO, Ahmadian et al. 2024, §2.3) times \((G-1)/G\). Its expected gradient points in the right direction, shrunk by a constant: unbiased up to scale. The std division is the part that is not a constant (it differs per prompt), and that is what the Dr. GRPO analysis attacks.

def grpo_loss(logp_new, logp_old, logp_ref, adv, mask, eps_low=0.2, eps_high=0.2, beta=0.0):
    # logp_*: (B, T) log-prob of the SAMPLED tokens; adv: (B,) one scalar per response; mask: (B, T)
    ratio = (logp_new - logp_old).exp()                                  # rho_{i,t}
    a = adv[:, None]                                                     # shared by every token of a response
    surr = torch.minimum(ratio * a, ratio.clamp(1 - eps_low, 1 + eps_high) * a)
    u = logp_ref - logp_new
    k3 = u.exp() - u - 1                                                 # >= 0; DeepSeekMath Eq. 4
    tok = (surr - beta * k3) * mask                                      # KL enters the loss, not the reward
    per_seq = tok.sum(-1) / mask.sum(-1)                                 # (1/|o_i|) sum_t
    return -per_seq.mean()                                               # (1/G) sum_i; negate to minimize

DeepSeek-R1-Zero and R1

R1-Zero: RL on a base model, with nothing else

DeepSeek-R1-Zero starts from DeepSeek-V3-Base (671B total parameters, 37B activated) and applies GRPO with no supervised fine-tuning at all, on the hypothesis that "human-defined reasoning patterns may limit model exploration" [DeepSeek-R1, §1]. The prompt tells the model to think inside <think> tags and answer inside <answer> tags and nothing else: "we intentionally limit our constraints to this structural format, avoiding any content-specific biases" (§2.3). The reward is a rule-based accuracy reward plus a format reward "with the same weight" (§2.2). The RL prompts are about 26K math questions (answers matched against a reference, reward 1 or 0, proofs excluded), 17K algorithm-competition problems and 8K bug-fixing tasks (hidden tests), 22K multiple-choice STEM questions, and 15K logic problems (App. B.3.1). The hyperparameters are in the table above: 32 questions times 16 samples per step, learning rate 3e-6, \(\beta=0.001\), 10,400 steps (1.6 epochs), with the length cap doubled from 32,768 to 65,536 tokens at step 8.2K.

The headline number: AIME 2024 pass@1 rises from 15.6% to 77.9% during training, and to 86.7% with self-consistency decoding (the figure shows cons@16). Those figures come from the January 2026 version (v2) of the paper. The January 2025 version reported 71.0% pass@1 and 86.7% with majority voting over 64 samples, so quote the version you mean. The average response length "exhibits a steady increase" during training, which the authors read as the model learning "to solve reasoning tasks with more thinking time" (Fig. 1b). Note that the paper itself says that "both the performance and response length of DeepSeek-R1-Zero exhibit a significant jump at the 8.2k step", the step at which the length cap doubled, so a part of the curve is a budget effect rather than pure emergence.

The "aha moment": what is claimed and what is shown

The paper's account, as stated: R1-Zero "exhibits an 'aha moment' (Table 2), characterized by a sudden increase in the use of the word 'wait' during reflections", which "marks a distinct change in reasoning patterns and clearly shows the self-evolution process". The supporting measurements are in App. C.2: a list of reflective words chosen by three human experts (wait, mistake, however, but, retry, error, verify, wrong, evaluate, check) rises 5- to 7-fold from the start of training, and "wait" was "nearly absent during early training, showed occasional usage between steps 4000-7000, and then exhibited significant spikes after step 8000". Table 2 shows an intermediate checkpoint writing "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here." while solving a nested-radical equation.

What this establishes is a change in the frequency of certain tokens during RL. What it does not establish is that RL created reflection, or that reflection causes the accuracy gain:

  • Dr. GRPO hosted DeepSeek-V3-Base itself and found that, under the R1 template, it "generates a decent amount of self-reflections", including "Aha" and "wait", before any RL [Liu et al. 2025, §2.3].
  • Within R1-Zero, they compared responses with and without self-reflection on the same question and found that in nearly half the cases the reflective responses were not more accurate. They add that reflection may still help exploration during training, which their analysis does not test (App. F).
  • MiniMax observed the mechanism from the other side: reflective tokens ("However", "Recheck", "Wait", "Aha") are rare and low-probability under the base model, so under PPO-style clipping they are clipped out after the first update and cannot be reinforced further [MiniMax-M1, §3.1]. This motivates CISPO below.

A defensible position: RL selects and amplifies latent behaviors (some of which look like reflection), the visible token statistics are real, and the causal story is not settled.

From R1-Zero to R1: the four-stage pipeline

R1-Zero reasons well but "faces challenges such as poor readability and language mixing", and its RL was "narrowly focused on reasoning tasks, resulting in limited performance in broader areas such as writing and open-domain question answering" (§1). R1 fixes both with four stages (§3, Fig. 2):

  1. Cold-start SFT. Fine-tune V3-Base on "thousands" of long chains of thought in a conversational, first-person style with reflection and verification. The data are built from R1-Zero outputs filtered for correct answers and readable format (sympy for math, repetition and language-mixing filters), rewritten by DeepSeek-V3 (including "Translate the thinking process to the same language as the question"), and passed through human annotators. The stated motivation is "primarily product-driven" (App. B.3.2).
  2. Reasoning-oriented RL. The same GRPO recipe as R1-Zero, plus a language-consistency reward, the fraction of target-language words in the chain of thought, added to the final reward. The authors' ablation shows "a slight degradation" in performance and better readability, and they keep it (§3.2.1, App. B.6).
  3. Rejection sampling and SFT. Sample from the stage-2 checkpoint, keep correct responses, and filter out mixed-language, long-paragraph and code-block chains of thought. Reasoning prompts that lack a rule-based check are judged by DeepSeek-V3 against the reference. The result is about 600K reasoning samples plus about 200K non-reasoning samples (writing, factual QA, translation; mostly reused from V3's SFT set). The 800K samples fine-tune DeepSeek-V3-Base again for 2 to 3 epochs (App. B.3.3, B.4.2). The RL checkpoint is only a data generator here, not the initialization.
  4. RL for all scenarios. Reasoning prompts keep rule-based rewards. General prompts use reward models: helpfulness judged on the final summary, harmlessness on the whole response. The reward sums reasoning, general and language terms. Temperature drops to 0.7 (higher values "lead to incoherent generation"), and of 1,700 steps the preference-based data are used only in the last 400 (§3.2.2).

What the cold start buys is readable, instruction-following, language-consistent behavior (IF-Eval 46.6 to 71.7, Arena-Hard 53.6 to 77.0) and a better-behaved starting policy for stage 2. The v1 paper also frames it as avoiding "the early unstable cold start phase of RL training from the base model". The final stage barely moves AIME (78.1 to 79.8) and mostly lifts the preference benchmarks (Arena-Hard 75.6 to 92.3), as the paper states. It would still be wrong to say R1 is just R1-Zero made readable: on math and GPQA the two are close (AIME 79.8 versus 77.9, GPQA 71.5 versus 75.8), but on code the extra stages matter a lot (Codeforces rating 1444 to 2029, LiveCodeBench 50.0 to 65.9).

What it cost

R1-Zero trained on 64 nodes of 8 H800 GPUs (512 GPUs) for about 198 hours: \(512\times198=101{,}376\) GPU-hours, which the paper rounds to 101K. R1 took about 80 hours on the same cluster (\(512\times80=40{,}960\approx41\)K GPU-hours), and creating the SFT data took 5K GPU-hours. The total is 147K GPU-hours [DeepSeek-R1, App. B.4.4, Table 7], which at the paper's assumed rental price of $2 per GPU-hour is about $294K ($202K for R1-Zero, $82K for R1, $10K for the data). DeepSeek-V3's pre-training used 2.664M H800 GPU-hours [DeepSeek-V3, §1], so R1's RL amounted to \(142\text{K}/2664\text{K}\approx5.3\%\) of it. Keep that number in mind: DeepSeek-V3.2 reports an RL budget that "exceeds 10% of the pre-training cost" (see test-time scaling).

What did not work: PRMs and MCTS

R1 reports two failed directions, "but this does not imply that these approaches are incapable of developing effective reasoning models" [DeepSeek-R1, App. G.2]. Process reward models: (1) it is hard to define a fine-grained step in general reasoning; (2) judging whether an intermediate step is correct is itself hard, since automatic annotation is unreliable and manual annotation does not scale; (3) any model-based PRM invites reward hacking and needs retraining, complicating the pipeline. Their verdict is that a PRM is good for reranking or guided search but its advantage is limited against the overhead in large-scale RL. Monte Carlo tree search guided by a value model: (1) unlike chess, token generation has an exponentially larger search space, and capping node extensions risks local optima; (2) the value model steers each search step, and a fine-grained value model is hard to train, so the self-improvement loop stalls. Compare the critic argument for GRPO above: it is the same difficulty (a token-level value function for open-ended text) surfacing in three places. Page 23 covers PRMs versus outcome reward models in more depth.

Fixes and successors

Every method below changes one piece of the GRPO objective, and it helps to keep the anatomy in mind: (1) how advantages are normalized (the std), (2) how the loss is averaged over tokens and responses (\(1/|o_i|\), \(1/G\)), (3) the unit and shape of the trust region (token ratio, clip bounds), (4) the KL term, and (5) which samples are used at all. A summary table closes the section.

The two biases in GRPO's normalization (Dr. GRPO)

Dr. GRPO ("GRPO done right") starts from the GRPO gradient in the on-policy limit. Token \(t\) of response \(i\) receives weight

\[ w_{i,t}\;=\;\frac{1}{G}\cdot\frac{\hat A_i}{|o_i|},\qquad \hat A_i=\frac{r_i-\bar r}{\operatorname{std}(r)} , \]

times \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid\cdot)\). Two normalizers appear in that weight, and each is a bias [Liu et al. 2025, §3.1]:

  • Response-level length bias, from \(1/|o_i|\). For a correct response (\(\hat A_i>0\)) a shorter answer gets a larger per-token push, so the policy learns brevity when it is right. For a wrong response (\(\hat A_i<0\)) a longer answer gets a smaller per-token penalty, so verbosity is cheap when it is wrong. The net effect is that wrong answers drift longer without any reasoning benefit. They also note that popular open-source PPO implementations (TRL, OpenRLHF, verl, SimpleRL-Zero, Open-Reasoner-Zero) normalize the loss by response length in the same way (their Table 2, as of March 2025).
  • Question-level difficulty bias, from the std. Prompts whose rewards have a small spread (almost all correct or almost all wrong) are divided by a small number and so get a larger weight. Advantage normalization is common in RL, but it is normally done over a whole batch, not per question.

DAPO: four changes that make long-CoT RL work

ByteDance Seed started from Qwen2.5-32B and found that naive GRPO reached only 30 on AIME 2024, against 47 for DeepSeek's R1-Zero-Qwen-32B, with "entropy collapse, reward noise, and training instability" [DAPO, §1]. DAPO (Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization) adds four techniques, written jointly as:

\[ \mathcal J_{\text{DAPO}}(\theta)=\E\!\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{G}|o_i|}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}\min\!\big(\rho_{i,t}\hat A_{i,t},\ \clip(\rho_{i,t},\,1-\varepsilon_{\text{low}},\,1+\varepsilon_{\text{high}})\hat A_{i,t}\big)\right]\quad\text{s.t. }\ 0\lt\big|\{o_i:\ o_i\ \text{correct}\}\big|\lt G \]
DAPO, Eq. 8 of the paper: token-level normalization, decoupled clip, no KL term, and only groups with mixed outcomes. The advantage keeps the group std.
  1. Clip-higher (against entropy collapse). The upper clip bounds how far one update can raise a token's probability, and that bound is far tighter for rare tokens than for likely ones. With \(\varepsilon=0.2\), a token with \(\pi_{\text{old}}=0.01\) can rise to at most \(0.01\times1.2=0.012\), while one at 0.9 can rise to 1.08, that is, to certainty. The authors measured that the mean probability of up-clipped tokens is below 0.2. So the clip protects "exploitation" tokens from nothing and blocks "exploration" tokens from growing. They raise \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}\) to 0.28 and keep \(\varepsilon_{\text{low}}=0.2\), "because increasing it will suppress the probability of these tokens to 0, resulting in the collapse of the sampling space" (§3.1). See Figure 3.
  2. Dynamic sampling (against zero-advantage groups). If all \(G\) responses are correct (or all wrong) the group's advantages are exactly 0, so it contributes no gradient, yet it still occupies batch slots. DAPO measured that the share of prompts with accuracy 1 keeps rising during training (their Fig. 3b), so the effective batch shrinks and gradient variance grows. The fix is to over-sample, drop groups with accuracy 0 or 1, and keep sampling until the batch is full of informative groups. It reached the same accuracy in fewer steps despite the extra sampling (Fig. 6).
  3. Token-level loss (against length under-weighting). The \(1/\sum_i|o_i|\) normalization of the worked table above. The authors report a small accuracy gain but healthier entropy and length dynamics (Fig. 4).
  4. Overlong reward shaping (against truncation noise). A response cut off at the length cap normally gets a punitive reward even if its reasoning was sound, which teaches the model that sound reasoning is wrong. Step one: mask the loss of truncated samples ("overlong filtering"). Step two: a soft, length-aware penalty added to the correctness reward, \[ R_{\text{length}}(y)=\begin{cases}0,& |y|\le L_{\max}-L_{\text{cache}}\\[2pt] \dfrac{(L_{\max}-L_{\text{cache}})-|y|}{L_{\text{cache}}},& L_{\max}-L_{\text{cache}}\lt|y|\le L_{\max}\\[6pt] -1,& L_{\max}\lt|y|\end{cases} \] with \(L_{\max}=16{,}384\) and a 4,096-token buffer, so 20,480 tokens in total. Examples: a 12,288-token response pays 0, a 14,336-token one pays \((12{,}288-14{,}336)/4096=-0.5\), and a 16,384-token one pays \(-1\).

Two further choices: DAPO drops the KL term ("the model distribution can diverge significantly from the initial model" during long-CoT training, so the restriction "is not necessary"), and uses a plain \(\pm1\) rule-based reward on DAPO-Math-17K, 17K prompts whose answers were rewritten to be integers (§2.3, §2.4, §3.5).

GSPO: make the importance ratio match the reward

Qwen's Group Sequence Policy Optimization starts from a statistical objection. Importance sampling corrects a mismatch between distributions by averaging over many samples, but GRPO applies a ratio \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\) at each token position using one sampled token from that position's next-token distribution, so it cannot perform the correction and instead "introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients, which accumulates over long sequences and is exacerbated by the clipping mechanism" [Zheng et al. 2025, §3]. Their principle: "the unit of the optimization objective should match the unit of reward". The reward is granted to the whole sequence, so the importance ratio and the clip should act on the whole sequence:

\[ s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}=\exp\!\Big(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\rho_{i,t}\Big),\qquad \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}=\E\!\left[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\!\big(s_i\hat A_i,\ \clip(s_i,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat A_i\big)\right] \]
GSPO: the ratio is the geometric mean of the token ratios; clipping removes whole responses from the gradient

The length normalization (the exponent \(1/|y_i|\)) keeps \(s_i\) in a common numerical range for short and long responses and stops a few tokens from swinging the ratio. Because \(s_i\) is a geometric mean of token ratios, its natural clip range is tiny: the paper uses \(3\times10^{-4}\) (left) and \(4\times10^{-4}\) (right), against 0.2 and 0.27 for their GRPO baseline. They observe that GSPO clips about two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO and is still more sample-efficient, and read this as evidence that GRPO's token-level gradients are noisy. The gradients also differ: GSPO weights all tokens of a response equally by \(s_i\hat A_i/|y_i|\), whereas GRPO weights each token by its own ratio \(\rho_{i,t}\), which ranges in \((0,1+\varepsilon]\) for \(\hat A>0\) and \([1-\varepsilon,\infty)\) for \(\hat A<0\) (§4.2). A variant, GSPO-token, allows per-token advantages and is numerically identical when they are equal.

The MoE motivation. In MoE models a gradient step changes which experts fire. For the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, "roughly 10% of the experts activated under the new policy \(\pi_\theta\) are different from those under the old policy" for the same response after each update, which makes token ratios fluctuate wildly. Qwen's earlier fix was Routing Replay: cache the experts activated under \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) and replay them when computing \(\pi_\theta\), at the price of memory, communication and a cap on the model's effective capacity. GSPO removes the need, because "the MoE model always maintains its language modeling capability", so the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate much even when individual token routes do (§5.3). DeepSeek chose the other route: Keep Routing in V3.2 (see below) is essentially the same mechanism as Routing Replay, while GSPO changes the objective instead. See page 08 for routing. A last GSPO claim: sequence-level likelihoods tolerate the numerical mismatch between the training and inference engines, so the inference engine's log-probabilities can be used directly without recomputation (§5.4), which matters for partial rollouts and multi-turn RL (page 25).

Caveats: the evidence is Qwen's own, on a cold-start Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model; GSPO keeps the group-std advantage and omits KL. As of late 2025 the same team proposed SAPO, which replaces hard clipping with a smooth temperature-controlled gate on the token ratio, \(f(\rho)=\tfrac{4}{\tau}\sigma(\tau(\rho-1))\), with a larger temperature for negative-advantage tokens (\(\tau_{\text{pos}}=1.0\), \(\tau_{\text{neg}}=1.05\)) because "negative updates tend to increase the logits of many inappropriate tokens" and are less stable [Gao et al. 2025]. The direction of travel is consistent: hard token-level clipping is the weak point of GRPO at scale.

CISPO: clip the weight, not the update

MiniMax-M1 met a different failure. In zero-RL runs on their hybrid-attention model, GRPO "failed to effectively promote the emergence of long CoT reasoning behaviors". Controlled ablations traced this to clipping: tokens that start reflective behavior (However, Recheck, Wait, Aha), which "often serve as 'forks' in reasoning paths", are rare and low-probability under the base model, so after one update their ratio \(\rho\) is high and they are clipped out of every later off-policy update. With 16 rounds of off-policy updates per generation batch, DAPO's larger \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}\) was found "less effective in our setup" [MiniMax-M1, §3.1]. Their fix starts from the REINFORCE objective with a stop-gradient importance weight and clips that weight rather than the update:

\[ \mathcal J_{\text{CISPO}}(\theta)=\E\!\left[\frac1{\sum_i|o_i|}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}\sg\big(\hat\rho_{i,t}\big)\,\hat A_{i,t}\,\log\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})\right],\qquad \hat\rho_{i,t}=\clip\big(\rho_{i,t},\,1-\varepsilon^{IS}_{\text{low}},\,1+\varepsilon^{IS}_{\text{high}}\big) \]
CISPO: the clipped importance weight is a constant (stop-gradient) multiplying a plain policy-gradient term, so every token keeps a gradient

Without clipping this is the standard policy gradient. In their experiments the lower bound was effectively removed and only \(\varepsilon^{IS}_{\text{high}}\) was tuned. The gradient is slightly biased by the cap but "preserves gradient contributions from all tokens, especially in long responses". CISPO also uses the token-level loss, group-relative advantages, dynamic sampling and a length penalty from DAPO, and no KL. On Qwen2.5-32B zero-RL with DAPO's math data it matched DAPO's AIME 2024 score with 50% of the training steps (a "2x speedup") and beat GRPO at equal steps. MiniMax-M1's whole RL run took three weeks on 512 H800 GPUs, about $0.53M of rental cost (§1). The three treatments of a token whose ratio has grown past the cap, for \(\hat A>0\):

M1 needed more machinery when it extended the output limit from 40K to 80K tokens: negative samples grew faster than positive ones and hit the window first, so large negative gradients accumulated in the tails of sequences and produced garbled endings. RLOO, REINFORCE++ and Kimi: baselines without a group std

RLOO (REINFORCE leave-one-out) uses, for sample \(i\), the mean reward of the other \(k-1\) samples as its baseline: \(\frac1k\sum_i\big[R(y_i)-\frac1{k-1}\sum_{j\ne i}R(y_j)\big]\nabla\log\pi(y_i\mid x)\). It is unbiased, needs no critic and no std, and was proposed for RLHF, where the authors also found that plain REINFORCE beat PPO by 3.2% to 20.3% in win-rate across datasets and models and that modeling partial completions is unnecessary [Ahmadian et al. 2024, §1, §2.3]. It is the cleanest member of this family and the same argument as GRPO's, minus the std and the clip. REINFORCE++ normalizes advantages across the global batch instead of per prompt: its \(k=1\) form puts a token-level k1 KL penalty in the reward and normalizes the resulting advantages over the batch, and its "w/ Baseline" form subtracts the group mean and then divides by the batch std, with a separate k2 KL loss [Hu et al., v9, §3]. The authors' claims (local std is biased and unstable, global statistics converge for large batches) are theirs; the paper has been revised nine times, so cite the version.

Kimi k1.5 uses a variant of online policy mirror descent with the mean reward of the group as baseline and an \(\ell_2\)-style regularizer to the current policy, and no value network. Its argument against a critic is worth quoting in an interview: suppose the model has written a partial chain of thought and can continue with a correct step or with a wrong one that it later recovers from. An oracle value function would give the wrong step a lower value and penalize it, but "exploring \(z'_{t+1}\) is extremely valuable for training the model to generate long CoT"; with only the final answer as the reward, the model can learn trial and error as long as it recovers [Kimi k1.5, §2.3.2].

What DeepSeek changed when it scaled GRPO (V3.2)

DeepSeek-V3.2 still uses GRPO, and describes its stabilizers precisely [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3.1]:

  • Advantage without the std: \(\hat A_{i,t}=R_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{R})\).
  • Unbiased KL estimate. Samples come from \(\pi_{\text{old}}\), so the k3 term is multiplied by the importance ratio \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\). Without this, "when the sampled tokens have substantially lower probabilities under the current policy than the reference policy", the k3 gradient assigns "disproportionately large, unbounded weights" to raising those tokens, which is noisy. They also note that for domains such as mathematics, "applying a relatively weak KL penalty or even omitting it entirely can yield improved performance".
  • Off-policy sequence masking. Mask (drop from the loss) any sequence with negative advantage whose average log-ratio \(\frac1{|o_i|}\sum_t\log\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}}{\pi_\theta}\) exceeds a threshold \(\delta\). Only negative sequences are masked: "the model benefits the most by learning from its own mistakes, whereas highly off-policy negative samples can be detrimental". \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) is the probability returned by the inference engine, so the measured divergence includes train-inference mismatch.
  • Keep Routing (MoE): record the expert routes used at sampling time and force the same routes in training; "crucial for RL training stability of MoE models" and used since DeepSeek-V3-0324.
  • Keep Sampling Mask: top-p and top-k truncation at sampling time makes \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) live on a subset of tokens, so the same mask is applied to \(\pi_\theta\) in training; combined with top-p sampling this "effectively preserves language consistency".
  • Rewards: rule-based outcome reward, a length penalty and a language-consistency reward for reasoning and agent tasks; a generative reward model with per-prompt rubrics for general tasks.
Which KL does the k3 term actually regularize?

The k3 estimator is unbiased for the value of \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\), but the loss is differentiated, and the samples are held fixed when you do. Differentiating k3 with the samples fixed gives \(\nabla_\theta\text{k3}=-\big(\tfrac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}-1\big)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\), whose on-policy expectation is \(-\sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y)=\nabla_\theta\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{ref}}\|\pi_\theta)\): the gradient of the forward KL, not the reverse KL that the estimate is named after. Multiplying by \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\) and differentiating through that ratio, as V3.2 does, restores the reverse-KL gradient; so does the k2 estimator \(\tfrac12(\log\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})^2\), which is why REINFORCE++ uses it as a loss. We checked all three identities numerically on a six-way categorical distribution (our derivation, not a claim from the papers). Page 22 treats the estimator family in full.

Training dynamics: entropy, length and what carries signal

Entropy collapse and its covariance mechanism

Let \(H\) be the policy's average per-token entropy on its own samples. In RLVR runs without intervention, \(H\) falls quickly and the policy becomes nearly deterministic: the \(G\) samples for a prompt become near-copies, groups become uniform (zero advantage), exploration stops and the reward plateaus. DAPO saw this with naive PPO and GRPO (their Fig. 2b), and Cui et al. turned it into a quantitative law: across many runs without entropy intervention, downstream performance \(R\) is fit by \(R=-a\,e^{H}+b\), so performance is "traded from" entropy and the ceiling at exhausted entropy (\(H=0\), \(R=-a+b\)) is predictable. In their runs over 95% of the entropy drop and of the performance gain happen early in training [Cui et al. 2025, Fig. 1] (entropy collapse 熵坍缩).

Why does entropy fall? For a softmax policy with logits \(z\), a first-order calculation gives

\[ H(\pi^{k+1})-H(\pi^{k})\;\approx\;-\operatorname{Cov}_{a\sim\pi^{k}(\cdot\mid s)}\!\Big(\log\pi^{k}(a\mid s),\ z^{k+1}_{s,a}-z^{k}_{s,a}\Big) \] \[ \text{policy gradient: }\ \Delta z=\eta\,\pi(a\mid s)A(s,a)\ \Rightarrow\ \Delta H\approx-\eta\operatorname{Cov}\big(\log\pi,\ \pi A\big),\qquad \text{natural PG: }\ \Delta z=\eta A\ \Rightarrow\ \Delta H\approx-\eta\operatorname{Cov}\big(\log\pi,\ A\big) \]
Entropy dynamics (Cui et al., Lemma 1, Proposition 1, Theorems 1 and 2; tabular softmax policy)

In words: an action that is already likely and has positive advantage lowers entropy (the update sharpens the distribution), while a rare action with positive advantage raises it. The authors verify empirically on Qwen2.5-7B that the covariance term tracks the measured entropy change, and that it stays mostly positive throughout training, which explains why entropy falls monotonically: after pretraining the model's confidence is roughly calibrated, so its high-probability tokens tend to be the good ones. RLVR is a machine for the first row: correct answers are mostly the high-probability continuations, so reinforcing them sharpens the policy. The remedies act on this covariance directly or indirectly:

  • Clip-higher (DAPO) and CISPO let rare, positive-advantage tokens keep growing, which is the entropy-raising row.
  • Clip-Cov and KL-Cov (Cui et al.) go after the cause: find the small fraction of tokens with the largest covariance and either remove them from the gradient (Clip-Cov, clip ratio \(2\times10^{-4}\)) or apply a KL penalty to them (KL-Cov, top-\(k\) fraction \(2\times10^{-3}\) for 7B and \(2\times10^{-4}\) for 32B, \(\beta=1\)). On Qwen2.5 with DAPO-Math data they report gains over GRPO of 2.0% average for 7B and 6.4% for 32B, and entropy more than 10 times higher than the plateaued baseline. They also report that clip-higher raises entropy early but later becomes unstable in their runs, and that a naive entropy bonus is ineffective (§4).
  • Sampling temperature and the KL leash. ProRL finds that a higher rollout temperature "only delays the onset of entropy collapse", and that a KL penalty to a reference policy plus periodic hard resets of the reference (and optimizer) sustains entropy over more than 2,000 steps when starting from an already-capable checkpoint [ProRL, §2].
  • Deliberate entropy control. Qwen3's reasoning RL controls entropy "to increase steadily or remain stable", which they call crucial for stability [Qwen3, §4.2]. DAPO's guidance is to monitor it: too low means no exploration, too high means over-randomness.

Response length: growth, overthinking and length control

Length growth is the most-watched curve in reasoning RL and the most misread. At least four things push it up, and only the first is "reasoning": (1) longer chains genuinely help on hard problems; (2) the per-response \(1/|o_i|\) bias (Dr. GRPO) lets wrong answers grow for free; (3) a schedule that raises the length cap, as at step 8.2K in R1-Zero; (4) unproductive loops (repetition, endless "wait"). DAPO's advice is to treat length as a health indicator only in conjunction with validation accuracy, since it "does not always maintain a continuous upward trend" and can stagnate or fall for long periods [DAPO, §4.3].

Once length grows, cost and overthinking 过度思考 follow. Kimi k1.5 observed that response length "significantly increases during RL training", which improves accuracy but is costly and "often not preferred by humans", and added a length reward computed within each group of \(k\) samples: with \(\text{len}(i)\) the response length, \(\lambda=0.5-\dfrac{\text{len}(i)-\text{min\_len}}{\text{max\_len}-\text{min\_len}}\), the reward is \(\lambda\) for a correct response and \(\min(0,\lambda)\) for a wrong one, and zero for all if every response has the same length [Kimi k1.5, §2.3.3]. Example: correct answers of 1,000, 2,000 and 4,000 tokens in a group whose shortest is 1,000 and longest 4,000 earn \(+0.5\), \(+0.167\) and \(-0.5\); a wrong 4,000-token answer earns \(-0.5\), a wrong 1,000-token one 0. The reward favors short-and-correct and never rewards short-and-wrong. Because it slows early learning, they train without it first and then hold it constant. Their long2short toolbox also includes model merging, shortest-rejection-sampling, DPO and a second RL phase with the penalty.

DeepSeek treats length as a dial. V3.2 uses a length penalty in its reasoning and agent rewards, and notes that its reported scores are "constrained by a length constraint reward model; upon removal of the restriction, we observe further improvement", while the experimental V3.2-Speciale was trained with a reduced length penalty on reasoning data only [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3, §4.1]. DeepSeek-V4 makes the dial explicit, training one specialist per reasoning-effort mode "under divergent RL configurations" with "distinct length penalties and context windows" (below). R1 itself lists overthinking on simple questions as a remaining limitation [DeepSeek-R1, §6].

Two lessons. First, as the policy improves, many training prompts drift to \(p\to1\) and the useless fraction climbs, which is exactly what DAPO measured (share of accuracy-1 prompts rising over training) and why filtering matters more late in training. Second, a larger \(G\) buys a better baseline, not efficiency: at \(p=0.9\), one informative group costs 14.0 samples at \(G=8\), 19.6 at \(G=16\) and 64.1 at \(G=64\). DeepSeekMath's \(G=64\) reflects a 7B model on 1,024-token answers, not a universal optimum; most open long-CoT recipes use 8 to 16.

Ways to keep the batch informative, in order of how early they act:

  • Offline difficulty filtering. Estimate pass rates with the starting model (or an earlier run) and drop prompts that are solved always or never. MiniMax used its 40K-token model's pass rates to remove easy prompts before the 80K stage; Qwen3's reasoning-RL prompts had to be "learnable" for the cold-start model, "as challenging as possible", unused in the cold start, and diverse, which left 3,995 query-verifier pairs [Qwen3, §4.2].
  • Online filtering. DAPO's dynamic sampling, above: measure this step's group accuracies and refill the batch.
  • Curricula and prioritized sampling. Kimi k1.5 starts with easier problems and moves to harder ones (curriculum), and samples prompt \(i\) with probability proportional to \(1-s_i\), where \(s_i\) is its tracked success rate (prioritized sampling), so weak spots get more attention [Kimi k1.5, §2.3.4].
  • Don't trust the training reward. DAPO found the final reward on the training set "often exhibits little correlation with the accuracy on the validation set", a sign of overfitting to the prompt set (§4.3). Always hold out prompts.

The flip side of filtering is a limit: prompts the model can never solve give no signal at all, so plain RLVR cannot teach what the model cannot sample. That is the bridge to the next section.

Does RL add capability? An open debate

The question has practical weight: it decides whether extra compute should go into pretraining, RL, or distillation from a stronger teacher. The evidence below is presented as a debate, because the literature has not converged (as of September 2026 it is still active, with new work on combining distillation and RL).

The sharpening view

The first hint is in DeepSeekMath itself: after GRPO, Maj@K improved but Pass@K did not, which the authors read as RL "rendering the output distribution more robust", boosting the correct response from the top-K rather than improving fundamental capability [Shao et al. 2024, §5.2.2]. Yue et al. made this the central experiment [Yue et al. 2025]. They compare base and RL-trained models by pass@k for large \(k\), up to 256 samples or more, on math, code and visual-reasoning benchmarks. The RL models are zero-RL GRPO checkpoints from SimpleRL-Zoo (Qwen2.5-7B, 14B, 32B and Llama-3.1-8B, trained on GSM8K and MATH with a correctness-only reward), plus Oat-Zero-7B and DAPO-32B. Their findings:

  • At small \(k\) (\(k=1\), the usual accuracy) RL models win. As \(k\) grows to tens or hundreds the curves cross and the base model overtakes: for example on Minerva with the 32B model the base model is about 9 points ahead at \(k=128\). Oat-Zero and DAPO start about 30 points above their bases at \(k=1\) and are eventually passed by them.
  • Coverage and perplexity analyses suggest that the reasoning paths of RL models already lie inside the base model's sampling distribution, i.e. the RL model mostly re-weights them.
  • Six RL algorithms (PPO, GRPO, REINFORCE++, RLOO, ReMax, DAPO) behave similarly. Defining a sampling-efficiency gap \(\Delta_{SE}\) as the base model's pass@256 minus the RL model's pass@1, all remain above 40 points on their in-domain set (from 43.9 for GRPO to 42.6 for RLOO), so none comes close to exploiting the base model's coverage.
  • Distillation behaves differently: the pass@k curve of DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B sits consistently above that of its base, so distillation can extend the boundary while RL, in their runs, mostly does not.

A lab-side data point points the same way. On Qwen3-8B, after off-policy distillation from a large teacher, further RL raised AIME'24 pass@1 from 55.0 to 67.6 and AIME'25 from 42.8 to 55.5 but left pass@64 exactly unchanged (90.0 and 83.3), while on-policy distillation from a stronger teacher reached 74.4 and 65.5 with pass@64 of 93.3 and 86.7, at 1,800 GPU-hours instead of 17,920 [Qwen3, §4.7, Table 21]. AIME has 30 problems, so one problem is 3.3 points and the pass@64 differences are one problem each: suggestive, not conclusive. A September 2026 study of a Qwen3-8B teacher and Qwen3-1.7B-Base student reports that on-policy distillation followed by RL beats pure OPD, pure RL and all joint schemes, with the explanation that OPD expands coverage of teacher-supported solutions and RL sharpens within that support [Li et al. 2026].

Counter-evidence: prolonged RL

ProRL argues the negative results reflect method limits, not RL's limits: (1) they concentrate on math, where models are heavily trained in pretraining and post-training, and (2) RL runs are stopped after "no more than hundreds of steps" [Liu et al. 2025, §1]. Their recipe trains DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (a distilled checkpoint, not a raw base) for more than 2,000 steps on 136K verifiable problems across math, code, STEM, logic puzzles and instruction following, using DAPO-style decoupled clipping (\(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}=0.4\)), dynamic sampling, temperature 1.2, a KL penalty and periodic reference resets, for about 16K GPU-hours. They report large gains in every domain (for example +54.8% on Reasoning Gym logic puzzles), success on tasks where the base model fails at every \(k\), and a higher "Creativity Index" (less overlap with the pretraining corpus) for the RL model's outputs. The improvement in pass@128 correlates negatively with the base model's own pass@128: "the weaker the start, the stronger the gain". They classify benchmarks into three regimes as training proceeds: diminish (breadth narrows, mostly math), plateau, and sustained gains (for example some code and puzzle tasks). So even ProRL's own evidence contains the Yue et al. effect on math.

Spurious rewards: what RLVR on Qwen actually optimizes

A third strand asks how much of the reward signal matters at all. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, GRPO trained for about 300 steps improved MATH-500 by +29.1 points with ground-truth rewards, +24.1 with a reward for matching majority-vote answers that are incorrect, and +21.4 with rewards assigned uniformly at random (probability 0.5); a format-only reward also helped [Shao et al. 2025, §2]. On AIME 2025, whose problems postdate the models, the gains from spurious rewards largely vanish; and on Llama-3 and OLMo-2 they yield little or nothing. Their proposed mechanism is a clipping bias: with random rewards the expected gradient is zero when clipping is removed, but with clipping the expected update raises the probability of tokens the model already favors, amplifying high-prior behaviors. In Qwen2.5-Math one such behavior is "code reasoning" (writing Python-style reasoning without executing it), whose frequency rises from 65% to over 90% under any of the rewards and correlates with accuracy (answers using it are correct 60.9% of the time). The authors stress that these rewards are analysis tools, not a training recipe, and recommend validating RLVR methods on several model families and using spurious rewards as a baseline. Dr. GRPO makes a compatible point: Qwen2.5 models answer best with no chat template (100% answering rate), and the authors hypothesize that Qwen2.5-Math was pretrained on concatenated question-answer text, so results on these models may not transfer [Liu et al. 2025, §2.2].

How to hold the debate in an interview

What everyone accepts. At typical open-source scale, RLVR raises pass@1 chiefly by making already-reachable solutions more probable. That is valuable in itself: users see pass@1, and running majority voting or best-of-\(k\) at inference costs \(k\) times more, so RLVR effectively distills the group's best behavior into a single sample. R1 shows the gap it closes: AIME 2024 pass@1 79.8, majority vote over 64 samples 86.7, pass@64 90.0 [DeepSeek-R1, App. E.4].

What is contested. Whether long, diverse, well-conditioned RL creates solution paths that had negligible base probability, and whether pass@\(k\) can even see that. Here is the measurement problem in numbers. A solution that a base model produces with probability \(q\) per sample is found by pass@256 with probability \(1-(1-q)^{256}\): 92% for \(q=10^{-2}\), 2.5% for \(q=10^{-4}\), 0.026% for \(q=10^{-6}\). Problems whose only successful chains have \(q\lesssim10^{-4}\) look "unsolvable by the base model" at \(k=256\), yet RL can raise such a \(q\) by orders of magnitude, which is a real capability gain that a \(k\le256\) test cannot separate from zero. Conversely, on numeric-answer tasks a fraction of large-\(k\) "successes" are lucky guesses, which inflates the base model's curve (Yue et al. audited samples by hand for this reason). Add pretraining confounds (Qwen's data), RL runs that are short and narrow, and contamination.

What would settle it: tasks with verifiably novel skills (ProRL's out-of-distribution puzzle tasks), RL scaled far past hundreds of steps on broad data, and per-problem probability estimates beyond \(k=256\). Until then the defensible answer is "both": RL sharpens reliably, expands under some conditions, and distillation expands more cheaply when a teacher exists.

Test-time scaling, thinking modes and where post-training is going

Two ways to spend inference compute

Test-time scaling 测试时扩展 means buying accuracy with inference tokens. There are two axes. Sequential scaling makes one chain of thought longer (more exploration, verification and backtracking inside a single response). Parallel scaling draws several independent responses and aggregates them by majority vote, or picks with a verifier (see page 06 for sampling and page 18 for pass@k). They complement each other. R1 spells this out: because non-reasoning models "lack the ability to backtrack or self-correct", drawing more independent samples from them just re-samples potentially wrong final solutions, whereas R1's long chains "still sometimes fail to be thorough or become trapped in incorrect logic paths", so sampling several chains does help: on AIME 2024 pass@1 is 79.8, majority vote over 64 samples gives 86.7 and pass@64 is 90.0 [DeepSeek-R1, App. E.4].

Budget forcing (s1). Muennighoff et al. showed how little machinery sequential scaling needs. They fine-tune Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on only 1,000 curated questions with reasoning traces (s1K, distilled from Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking, 26 minutes on 16 H100 GPUs). At decode time budget forcing controls thinking length: to stop early, append the end-of-thinking delimiter (optionally "Final Answer:"); to think longer, suppress the delimiter and append "Wait", which often makes the model re-check and fix an error. On AIME 2024 s1-32B scores 50.0 without forcing and 56.7 with it (MATH-500 92.6 to 93.0, GPQA Diamond 56.6 to 59.6). The gain flattens after about six forced continuations and too many "Wait"s send the model into repetitive loops, and for their setup majority voting on the base model could not catch up with sequential scaling [Muennighoff et al. 2025, §3, §4.2, Table 1]. What this does and does not say about RL: the format of long reasoning and its length control can be elicited by tiny SFT sets; RL is what makes a model use extra tokens productively without an external "Wait" injection.

Thinking budgets and hybrid modes

Products need both a fast answer and a deep one from the same weights, with a cost dial. Three designs are documented:

  • Qwen3: thinking-mode fusion by SFT. After reasoning RL, continue SFT on a mix of thinking data (rejection-sampled from the reasoning-RL model on the cold-start queries) and non-thinking data, with /think and /no_think flags in the prompt and an empty think block for non-thinking samples. A thinking budget then falls out for free: when the chain reaches a user-set length, insert "Considering the limited time by the user, I have to give the solution based on the thinking directly now.\n</think>" and let the model answer. The authors state this ability "is not explicitly trained but emerges naturally", and show smooth accuracy growth with budget up to 32K tokens for Qwen3-235B-A22B [Qwen3, §4.3, §4.7].
  • DeepSeek-V3.1: one model, two chat templates. "One model supports both thinking mode and non-thinking mode by changing the chat template", with different assistant prefixes for each [DeepSeek-V3.1 model card]. The card also says the thinking mode reaches answer quality comparable to R1-0528 while responding faster.
  • DeepSeek-V4: reasoning effort baked into RL. Three modes, Non-think, Think High and Think Max. "For each mode, we apply distinct length penalties and context windows during RL training", producing different reasoning lengths, and Think Max also prepends an instruction to the system prompt. Evaluation context windows are 8K, 128K and 384K tokens respectively [DeepSeek-V4, §5.1.1, §5.3.1].

RL compute as a scaling axis

The R1-era pattern was small RL budgets and large pretraining. That ratio is moving. DeepSeek-V3.2 states that its post-training RL budget "exceeds 10% of the pre-training cost" and that it has seen "consistent performance improvements correlating with extended RL training budget" [DeepSeek-V3.2, §1, §4.1]. For scale: R1's whole RL effort was about 142K H800 GPU-hours against 2.664M for V3's pretraining, about 5.3% (computed above); MiniMax-M1's full RL run was three weeks on 512 H800 GPUs, \(512\times24\times21=258{,}048\) GPU-hours, which at their reported $0.53M implies about $2.05 per GPU-hour, consistent with the $2 rate R1 assumed. Ways RL compute gets spent: more steps (ProRL: over 2,000), longer sequences (Kimi k1.5 scaled the RL context window to 128K; MiniMax staged 40K to 80K), more prompts and domains, and more samples per prompt. Rollouts, not gradient steps, tend to dominate wall-clock time, because long-tail samples stall synchronous batches (DAPO §3.2, MiniMax §3.2); page 25 covers the systems side.

Post-training trends as of September 2026

  • Specialists, then on-policy distillation instead of mixed RL. DeepSeek-V3.2 trained specialists per domain with large-scale RL, distilled their data into the main model, and then ran one mixed RL stage over reasoning, agent and alignment data (to avoid the forgetting of multi-stage pipelines). DeepSeek-V4 keeps the specialists (SFT then GRPO with hyperparameters "closely aligned" with prior work) but replaces the mixed RL stage entirely with multi-teacher on-policy distillation: the unified student samples its own responses and minimizes \(\sum_i w_i\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{E_i})\) against more than ten expert teachers [DeepSeek-V4, §5.1]. The motivation is avoiding "the performance degradation often encountered in traditional weight-merging or mixed RL techniques". They also choose full-vocabulary reverse KL over the common shortcut of using \(\operatorname{sg}[\log\pi_E-\log\pi_\theta]\) at the sampled token as a per-token RL advantage, because the shortcut has high gradient variance and can be unstable.
  • OPD is RL with a dense reward. Written as a policy gradient, on-policy distillation uses the per-token advantage \(\log\pi_{\text{teacher}}-\log\pi_\theta\) in place of one outcome reward per response, which is why it plugs into RL infrastructure and why it is so much cheaper per unit of signal (Qwen3: 1,800 versus 17,920 GPU-hours for the 8B model). Qwen3 already used it for its smaller models. Order matters: the September 2026 study above finds OPD then RL beats fusing the two signals in one step [Li et al. 2026].
  • GRPO survives, with patches. The frontier open recipes are GRPO plus the fixes of the previous section (V3.2's list, DAPO-style shaping, GSPO-style stability for MoE), not a replacement algorithm.
  • Generative reward models for what rules cannot check. V4 uses rubric-guided data and a generative reward model whose weights are the actor's own, with RL applied to the judging skill as well [DeepSeek-V4, §5.1.1].
  • Reasoning persists across tool calls. V3.2 keeps thinking tokens across tool results and drops them at a new user message; V4 keeps all reasoning across user turns when tools are in play (details on page 25 and page 28).

A budget calculation. Suppose 256 prompts \(\times\) 8 responses averaging 3,000 tokens: \(256\times8\times3000=6.14\)M generated tokens per step. Common misconceptions

  • "GRPO has no baseline." It has one: the mean reward of the group. Algebraically the group-mean advantage equals the leave-one-out (RLOO) advantage times \((G-1)/G\). What GRPO lacks is a learned per-token value function, and with it any token-level credit assignment.
  • "The KL term goes into the reward, as in RLHF." In GRPO it is subtracted in the loss, inside the token sum, as the k3 estimator. Also remember which KL it regularizes: differentiated on-policy with samples held fixed, k3's expected gradient is that of the forward KL, unless corrected by the importance ratio as in DeepSeek-V3.2.
  • "The advantage says which tokens were good." Every token of a response shares one scalar. Nothing in the objective distinguishes the decisive token from filler; the model learns which is which only because the statistics differ across many samples.
  • "Clipping keeps the policy close to the reference." The clip acts relative to \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) within one rollout. Closeness to the reference is the KL term's job. With one update per rollout, as in DeepSeekMath, \(\rho=1\) and the clip never fires.
  • "Zero-advantage groups are harmless." They add no gradient but occupy batch slots and, under token-mean normalization, still enter the denominator. Their share grows as the policy improves (for \(p=0.9\), \(G=8\): 43% of groups), which is why DAPO filters them.
  • "A growing response length means better reasoning." Length also grows from the \(1/|o_i|\) bias (wrong answers get cheap verbosity), from a raised length cap (R1-Zero's jump at step 8.2K), and from loops. Only length together with validation accuracy is informative.
  • "The aha moment proves RL invents reflection." The frequency change of "wait" is measured, but base models, including DeepSeek-V3-Base, already produce "wait" and "aha", and in R1-Zero reflective responses were often no more accurate. RL amplifies; the causal story is open.
  • "Higher pass@1 means the model learned to solve new problems." Usually it means it solves the same problems more reliably. Check pass@\(k\) at large \(k\), and remember that random rewards lift Qwen2.5-Math-7B's MATH-500 accuracy by 21 points.

Interview questions

Warm-up What is RLVR, and how does it differ from RLHF?

RLHF optimizes a learned reward model trained on human preference pairs. RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, named in Tülu 3) replaces it with a program: extract and normalize the final answer and compare it with a reference, run unit tests, check a format constraint. The reward is usually binary. Consequences: there is no reward model to train or exploit (R1 explicitly avoids neural reward models on reasoning tasks because they get hacked at scale); the reward is exact but exists only for checkable domains; it is sparse, one bit per trajectory of thousands of tokens; and sampling many responses per prompt is the natural way to get a baseline. It is not immune to hacking: the checker is the specification, so parser false positives, weak tests and lucky guesses get optimized. Some recipes drop the KL leash entirely because the reward cannot drift the way a learned proxy does, and because long-chain-of-thought training is expected to move far from the initial model.

Warm-up Why is there no value model in GRPO, and what replaces it?

PPO's critic is a second model the size of the policy and must predict, token by token, the expected final reward. With an outcome-only reward and long, self-revising chains of thought this is hard to learn (R1: content generated early "may later be revised or contradicted"), and it costs memory (a 7B critic is roughly 112 GB of training state). GRPO samples \(G\) responses per prompt and uses their mean reward as the baseline: \(\hat A_i=(r_i-\text{mean})/\text{std}\), the same scalar for every token of response \(i\). It is a valid baseline because \(r_i-\bar r=\frac{G-1}{G}(r_i-\bar r_{-i})\) and \(\bar r_{-i}\) does not depend on \(o_i\), so the gradient is unbiased up to a constant. Costs: \(G\) samples per prompt and no per-token credit. Kimi adds an argument for dropping the critic: a value function would penalize a wrong but recoverable reasoning step, and exploring such steps is exactly what long-CoT training needs.

Warm-up Compute the group advantages for \(G=8\) with 3 correct answers (reward 1) under GRPO, Dr. GRPO and RLOO.

Mean \(=3/8=0.375\). Sample variance \(=\tfrac17[3(0.625)^2+5(0.375)^2]=1.875/7=0.2679\), std \(=0.5175\) (population std 0.4841). GRPO: correct \(0.625/0.5175=+1.208\), wrong \(-0.375/0.5175=-0.725\) (with the population std: \(+1.291\), \(-0.775\)). Dr. GRPO (no std): \(+0.625\) and \(-0.375\). RLOO (subtract the mean of the other 7): a correct response sees baseline \(2/7\), so \(1-2/7=+5/7=+0.714\); a wrong one sees \(3/7\), so \(-3/7=-0.429\). Each set sums to zero over the group. RLOO is Dr. GRPO times \(8/7\); GRPO is Dr. GRPO divided by the std. If all 8 (or none) are correct, every advantage is 0.

Warm-up Why do groups where all samples are correct, or all wrong, contribute nothing? How often does that happen?

If every reward in the group is equal, \(r_i-\text{mean}=0\) for all \(i\), so every advantage is 0 and the surrogate gradient vanishes (a KL term, if present, is the only remaining gradient). The std is also 0, which is why implementations add an \(\epsilon\) to the denominator. Dr. GRPO and RLOO have the same property, so it does not come from the std. The chance that a prompt with pass rate \(p\) yields a useless group is \(p^G+(1-p)^G\): for \(p=0.9\), \(G=8\) it is 43%, for \(G=16\) it is 18.5%, for \(G=64\) it is 0.1%. As training improves the policy, more prompts drift to \(p\to1\), so the fraction rises (DAPO Fig. 3b). Consequences: wasted rollouts, a smaller effective batch, noisier gradients. Remedies: dynamic sampling (drop and refill), offline difficulty filtering, larger \(G\), and continuous or partial-credit rewards that avoid exact ties.

Core Write the GRPO objective. How does it differ from PPO?

For each prompt \(q\), sample \(\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim\pi_{\text{old}}\), compute rewards, \(\hat A_i=(r_i-\text{mean}(r))/\text{std}(r)\), and maximize \[ \E\Big[\tfrac1G\sum_i\tfrac1{|o_i|}\sum_t\Big\{\min\big(\rho_{i,t}\hat A_i,\ \clip(\rho_{i,t},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat A_i\big)-\beta\,\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}]_{i,t}\Big\}\Big],\quad \rho_{i,t}=\tfrac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,\lt t})}, \] with \(\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}=\tfrac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}-\log\tfrac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}-1\) (the k3 estimator). Differences from PPO: (1) the baseline is the group mean, not a learned \(V(s_t)\), so no critic; (2) the advantage is one scalar per response shared by all its tokens, no GAE; (3) the KL to the reference is a loss term, not a per-token reward penalty; (4) it needs \(G\) samples per prompt; (5) the ratio, clip and \(1/|o|\) length normalization are inherited from PPO (DeepSeekMath's PPO formula already has the \(1/|o|\)). In DeepSeek's own comparison, PPO with \(\lambda=0.95\) was much worse than GRPO on MATH and with \(\lambda=1\) came close, so the baseline estimator is the main difference.

Core Where does the KL term go in GRPO, why the k3 estimator, and which KL does its gradient regularize?

It is subtracted from the per-token objective, so it lives in the loss and shares the \(1/|o_i|\) normalizer, rather than being folded into the reward (which would enter the advantage and penalize cumulative KL). With \(u=\log\pi_{\text{ref}}-\log\pi_\theta\) at the sampled token, k3 \(=e^u-u-1\). It is non-negative and low-variance and, for tokens sampled from \(\pi_\theta\), its expectation is \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\). The subtlety is the gradient: differentiating with the samples fixed gives \(-(\pi_{\text{ref}}/\pi_\theta-1)\nabla\log\pi_\theta\), whose expectation is the gradient of the forward KL. DeepSeek-V3.2 multiplies k3 by \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\), which restores the reverse-KL gradient; the k2 estimator does too. In practice many reasoning recipes drop the KL (DAPO, Dr. GRPO, CISPO), R1 used \(\beta=0.001\) with the reference replaced every 400 steps, and V3.2 notes that for math a weak or absent penalty works better. Setting \(\beta=0\) also removes the reference model's memory and forward pass.

Core What biases does GRPO's normalization introduce, and how do Dr. GRPO and DAPO fix them?

Two. Length bias from the per-response \(1/|o_i|\): token \(t\) of response \(i\) has weight \(\hat A_i/(G|o_i|)\), so among correct answers shorter ones get bigger per-token rewards, and among wrong answers longer ones get smaller per-token penalties; wrong answers drift longer for free. Difficulty bias from dividing by the group std: prompts with rare successes or rare failures (small std) are up-weighted, by about 1.5 times relative to 50/50 prompts at \(G=8\). Dr. GRPO removes both: it divides by a constant (for example the maximum generation length) instead of \(|o_i|\), and does not divide by the std. DAPO removes the length term by normalizing over all tokens in the batch (token-mean) but keeps the std. DeepSeek-V3.2 drops the std as well. Dr. GRPO reports that incorrect-response length stops inflating and reaches 43.3% AIME 2024 with a 7B model; DAPO's argument is complementary: under sequence-mean, tokens in long responses are under-weighted, so good long reasoning is learned slowly and long gibberish is penalized too little.

Core What happened in DeepSeek-R1-Zero, and why did R1 need a cold start?

R1-Zero applied GRPO directly to DeepSeek-V3-Base with a rule-based reward (accuracy plus a format reward for <think> tags), no SFT and no neural reward model. AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% (86.7% with self-consistency), response length grew through training, and reflective behavior ("wait", verification, trying alternatives) became more frequent, with the "aha moment" example. Problems: poor readability, language mixing, and weak general ability (IF-Eval 46.6). R1's four stages: (1) cold-start SFT on thousands of readable, first-person long-CoT examples; (2) reasoning RL with a language-consistency reward; (3) rejection sampling from the stage-2 model, about 600K reasoning and 200K other samples, SFT again from V3-Base; (4) a final RL stage with rules for reasoning and reward models for general helpfulness and harmlessness. The cold start is not for reasoning strength: the cold-start checkpoint scores lower on AIME (59.0) than R1-Zero (77.9). It gives a readable, instruction-following starting policy (IF-Eval 71.7), which RL then upgrades. The aha moment shows frequency changes of reflective tokens, not that RL created reflection: base models already produce them.

Core What is entropy collapse in RLVR and how do you prevent it?

Policy entropy falls to near zero within a small fraction of training: samples for a prompt become near-identical, groups become uniform (zero advantage), exploration and the reward plateau. Cui et al. fit \(R=-ae^{H}+b\), so exhausting entropy sets a predictable ceiling. The cause: to first order \(\Delta H\approx-\eta\operatorname{Cov}(\log\pi(a),A(a))\) (natural-gradient case; \(\operatorname{Cov}(\log\pi,\pi A)\) for vanilla PG), so reinforcing high-probability, high-advantage actions sharpens the distribution, and RLVR does that constantly since correct chains are usually the likely ones. Prevention: (1) clip-higher (\(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}=0.28\) versus \(\varepsilon_{\text{low}}=0.2\)) or CISPO so rare positive tokens can keep growing; (2) restrict high-covariance tokens (Clip-Cov, KL-Cov); (3) dynamic sampling and diverse prompts; (4) a KL to a periodically reset reference plus high temperature when starting from an SFT checkpoint (ProRL); (5) monitor entropy against validation accuracy, since too high is also bad. A plain entropy bonus was reported ineffective.

Core Explain clip-higher with numbers, then compute how much extra sampling dynamic sampling needs for prompts with pass rate 0.9 and \(G=16\).

With \(\varepsilon=0.2\) and \(A>0\), a token can raise its probability by at most a factor 1.2 per rollout: a rare "exploration" token at \(\pi_{\text{old}}=0.01\) reaches at most 0.012, while a token at 0.9 could reach 1.08, that is, certainty. The clip therefore limits rare tokens and barely limits likely ones (DAPO measured the mean probability of up-clipped tokens below 0.2). Setting \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}=0.28\) lets the rare token reach 0.0128 and keeps far more of them inside the trust region. \(\varepsilon_{\text{low}}\) stays 0.2, because loosening the lower bound would let negative advantages push rare tokens to probability 0 and collapse the sampling space. Dynamic sampling: the chance a group is useless is \(0.9^{16}+0.1^{16}\approx0.185\), so 81.5% of groups carry signal. To fill a batch of 512 informative groups you need about \(512/0.815\approx628\) prompts, 1.23 times the rollouts (real data is heterogeneous in \(p\), so measure it). At \(G=8\) the useless share is 43% and the multiplier 1.76, but the group costs half as many samples: informative-group cost is \(G/(1-\text{useless})\), 14.0 samples at \(G=8\) versus 19.6 at \(G=16\).

Core What do GSPO and CISPO each change, and what problem motivated each?

GSPO (Qwen) replaces token-level ratios with the length-normalized sequence ratio \(s_i=(\pi_\theta(y_i)/\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i))^{1/|y_i|}\) and clips whole sequences (\(\varepsilon\approx3\text{-}4\times10^{-4}\)). Motivation: a per-token ratio is a single-sample importance weight, high-variance noise that accumulates with length, and in MoE models about 10% of the activated experts change after one update (Qwen3-30B-A3B), making token ratios unreliable. GRPO needed Routing Replay; GSPO does not, because the sequence likelihood is stable. It also tolerates train-inference numerical mismatch. CISPO (MiniMax-M1) keeps token-level updates but clips the importance weight (stop-gradient) instead of zeroing the update: \(\sg(\clip(\rho))\hat A\log\pi_\theta\). Motivation: rare fork tokens ("Wait", "Recheck") get a high ratio after the first update and PPO clipping removes them from later off-policy updates, so long reflection never gets reinforced. CISPO keeps every token's gradient at the cost of a small bias, and matched DAPO's AIME score in half the steps. DeepSeek's alternative for MoE is to keep the sampling-time routes (Keep Routing) and mask far-off-policy negative sequences.

Core Compute the per-token gradient weights of a 100-token and a 1,000-token response under sequence-mean, token-mean and a constant normalizer.

Take a group of \(G=8\), four responses of 100 tokens and four of 1,000 (4,400 tokens), all with \(|\hat A|=1\). The weight multiplying each token's \(\nabla\log\pi\) is: sequence-mean \(1/(G|o_i|)\): \(1/800=1.25\times10^{-3}\) for the short response and \(1/8000=1.25\times10^{-4}\) for the long one, a 10\(\times\) difference per token, with equal total weight 0.125 each. Token-mean \(1/\sum|o_i|=1/4400=2.27\times10^{-4}\) for every token, so the long response has 10\(\times\) the total weight (0.227 versus 0.0227). Constant normalizer with \(L_{\max}=1000\): \(1/(8\cdot1000)=1.25\times10^{-4}\) for every token. The lesson: sequence-mean equalizes responses and makes short responses' tokens count more (length bias); the other two equalize tokens, and only the constant does not depend on batch composition. With a two-response group of 100 and 1,000 tokens the sequence-mean ratio is again 10:1.

Deep-dive Does RL teach models new reasoning abilities? Argue both sides with evidence.

No, it sharpens. DeepSeekMath saw Maj@K improve but not Pass@K. Yue et al. compared base and zero-RL models by pass@\(k\) up to 256 or more and found RL wins at \(k=1\) but the base model overtakes at large \(k\) (about 9 points ahead on Minerva at \(k=128\) for 32B); RL reasoning paths have high likelihood under the base model; six RL algorithms all leave a sampling-efficiency gap above 40 points; distillation, by contrast, extends the boundary. Qwen3-8B: RL raised AIME'24 pass@1 by 12.6 points with pass@64 unchanged, on-policy distillation raised pass@64. Spurious rewards: random rewards lift Qwen2.5-Math-7B's MATH-500 by 21.4 points versus 29.1 for ground truth, via clipping bias amplifying priors. Dr. GRPO: base models already show "aha". Yes, under the right conditions. ProRL trains a distilled 1.5B model for over 2,000 steps on 136K problems across five domains with KL resets and clip-higher and finds success on tasks where the base fails at every \(k\), higher novelty scores, and gains largest where the start is weakest, while noting that math shows narrowing. Reconciling. Pass@256 cannot see a path whose per-sample probability is \(10^{-6}\) (found with probability 0.026%), yet RL can amplify it enormously; numeric answers inflate pass@\(k\) via guesses; published RL runs are short and math-heavy; Qwen models are atypical. My answer: RL reliably converts latent ability into pass@1, may expand with long, broad, entropy-preserving training, and distillation expands most cheaply when a teacher exists.

Deep-dive Your run's training reward keeps rising, but held-out accuracy has fallen and response length has exploded. How do you diagnose it?

Separate three hypotheses and test each cheaply. (1) Checker exploitation or overfitting: read 50 high-reward samples. Look for answer-first outputs with no reasoning, multiple boxed answers where the parser takes the wrong one, guessing on multiple choice, or copying the prompt's numbers. Fix the verifier and add a held-out check on a different distribution. DAPO found training reward poorly correlated with validation accuracy, and R1 saw reward rise while Codeforces performance fell with a learned reward model. (2) Length bias or loops: if the loss is per-response length-normalized, wrong answers grow for free; compare mean length of correct versus incorrect samples (Dr. GRPO's diagnostic), look at the truncation rate and repetition, and check whether the length cap was raised. Switch to token-mean or a constant normalizer, add soft overlong shaping, repetition-based early stopping. (3) Off-policy or numerical instability: check the clipped fraction, the log-prob mismatch between sampler and trainer at the first minibatch, KL if used, gradient-norm spikes, and entropy spikes; reduce minibatches per rollout or the learning rate, mask far-off-policy negative sequences, fix precision (MiniMax's FP32 LM head), and for MoE keep the sampling routes. Also check that entropy has not collapsed (uniform groups) and that the zero-advantage fraction is not rising. A checkpoint sweep of held-out accuracy tells you where it broke; roll back to before that step with the fix applied.

Deep-dive Compare the compute of R1's RL with V3's pretraining, say how that changed in V3.2, and explain when you would distill instead of running RL.

R1-Zero used 512 H800 GPUs for about 198 hours, \(512\times198\approx101\)K GPU-hours; R1 about 41K more; SFT data creation 5K; total 147K, about $294K at $2 per GPU-hour. V3's pretraining used 2.664M H800 GPU-hours, so R1's RL was about \(142\text{K}/2664\text{K}\approx5.3\%\). DeepSeek-V3.2 reports an RL budget above 10% of its pretraining cost and steady gains with more RL, so RL compute is now a first-class scaling axis. Distill when a stronger teacher exists and you want a small model quickly: R1's 800K-sample SFT of Qwen2.5-32B reached 72.6 AIME pass@1 versus 47.0 for RL alone at more than 10K steps, and Qwen3's on-policy distillation of the 8B model beat RL on every benchmark at 1,800 versus 17,920 GPU-hours (74.4 versus 67.6 on AIME'24). Distillation can also extend pass@\(k\) coverage, while RL mostly sharpens. Run RL when there is no teacher (you are the frontier), the base model is large enough to explore (R1's 7B and 16B pure-RL attempts failed), the domain is verifiable, and you want to exceed the teacher's ceiling. The strongest recent pattern combines them: specialists trained with RL, merged by on-policy distillation (V4), or OPD followed by RL (a September 2026 study). Caveats: distillation needs teacher traces that RL had to produce first, and the RL baselines above were the early recipes; DAPO-style fixes narrow but did not close the gap on AIME (50 versus 72.6, with different evaluation protocols).

Hands-on

GRPO and its successors, at the loss level. Implement five small functions: group_advantages (GRPO with sample std, Dr. GRPO, and leave-one-out RLOO; uniform groups must give exactly 0, never NaN); grpo_loss (per-token min-clip surrogate with decoupled \(\varepsilon_{\text{low}}\) and \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}\), the k3 KL term in the loss, and three aggregations: sequence-mean, token-mean, and the Dr. GRPO constant normalizer; padding must not leak into the value or the gradient); gspo_ratio and gspo_loss (the length-normalized sequence ratio and whole-sequence clipping); and dynamic_sampling_filter. The tests check hand-computed advantages (the 3-of-8 example of this page), the four clip quadrants and the clip-higher asymmetry, the per-token gradient weights of a 100-token versus a 1,000-token response, the k3 values, the geometric-mean identity, and that a uniform-reward group receives exactly zero gradient. An interviewer would expect group_advantages and grpo_loss in about 20 minutes and the remaining three in 10 to 15 more. exercises/drills/grpo.py

Run pytest exercises/tests/test_grpo.py; add --solutions to check the reference implementation. Extensions to try on your own: add a CISPO loss and check its gradient weight against the table in the fixes section; add Kimi's length reward; implement the V3.2 importance-corrected k3 and verify numerically that its expected gradient matches the reverse KL.

Cheat sheet

  • RLVR: reward from a program (answer match, unit tests, format), usually binary; term from Tülu 3. No reward model to hack, but the checker is the spec. Only checkable domains; one bit per trajectory.
  • GRPO (DeepSeekMath Eq. 3): \(\hat A_i=(r_i-\text{mean})/\text{std}\), shared by all tokens; \(\frac1G\sum_i\frac1{|o_i|}\sum_t\{\min(\rho\hat A,\clip(\rho,1\pm\varepsilon)\hat A)-\beta\,\text{k3}\}\); k3 \(=e^u-u-1\), \(u=\log\pi_{\text{ref}}-\log\pi_\theta\). KL in the loss, not the reward. No critic. Group-mean advantage \(=\frac{G-1}{G}\times\) RLOO.
  • Worked case (\(G=8\), 3 correct): GRPO \(+1.208/-0.725\) (sample std 0.5175), Dr. GRPO \(+0.625/-0.375\), RLOO \(+0.714/-0.429\). All-correct or all-wrong: 0.
  • Biases: \(1/|o_i|\) gives short responses' tokens 10\(\times\) the weight at 100 vs 1,000 tokens (1/800 vs 1/8000 at \(G=8\)); std up-weights extreme prompts about 1.5\(\times\). Dr. GRPO: constant normalizer, no std. DAPO: token-mean, keeps std. V3.2: no std.
  • Useless-group probability: \(p^G+(1-p)^G\); \(p=0.9\): 43% at \(G=8\), 18.5% at \(G=16\). Cost per informative group \(G/(1-p^G-(1-p)^G)\).
  • DAPO: \(\varepsilon_{\text{low}}=0.2\), \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}}=0.28\) (clip-higher); dynamic sampling; token-level loss; overlong shaping (\(L_{\max}=16{,}384\) + 4,096 buffer); no KL. AIME'24 30 to 50 (Qwen2.5-32B).
  • GSPO: \(s_i=(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}})^{1/|y_i|}\), clip \(3\text{e-}4/4\text{e-}4\); about 10% of experts change per update in Qwen3-30B-A3B, so GRPO needed Routing Replay. CISPO: \(\sg(\clip(\rho))\hat A\log\pi\), all tokens keep gradients, matches DAPO in 50% of steps.
  • V3.2 GRPO: \(\hat A=R-\text{mean}\); k3 times \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\); mask negative sequences that drift; Keep Routing; Keep Sampling Mask; length penalty; RL budget above 10% of pretraining cost.
  • R1: R1-Zero AIME'24 15.6 to 77.9 (v1: 71.0), 86.7 with voting; four stages (cold-start SFT, reasoning RL + language reward, ~600K + ~200K rejection-sampled SFT from V3-Base, final RL). Cold start buys readability, not reasoning (AIME 59.0 after it). RL cost 147K H800 GPU-hours (\(\approx\$294\)K), about 5.3% of V3 pretraining. Distilling Qwen2.5-32B: 72.6 AIME vs 47.0 for RL alone.
  • Entropy: \(R=-ae^{H}+b\); \(\Delta H\approx-\eta\operatorname{Cov}(\log\pi,A)\). Fixes: clip-higher, CISPO, Clip-Cov and KL-Cov, dynamic sampling, KL with reference resets.
  • Debate: Maj@K up but Pass@K flat (DeepSeekMath); base overtakes RL at large \(k\) (Yue); ProRL (2K+ steps) expands where the base is weak; random rewards give +21.4 MATH-500 points on Qwen2.5-Math-7B; distillation expands boundary. Pass@256 cannot see paths with \(q\lesssim10^{-4}\).
  • Test-time scaling: s1 budget forcing 50.0 to 56.7 AIME'24; Qwen3 thinking budget; V3.1 template switch; V4 Non-think, Think High, Think Max via distinct RL length penalties. V4: GRPO specialists, then on-policy distillation from more than 10 teachers instead of mixed RL.
  • Recipe: learning rate \(10^{-6}\), \(G=8\) to 16, \(\varepsilon\) 0.2/0.28, \(\beta=0\), dynamic sampling or pass-rate pre-filter, 4K to 8K cap; monitor validation vs train reward, zero-advantage fraction, length, entropy, clip fraction and train-sampler mismatch.
