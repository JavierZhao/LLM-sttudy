RL systems & agentic RL

P1 ~50 min Prereqs: 17 Inference & serving, 22 RL foundations, 24 RLVR & reasoning

Large-scale reinforcement learning 强化学习 for language models is mostly a systems problem. Generating rollouts costs more GPU time than learning from them, the engine that samples is not numerically the engine that trains, and agents add slow environments on top. This page covers the anatomy of an RL step, how colocated, disaggregated and asynchronous systems trade idle GPUs against staleness, why training-inference mismatch silently corrupts the importance ratio and how it is fixed, and how multi-turn tool-use training is set up: loss masks, environments, rewards without verifiers, reward hacking, and what to monitor.

What interviewers probe
  • Back-of-envelope for an RL step: tokens generated, GPU-hours for generation versus training, and how to split GPUs between the two. "Why is rollout the bottleneck?" needs an answer with numbers, not adjectives.
  • System design trade-offs: colocated versus disaggregated, synchronous versus asynchronous, partial rollouts, what staleness costs and how it is bounded.
  • Training-inference mismatch: why the same weights give different log-probs in vLLM and in the trainer, why the PPO clip does not fix it, truncated importance sampling, sequence-level versus token-level ratios, MoE routing.
  • Agentic RL setup: what is masked out of the loss, why token IDs must not be re-tokenized, trajectory-level versus turn-level credit, environments and sandboxes, context management.
  • Rewards and failure modes: rubric and generative rewards for tasks without a verifier, reward hacking in coding agents, and which run metrics catch trouble early.

Why generation dominates

Count FLOPs first. Generation costs \(2N\) FLOPs per token (forward only). Training costs \(6N\) per token (forward plus backward), and with the old-policy and reference log-probability passes about \(10N\) per trained token. On FLOPs alone, training looks five times more expensive. But FLOPs are not time. Decoding one token per sequence per step is memory-bound (page 06, page 07): every step streams all the weights plus each sequence's KV cache from HBM, and at 8K-token responses the KV cache is the larger of the two. So the engine runs at a few percent of peak FLOP/s while the trainer runs at roughly 40% MFU (Llama 3 reports 38 to 43% on H100s, page 15). Per token, generation ends up costing as much GPU time as training or more, and RL trains on every token it generates. The worked numbers below make this concrete.

Three structural effects push the wall-clock share above the GPU-second share.

  • The length distribution has a heavy tail 长尾. Seer measured mean output lengths of 7.6K (Qwen2-VL-72B), 22.4K (Moonlight) and 39.0K tokens (Kimi-K2), with single responses up to 96K. In a synchronous rollout, the last 10% of requests took up to 50% of the rollout time, because the batch shrinks while a few long sequences finish and the GPUs sit mostly idle [Qin et al. 2025, §2, §4.2.2]. Magistral reports that the longest completions take up to 5 times longer than the shortest [Magistral, §3].
  • Latency does not shard. Decoding is bandwidth-bound, so extra GPUs raise throughput only by adding engine replicas. They do not shorten the time a single 30K-token response needs [ROLL Flash, §1].
  • Lengths grow during training. RL pushes response length up (Kimi k1.5 adds a length penalty and K2 a per-task token budget for this reason [k1.5, §2.3.3], [K2, §3.2.3]), so the rollout share of a run drifts upward.

Three lessons follow. (1) Even with a well-tuned engine, generation is about two thirds of the GPU-seconds, and per token it costs twice as much as training despite five times fewer FLOPs: 4% versus 40% MFU. (2) The 7-minute figure assumes perfect balance. With the heavy tail, the step waits for stragglers, which is where systems work goes (next section). (3) In a disaggregated system the step time is \(\max(W_g/N_r,\; W_t/N_t)\), where \(W_g, W_t\) are the generation and training GPU-seconds and \(N_r + N_t = N\). That is minimized when both terms are equal, so the rollout pool should get the fraction \(W_g/(W_g + W_t)\) of the GPUs: \(0.65 \times 64 \approx 42\) rollout GPUs and 22 trainer GPUs here. AReaL's runs used a 75/25 split, chosen empirically over 50/50 [AReaL, §6.1], which is consistent with a generation share between 65% and 75%.

Key idea

RL for LLMs spends most of its GPU time on memory-bound decoding, and the wall-clock is set by the longest sequence, not the average one. Every design on this page either fills the idle slots (asynchrony, queue scheduling, partial rollouts), makes decode faster (speculative decoding, low-precision rollouts), or hides the non-GPU work (rewards, environments, weight sync).

System designs: placement, synchrony and weight sync

Two independent choices define an RL system. Placement: do the inference engine and the trainer share GPUs (colocated) or own separate pools (disaggregated)? Synchrony: does the trainer wait for a full on-policy batch (synchronous), or consume samples as they arrive from engines that keep generating (asynchronous)? Figure 2 shows the resulting timelines.

Colocated placement

In a colocated design 训推一体 the trainer and the engine take turns on the same GPUs, and whichever is idle releases its memory. Kimi k1.5 runs Megatron and vLLM as sidecar containers in one pod. After training, Megatron offloads its GPU memory and hands over the weights (RDMA, via Mooncake); vLLM starts from dummy weights, loads them, and is terminated after rollout so Megatron can reload. Training to inference takes under a minute and the reverse about ten seconds. vLLM is killed rather than offloaded because CUDA graphs, NCCL buffers and the driver make its memory hard to release [k1.5, §2.6.3]. Kimi K2 keeps the design at 1T parameters: training parameters sit in DRAM during rollout, and a checkpoint engine broadcasts the full parameter set so each inference worker pulls its own shard, in under 30 seconds. They deliberately send several times more data than a minimal transfer, to keep the two engines decoupled and easy to maintain [K2, §3.3.1 to §3.3.2].

HybridFlow's 3D-HybridEngine attacks the same switch. Training and generation use different parallel layouts, resharded with zero memory redundancy, which cut transition time by 55.2% on average (up to 89.1% at 70B). A smaller generation tensor-parallel size than training's also cut generation latency by 60.3% (7B) and 36.4% (13B) [HybridFlow, §8.4]. Its placement study gives a rule of thumb: colocating everything was best on 16 to 64 GPUs, while split or standalone placements won at 96 to 128 GPUs for 13B and 34B models, because a fixed global batch leaves each worker of a large colocated pool less to do [§8.3]. That study used short fixed-length PPO responses, so treat the crossover as illustrative.

Disaggregated placement

In a disaggregated design 训推分离 the engines and the trainer own separate pools. OpenRLHF's original architecture is the reference: Ray schedules vLLM rollout engines and DeepSpeed ZeRO training engines on different GPUs, so the actor exists twice and weights are broadcast after each update [OpenRLHF, §3.1], [HybridFlow, Tab. 1]. AReaL puts SGLang rollout workers and Megatron trainer workers on disjoint devices, three quarters of them for inference [AReaL, §6.1]. The benefits are no offload or restart per step, independent parallelism and scaling per side, and the possibility of overlap. The costs are idle GPUs whenever the pools are not both busy (the split derivation in the worked numbers), and a fixed partition that drifts out of balance as responses grow.

Weight synchronization

Weight sync 权重同步 moves every parameter from the trainer's layout (FSDP shards, or Megatron TP, PP and EP shards) to the engine's (tensor parallelism inside a replica, many replicas). Reported options: a GPU-to-GPU NCCL broadcast (Magistral: under 5 seconds per update [§3]); a checkpoint engine with a pipelined transfer (K2 overlaps host-to-device copy, broadcast and reload, but on H800s the first two saturated the shared PCIe fabric, so it settled on a two-stage pipeline [App. G]); RDMA (k1.5), peer-to-peer RDMA or CUDA IPC (Miles [§1.1]) and delta updates (slime [README]).

A second question is what happens to requests in flight when weights change. AReaL interrupts them, discards the KV cache computed under the old weights, recomputes it under the new ones, and continues [AReaL, §3.1]. Magistral keeps the sequences and their slightly outdated KV cache, and reports that recomputing was not necessary [§3]. Miles offers abort, leave-in-place and retract (roll back and resume on the new weights), with retract as the default [§4.4].

Synchronous, one-step-off and fully asynchronous training

A synchronous system generates a batch with the current weights, trains, syncs, and repeats. The data is on-policy up to the numerical mismatch of the next section. Overlapping one step (train on batch \(k\) while generating \(k{+}1\) with older weights) removes some idle time but still waits for the longest response in each batch [AReaL, §1]. Fully asynchronous training 异步训练 removes the barrier. AReaL's design has three parts:

  1. Interruptible rollout workers that stream trajectories and accept weight updates mid-generation (above), worth 12% and 17% more generation throughput at 1.5B and 7B, plus dynamic micro-batch packing (about 30% more training throughput) [§6.5].
  2. A staleness bound. With policy version \(i\), trainer batch size \(B\), \(N_r\) trajectories generated so far (counting the request being submitted) and maximum staleness \(\eta\), a new request is admitted only if \[ \lfloor (N_r - 1)/B \rfloor \le i + \eta . \] Older trajectories are prioritized when forming a batch, and \(\eta = 0\) recovers synchronous RL [AReaL, Eq. 3]. The rule caps the samples generated but not yet trained at \((\eta + 1) B\): the first \(iB\) were consumed by earlier steps and the counter may reach \((i + \eta + 1)B\) (my derivation; ROLL Flash's per-sample "asynchronous ratio" gives the same bound, \((1 + \alpha) \times\) batch size [§4.3]). For our \(B = 8192\) and \(\eta = 4\), at most 40,960 outstanding samples.
  3. A decoupled PPO objective that separates the behavior policy \(\pi_{\text{behav}}\) (whatever generated the tokens, possibly several versions) from a proximal policy \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\) (the weights just before this update), the trust-region center: \[ J(\theta) = \E\Big[ \tfrac{\pi_{\text{prox}}}{\pi_{\text{behav}}} \min\big( u^{\text{prox}}_t \hat A_t,\ \clip(u^{\text{prox}}_t, 1{-}\epsilon, 1{+}\epsilon)\, \hat A_t \big) \Big], \quad u^{\text{prox}}_t = \tfrac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{prox}}} \] Clipping around the stale behavior policy would pull the update back toward old, worse policies. \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\) is computed by recomputing token probabilities when a global batch arrives [AReaL, §4.2].

The staleness ablation is the part to remember. On a 1.5B math run, naive PPO with \(\eta = 4\) scored 23.3 on AIME24 against 42.0 for the synchronous oracle, while the decoupled objective scored 42.2 at the same \(\eta\). Synchronous systems are not free of staleness either: splitting a rollout batch into several minibatch updates makes later minibatches stale, and Magistral found quality degrades beyond 2 minibatches per batch [§6.3].

Asynchronous RL with bounded staleness
Problem
A synchronous step waits for the longest response, so GPUs idle at the tail, and adding GPUs does not shorten a single long decode.
Idea
Run rollout and training on separate pools. Rollout workers stream trajectories into a buffer, the trainer consumes a batch as soon as one is ready and pushes weights, and an admission rule caps how stale a sample can be.
Why it works
The tail is hidden behind training, and at most \((\eta+1)B\) samples are ever outstanding, so data is at most about \(\eta\) versions old. The decoupled objective separates "how stale is this data" from "how far may the policy move".
Evidence
AReaL about 1.5× to 2.8× faster end to end with comparable accuracy (Tab. 1) and a clear \(\eta\) ablation (Tab. 2) [AReaL]. ROLL Flash up to 2.24× (RLVR) and 2.72× (agentic) [ROLL Flash].
Cost
Accuracy degrades if staleness is unbounded. It needs extra log-probability recomputation, awkward when a partial rollout spans versions and the old logits no longer exist [Guan et al. 2026]. Short samples arrive first, which can skew lengths [Seer, §1]. Runs are harder to reproduce.
Used by
AReaL, Magistral, ROLL Flash, and the fully asynchronous modes of slime, Miles and verl (still under verl/experimental in its README as of September 2026). Kimi k1.5 and K2 stay iteration-synchronous and use partial rollouts.

Partial rollouts, and why length bias matters

Kimi's partial rollout is a middle path. Each iteration has a fixed output-token budget. A trajectory that exceeds it is saved to a replay buffer and continued in the next iteration, so no single long trajectory monopolizes the system. Only the current segment needs fresh on-policy computation, earlier segments are reused, and some can be excluded from the loss [k1.5, §2.6.2]. K2 uses it so that long-tail agentic tasks are paused and resumed in the next iteration [K2, §3.3.4].

The subtlety is length bias: short responses finish first, so any scheme that stops early or restarts stragglers changes which lengths reach the trainer. Seer found that an over-issue variant of partial rollout gave a significantly lower share of long outputs than synchronous rollout [Seer, §4.4.3], and Magistral lists "no bias on sequence lengths" as a hard constraint [§3]. DeepSeek-V4 is the sharpest. Its cluster can preempt any task at any time, so it logs every generated token to a token-granular write-ahead log and resumes from the log and the saved KV cache. Regenerating an interrupted request from scratch would be "mathematically incorrect", because shorter responses are more likely to survive an interruption and the model would drift toward shorter outputs. (With a batch-invariant, deterministic stack, regenerating with the same seed would also work but repeats the decoding.) [DeepSeek-V4, §5.2.3]. In one line: discarding and regenerating conditions the delivered sample on "finished before the interruption", and that condition depends on length.

Faster rollouts without changing the algorithm

  • Tail-aware scheduling. Seer stays strictly on-policy yet reports 44% to 104% more rollout throughput than veRL and 72% to 94% less tail latency. It splits requests into chunks that can migrate between engines, uses one early "probe" response per GRPO group to predict the group's length (siblings have similar lengths) and schedules longest-first, and drafts speculative tokens from the group's other responses (cumulative 1.42×, 1.56×, 2.04× on Qwen2-VL-72B) [Seer, Tab. 4]. ROLL Flash queues every response as an independent task and expands "n samples per prompt" into n tasks so siblings land on different GPUs (149 s to 81 s for 64 prompts × 16 samples) [§5.1].
  • Cheaper decode. DeepSeek-V4 rolls out with native FP4 weights so sampling matches deployment [§5.2.1]. Quantized rollouts speed decode but widen the training-inference gap (next section).
  • Prefix reuse for multi-turn. Each turn repeats almost all earlier context, so route a session to the engine that holds its prefix; Miles reports a 96% prefix-cache hit rate with affinity routing [§9.2].

Off-policy problems: training-inference mismatch

Even a perfectly synchronous system is slightly off-policy, because the sampler is not the trainer. The inference engine and the trainer hold the same weights, yet they compute the token probabilities with different kernels, reduction orders, precisions and sometimes different sampling rules. The result is a training-inference mismatch 训推不一致, and it is one of the most common causes of RL runs that look fine for a hundred steps and then collapse.

The importance ratio has two factors

Let \(\mu\) denote the inference engine's policy and \(\pi\) the trainer's. A token \(y_t\) was sampled from \(\mu_{\text{old}}\), the engine at the weights used for generation, but we want to optimize \(\pi_\theta\). The importance sampling 重要性采样 ratio factors as [Zheng et al. 2025b, Eq. 5]:

\[ \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid \cdot)}{\mu_{\text{old}}(y_t \mid \cdot)} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t \mid \cdot)}{\mu_{\text{old}}(y_t \mid \cdot)}}_{\text{training-inference discrepancy}} \;\times\; \underbrace{\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid \cdot)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t \mid \cdot)}}_{\text{policy staleness}} \]
\(\pi_{\text{old}}\): the trainer's log-probabilities for the same weights the engine used. The standard PPO and GRPO loss keeps only the second factor.

Standard PPO and GRPO code recomputes \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) on the trainer, uses it as the denominator, and never looks at the engine's own \(\mu_{\text{old}}\) log-probabilities. With one gradient step per batch, \(\pi_\theta = \pi_{\text{old}}\), the ratio is exactly 1, the clip never engages, and the loss cannot see that the samples came from a slightly different distribution. AReaL's decoupled objective has the same structure, with \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\) as \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) [Guan et al. 2026, §1]. The clip cannot stand in for a correction: it is an advantage-sign-dependent filter that limits update size, whereas repairing a discrepancy needs a symmetric constraint centered on 1 [Guan et al. 2026, §4.1]. In Yao et al.'s INT8 experiments, folding the engine-to-trainer ratio into the clip ("PPO-IS") or using an untruncated ratio ("Vanilla-IS") both reached near-zero accuracy [Fig. 3].

Where the mismatch comes from

  • Kernels and reduction order. Floating-point addition is not associative, so split-KV attention, split-\(k\) matmuls, fused kernels and different tensor-parallel degrees change the last bits, and the last bits change sampled-token probabilities. Even inside one engine, results depend on batch composition (page 06). The trainer is not exactly repeatable either: two Megatron forward passes over the same sequences of a 30B MoE model gave a per-token KL of \(8.4 \times 10^{-4}\) [Ma et al. 2025, §3.3].
  • Precision. Quantized rollouts (FP8, INT8, FP4) make decoding cheaper and the gap larger [Yao et al. 2025, §4.2]. The Qwen study deliberately uses FP8 inference with BF16 training as a stress test [Zheng et al. 2025b, §4.2].
  • The LM head. MiniMax-M1 traced its mismatch, which prevented reward growth, to high-magnitude activations at the output head. Computing the head in FP32 raised the Pearson correlation between training and inference token probabilities from 0.987 to 0.997 (Fig. 3) [MiniMax-M1, §3.2].
  • Sampling adjustments. Temperature, top-\(p\) and top-\(k\) change the distribution actually sampled, and engines often return adjusted log-probabilities. The trainer scores the full softmax, so the two policies live on different action sets, which violates importance sampling [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3.1].
  • Tokenization drift in multi-turn rollouts: the trainer scores tokens that were never sampled (next section).
  • MoE routing. A tiny numeric difference flips the top-\(k\) expert choice, so the two engines run different parameters for the same token (below).

The gap is not exotic. In a dense-model RL setup, Yao et al. found tokens for which vLLM assigned probability 1 and the FSDP trainer assigned 0, and patching vLLM to return the true sampling probabilities and to compute the LM head in FP32 did not remove the mismatch [Yao et al. 2025, Fig. 1, §3.1]. In SGLang-versus-Megatron comparisons on math responses (about 20M tokens for the MoE), the per-token \(k_3\) KL was \(1.5 \times 10^{-3}\) for Qwen3-30B-A3B and \(6.4 \times 10^{-4}\) for the dense Qwen3-8B [Ma et al. 2025, §3.1]. A 744B agentic MoE run reports a mean divergence of 0.0369 by its own metric [Miles, §9.2].

Three observations. Fixes, from cheapest to most drastic

1. Correct with importance sampling. Truncated importance sampling (TIS) multiplies each token's policy-gradient term by the detached, capped engine-to-trainer ratio. The formulation from Yao et al. is

\[ \E_{a \sim \pi_{\text{vllm}}(\theta_{\text{old}})}\Big[ \min\!\Big( \tfrac{\pi_{\text{fsdp}}(a,\theta_{\text{old}})}{\pi_{\text{vllm}}(a,\theta_{\text{old}})},\, C \Big) \cdot \nabla_\theta \min\!\Big( \tfrac{\pi_{\text{fsdp}}(a,\theta)}{\pi_{\text{fsdp}}(a,\theta_{\text{old}})} \hat A,\ \clip(\cdot, 1{-}\epsilon, 1{+}\epsilon)\, \hat A \Big) \Big] \]

[Yao et al. 2025, §3.3]. It is the decoupled-PPO structure again, with the engine as the behavior policy and a one-sided cap \(C\). Why token-level weights are principled: the sequence-level objective weights each response by \(\prod_t (1 + \delta_t)\), whose first-order expansion is \(1 + \sum_t \delta_t\), which is exactly what a sum of per-token ratios gives [Zheng et al. 2025b, §2.3]. The same paper shows the weight is not optional: with a 30B MoE trained on-policy (FP8 inference, BF16 training), removing the training-inference correction caused rapid collapse and an entropy crash, and adding length normalization to the loss was stable but worse, because it breaks the first-order argument (§4.3).

import math, torch

def rl_loss(logp, logp_old, logp_rollout, adv, mask, eps_low=0.2, eps_high=0.27, cap=5.0):
    # all (B, T). logp: trainer log-probs, requires grad. logp_old: trainer log-probs at the start of
    # the step (pi_prox), no grad. logp_rollout: what the inference engine reported for the sampled tokens.
    w_ti  = torch.exp((logp_old - logp_rollout).clamp(max=math.log(cap)))   # capped engine-to-trainer weight
    ratio = torch.exp(logp - logp_old)                                      # staleness / update-size ratio
    surr  = torch.minimum(ratio * adv, ratio.clamp(1 - eps_low, 1 + eps_high) * adv)
    return -(w_ti.detach() * surr * mask).sum() / mask.sum()               # mean over unmasked tokens only
Truncated importance sampling (TIS)
Problem
The engine and trainer disagree, so on-policy RL is secretly off-policy, and the PPO clip cannot see it.
Idea
Weight each token by \(\min(\pi_{\text{train}}/\mu_{\text{rollout}}, C)\), stop-gradient, on top of the usual clipped surrogate.
Why it works
Per-token weights are the first-order approximation of the sequence-level correction, and the cap bounds variance at the price of a small bias. The one-sided cap lets the ratio fall freely below 1.
Evidence
DAPO-style training of Qwen2.5-32B improved on AIME24 with TIS [Yao et al. 2025, Fig. 1]. With INT8 rollouts, uncorrected training degraded sharply (Fig. 2), the PPO-IS and untruncated variants fell to near-zero accuracy (Fig. 3), and TIS brought performance close to bf16 rollouts. In a 30B MoE study, dropping the correction collapsed training [Zheng et al. 2025b, §4.3].
Cost
Needs the trainer's log-probs for every sampled token and the engine's log-probs returned with the samples. The cap \(C\) is a hyperparameter (2 in the R3 baselines, 5 in the Qwen study, 1.5 to 5 suggested in verl's docs). For MoE it delayed but did not prevent collapse in one R3 setting (crash at step 105 instead of 60) [Ma et al. 2025, Tab. 1].
Used by
Miles' GLM-5.2 agentic run, verl's rollout-correction options, the Qwen stability study. Related: MiniMax's CISPO clips the importance weight instead of dropping clipped tokens [MiniMax-M1, §3.1].

2. Use sequence-level tools. GSPO defines the ratio \(s_i = \big(\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)/\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)\big)^{1/|y_i|}\), clips whole responses, and weights all tokens of a response equally. The Qwen team argues the sequence likelihood is far more tolerant of numerical noise than any single token's, so the engine's likelihoods could be used directly instead of recomputing them on the trainer, which helps partial rollouts, multi-turn RL and disaggregated setups [GSPO, §5.4]. DeepSeek-V3.2 instead masks: it drops sequences with negative advantage whose mean log-ratio \(\log(\pi_{\text{old}}/\pi_\theta)\) exceeds a threshold \(\delta\), where \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) is the probability returned by the inference framework, so one mask covers both minibatch staleness and engine mismatch. Only negative-advantage sequences are masked, on the argument that the model learns most from its own mistakes while highly off-policy negative samples can mislead it [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3.1]. A widely read blog by Liu et al. argues for sequence-level correction and masking on the same grounds [Liu et al. 2025].

3. Make the engines agree. Each source has a targeted fix: an FP32 LM head (MiniMax-M1), returning true sampling probabilities and keeping the top-\(p\)/top-\(k\) masks so both policies share an action set ("Keep Sampling Mask" in DeepSeek-V3.2), and, at the extreme, bitwise identity.

  • Batch-invariant, deterministic kernels. DeepSeek-V4's stated goal is bitwise alignment among pretraining, post-training and inference. Batch invariance means a token's output is bitwise identical wherever it sits in a batch. To get it, attention cannot use split-KV, so V4 uses a dual-kernel decode scheme with one fixed accumulation order; matmuls move from cuBLAS to DeepGEMM and drop split-\(k\); backward passes replace atomicAdd with per-SM buffers and a deterministic sum. It reports negligible overhead for batch-invariant decoding attention and motivates the kernels by debugging, stability analysis and consistent post-training behavior [DeepSeek-V4, §3.3]. It is a systems-level fix for the same problem TIS treats algorithmically (see also page 15).
  • True-on-policy mode. Miles pins both sides to the same FlashAttention-3 kernel, batch-invariant matmuls and deterministic settings, makes tensor-parallel reductions invariant to the parallel degree, and has the engine re-score each finished sequence with a prefill pass instead of reporting decode-time log-probabilities. In supported configurations the two engines then agree exactly (absolute difference zero). It costs throughput, since rollout gets slower [Miles, §5.3]. The trade is exactness against speed; with exact agreement the first ratio factor is identically 1 and no correction is needed.

MoE routing mismatch

In a mixture-of-experts model (page 08) a small numeric difference can flip which experts a token uses, so the engine and trainer run different parameters for the same token. Ma et al. measured that about 10% of router decisions differ between SGLang and Megatron on Qwen3-30B-A3B, and 94% of tokens had a different expert in at least one layer [Ma et al. 2025, §3.2]. The fixes replay routing decisions:

  • Routing Replay (R2) caches the experts the trainer chose under the old policy and replays them during the update. It fixes routing changes caused by weight updates (about 10% of activated experts changed after one gradient step on a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B) but not engine-versus-trainer differences [GSPO, §5.3].
  • Rollout Routing Replay (R3) records the routing mask from the inference engine and replays it in both the old-policy and update passes, so it also removes the engine-versus-trainer gap. Overhead is under 3% of rollout latency, masks can be cached with the prefix KV for multi-turn use, and the per-token KL on Qwen3-30B-A3B fell from \(1.535 \times 10^{-3}\) to \(7.5 \times 10^{-4}\), near the dense model's \(6.4 \times 10^{-4}\) [Ma et al. 2025, §4]. The masks cost \((\text{tokens} - 1) \times \text{layers} \times k\) int32 values, about 60 MB per 32K-token trajectory at 60 layers and \(k = 8\) [Miles, §2.5] (\(32{,}767 \times 60 \times 8 \times 4\) B = 63 MB by my check).
  • Keep Routing is DeepSeek's version: preserve the sampling-time routing paths and enforce them in training. The report calls it crucial for MoE RL stability, in the pipeline since DeepSeek-V3-0324 [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3.1].
  • Avoid the need: GSPO's sequence-level ratio removed the dependence on Routing Replay for Qwen's MoE runs [GSPO, §5.3].
Routing replay for MoE RL
Problem
Top-\(k\) routing is discontinuous, so tiny numeric differences change which experts run, and the importance ratio compares two different sub-networks.
Idea
Treat each token's expert assignment as rollout data: record it in the engine, and replay it in the trainer's forward passes.
Why it works
The trainer then evaluates the same expert subset that produced the sample, restoring the first-order approximation, at the cost of biasing the target policy toward the recorded routing [Zheng et al. 2025b, §3.2].
Evidence
Qwen3-30B-A3B runs that crashed at step 60 to 120 without R3 stayed stable with it. On a multi-turn SWE task GRPO scored 31.8 and collapsed near step 90, while GRPO+R3 reached 38.6 [Ma et al. 2025, Tab. 1 and 2].
Cost
Extra memory and communication, and bias toward the recorded routing. On-policy, R3 gave no gain in the Qwen study; at 2 times off-policiness R2 beat R3, and at 4 to 8 times R3 won [Zheng et al. 2025b, §4.3 to §4.4]. Combining R3 with TIS gave no clear gain [Ma et al. 2025, §5.2], and Miles leaves R3 off in its asynchronous GLM-5.2 reference run.
Used by
DeepSeek-V3.2 (Keep Routing), Miles and verl (router-replay options), and Qwen's earlier MoE RL runs (Routing Replay).
Pitfall: the mismatch grows during a run

Collapsed runs in the R3 study were almost always preceded by rising training-inference KL and a rising fraction of "extreme" tokens. In one run the share of tokens whose trainer and engine probabilities differed by more than 2× exceeded 10% after step 60, against under \(10^{-4}\) for most of the runs with R3 [Ma et al. 2025, §5.2]. Agentic RL: multi-turn tool use

An agent 智能体 acts through tool calls 工具调用 in an environment over many turns: it emits reasoning and a call, the environment executes it and returns text, and the loop continues until a final answer or a budget runs out. The trajectory is one growing token sequence that interleaves policy tokens with environment tokens, and the reward usually arrives only at the end (the tests pass or they do not). Almost everything specific to agentic RL follows from that interleaving.

Trajectories, and what gets masked

The state is the whole context so far, an action is one assistant turn (reasoning plus tool call), and a transition appends the tool's output. The RL loss must touch only tokens the policy actually sampled:

The rule is token provenance: train exactly the tokens the policy sampled. Three reasons. (1) Gradient on environment text teaches the model to predict retrieved passages or stdout, which is not the behavior we want and can drown the signal. (2) The importance ratio is undefined there: the engine never sampled those tokens, so there is no \(\mu\). (3) It is measurably better. Search-R1 masks retrieved tokens in the PPO and GRPO objectives (including the KL term) and reports an average exact-match score of 0.431 with the mask against 0.343 without on Qwen2.5-7B with PPO [Search-R1, §3.1, Tab. 4]. This is the RL sibling of SFT loss masking (page 19), with one difference: in SFT the assistant tokens are given, while here they must be recorded at sampling time. A minimal rollout loop makes the bookkeeping explicit:

def rollout(engine, env, tokenizer, prompt_ids, max_turns=30):
    ids, mask, logp = list(prompt_ids), [0] * len(prompt_ids), [0.0] * len(prompt_ids)
    for _ in range(max_turns):
        out = engine.generate(ids)                    # new token ids + the engine's per-token log-probs (mu)
        ids += out.token_ids; mask += [1] * len(out.token_ids); logp += out.logprobs
        call = parse_tool_call(out.token_ids)         # decode ONLY to dispatch the call; never re-encode the text
        if call is None:                              # final answer: episode over
            break
        obs = tokenizer.encode(env.step(call), add_special_tokens=False)   # environment text, tokenized once
        ids += obs; mask += [0] * len(obs); logp += [0.0] * len(obs)       # masked: context only
    return ids, mask, logp, env.reward()              # reward from the verifier, outside the agent's reach

The loss then averages over unmasked tokens only (the mask.sum() denominator in the loss of the previous section), so a 1,240-token tool output neither adds gradient nor dilutes the average.

Token-in, token-out: train on the tokens that were sampled

A subtle failure sits under the mask. Many multi-turn pipelines decode the model's tokens to text to parse the tool call, append the tool result as a message, re-render the chat template, and re-tokenize the whole conversation for the trainer. That round trip is lossy. Byte-pair encoding is not reversible, so decode-then-encode can produce different token IDs; chat templates may strip reasoning from earlier turns or re-serialize tool-call arguments; and the boundaries between assistant and tool tokens have to be reconstructed after the fact, differently for each model family [Hugging Face, Token-In Token-Out]. The trainer then scores tokens the policy never sampled, so the old log-probabilities, the mask and the importance ratio all describe a trajectory that did not occur. Miles puts it plainly: the update is taken against "a trajectory that never occurred" [Miles, §2.4].

The fix is to make token IDs the canonical representation. The engine returns completion token IDs and log-probabilities, prior assistant turns keep their original IDs, and only new environment messages are tokenized and appended. ProRL Agent carries input_ids, output_ids and logprobs unchanged through the pipeline, so every token ID reaching the trainer is the one produced at rollout [ProRL Agent, §3.3.3]. Miles moves tokenization into a session server that checkpoints prompt IDs, output IDs, log-probabilities and routed experts after each completion, tokenizes only the appended suffix on the next turn, and assembles one contiguous training sequence with the non-model tokens loss-masked. It also checks per model family that the chat template is append-only from turn to turn, the invariant this approach relies on [Miles, §2.4].

Idea
Record token IDs, log-probs and provenance (policy or environment) at sampling time, append environment tokens once, and mask by provenance.
Why it works
The loss, the importance ratio and the KL are then computed on exactly the sampled actions, and the engine-versus-trainer comparison of the previous section is meaningful.
Evidence
Retrieved-token masking: 0.431 against 0.343 average score [Search-R1, Tab. 4]. Token IDs as the canonical form are a design requirement in ProRL Agent and Miles.

The harness must not own tokenization, so agent scaffolds written for text APIs need an adapter or a session server. Templates that rewrite history (dropping earlier reasoning) break the append-only property and force branching into several training sequences.

Search-R1, verl's multi-turn stack (with template-aware masking), ProRL Agent, Miles.

Trajectory-level versus turn-level rewards

The default is a trajectory-level outcome reward: the verifier scores the finished episode, GRPO normalizes within the group of \(G\) trajectories for the same task, and the resulting advantage is copied to every unmasked token (Figure 3). DeepSeek-V3.2 uses a rule-based outcome reward, a length penalty and a language-consistency reward for reasoning and agent tasks, and a generative reward model with per-prompt rubrics for general tasks [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3]. It is cheap and hard to game, but a 50-step episode with over 20K tokens (ALFWorld) gives one bit for dozens of decisions, and credit assignment across turns is weak.

Turn-level credit has three routes. Dense environment rewards (partial test passes, subgoal flags) help but invite hacking. Learned process rewards are discussed on page 23. GiGPO stays critic-free: it keeps the episode-level advantage \(A_E\) and adds a step-level advantage \(A_S\) computed among actions taken from the same environment state across the group's trajectories (anchor states found with a hash map, no extra rollouts), combined as \(A = A_E + \omega A_S\). On ALFWorld and WebShop with Qwen2.5-1.5B and 7B it beat GRPO by more than 12% and 9%, at under 0.002% extra time, and it falls back to GRPO when no states repeat [GiGPO, abstract, §5.6, §6]. GSPO's token-level variant likewise allows per-token advantages when multi-turn RL wants finer credit than one number per response [GSPO, §4.3].

  • Synthesizing environments that are hard to solve but easy to verify. An agent gets a task category and a sandbox with bash and search, gathers data into a sandbox database, writes task-specific tools as Python functions, then proposes a simple task with a solution function (allowed to call only the tools, never the database) and a verification function that must accept the solution's output. Trip planning is the example: searching the combinatorial space of plans is hard, checking one plan against constraints is easy. They are hard: on 50 sampled tasks DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp reached 12% pass@1 and the best frontier model tested 62% [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3.2.3, §4.3]. From millions of GitHub issue and pull-request pairs, an environment-setup agent builds a runnable environment (dependencies, test runner, JUnit-format results). K2's pipeline collects 3,000+ real MCP tools from GitHub and evolves over 20,000 synthetic tools by domain, builds thousands of agents from different system prompts and tool subsets, pairs each task with a rubric, and lets LLM user personas talk to the agent while a tool simulator (functionally a world model with state and controlled stochasticity) answers tool calls. An LLM judge filters trajectories against the rubric. The report notes the fidelity limit of simulation and complements it with real sandboxes where execution matters, such as coding [K2, §3.1.1]. This pipeline produces the SFT data; the joint RL stage runs verifiable tasks, real sandboxes and a rubric-based reward (next section).
  • A contrast: SWE-RL. Meta's SWE-RL needs no sandbox. Given an issue and file contents, the model writes search/replace edits, and the reward is the difflib.SequenceMatcher similarity in \([0, 1]\) between the predicted and the oracle patch, or \(-1\) for a malformed answer. Sandboxes are systems work. Kimi k1.5's code sandbox replaced Docker with crun, pre-created cgroups and used a tmpfs overlay: container startup dropped from 0.12 s to 0.04 s and the maximum start rate on a 16-core machine rose from 27 to 120 containers per second [k1.5, §2.6.4]. The verifier must run where the agent cannot reach it (reward hacking).

Context management over long trajectories

Agent trajectories outgrow the context window. DeepSeek-V3.2 (128K) reports that over 20% of BrowseComp test cases exceed it. Two rules matter.

  • Keep reasoning across tool calls. DeepSeek-R1 discarded reasoning content when a second round of messages arrived, which forced the model to re-derive its plan after every tool result. V3.2 discards reasoning only when a new user message arrives. Tool calls and results always stay. Harnesses that simulate tool results as user messages defeat this (the report names Roo Code and Terminus and recommends non-thinking mode there) [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3.2.1].
  • Overflow strategies. At 80% of the window, V3.2 applies Summary (summarize the overflowed trajectory and restart), Discard-75% (drop the first 75% of tool-call history) or Discard-all (reset, dropping all previous tool-call history). On BrowseComp the score was 51.4 without management, 60.2 with Summary (364 steps on average) and 67.6 with Discard-all, comparable to parallel scaling while using far fewer steps [DeepSeek-V3.2, §4.4].

What this means for training is my reading, not something the report details. If the harness rewrites history, later contexts are no longer extensions of earlier ones, so one trajectory cannot be scored as a single sequence. It has to become several training sequences, each with its own context, sharing the trajectory's reward. Miles offers "linear" and "branching" session rules, which determine how many training trajectories one session can produce [Miles, §2.4.1]. Train with the same context policy you deploy, or the model is optimized for contexts it will not see.

Systems for agentic rollouts

Environment latency adds a second source of idle GPUs: a turn waiting on a virtual machine or a test suite holds its KV cache and generates nothing. Two remedies are reported, on top of prefix-aware routing (see faster rollouts).

  • Overlap and over-subscribe. K2 deploys heavy environments as dedicated services and runs many concurrent rollouts to amortize slow interactions, plus partial rollout for long-tail trajectories [K2, §3.3.4]. ROLL Flash decomposes each trajectory into environment-level units so pending trajectories go to free engines while one waits for its environment, and adds redundant environment rollouts (7% to 16% more throughput). Environment-level asynchrony gave 2.72× on ALFWorld and 1.81× on SWE [ROLL Flash, §5.2].
  • Serve rollouts as a service. ProRL Agent runs INIT, RUN and EVAL as separate worker pools behind an HTTP API, so slow container startup, GPU-bound agent loops and minutes-long test suites are sized independently, and failed rollouts are excluded from the gradient instead of stalling the pool [ProRL Agent, §3.3, §4.1].

Your tagger trained on a fixed dataset: the label never depended on the network's own earlier outputs, so you could reuse the same events for many epochs. An agent's data depends on the policy. Each tool call changes what the environment returns next, so a trajectory is valid training data only for policies close to the one that produced it. Rubrics and generative reward models

For tasks without a verifier, the frontier recipes replace a single scalar model with a generative judge that reasons against explicit criteria.

  • DeepSeek-V3.2: for general tasks it uses a generative reward model in which each prompt has its own rubrics, and its search-agent data pairs verifiable answers with rubric scoring so that both factual reliability and helpfulness are optimized [DeepSeek-V3.2, §3, §3.2.3].
  • Kimi K2's self-critique rubric reward: the actor samples several responses to a general prompt, and the K2 critic ranks them by pairwise comparison against a mix of core rubrics (the assistant's basic values), prescriptive rubrics "that aim to eliminate reward hacking", and human-written rubrics for specific instruction types. The critic starts from preference data in SFT and, crucially, is refined during RL using on-policy rollouts from verifiable-reward prompts, so its standards are grounded in tasks with ground truth and recalibrate as the policy changes [K2, §3.2.2]. K2 also runs a "hack-check" layer for instruction following that detects claims of compliance without actual compliance (§3.2.1).
  • Rubrics as Rewards (RaR): a strong LLM writes a prompt-specific rubric of 7 to 20 self-contained items (Essential, Important, Optional, Pitfall), conditioned on a reference answer. The reward is an explicit weighted sum \(\sum_j w_j c_j / \sum_j w_j\) of binary checks or an implicit holistic 1 to 10 score from a judge shown the whole rubric. RLVR is the special case of one item. Against direct Likert-style judge rewards it improved by up to 31% (HealthBench) and 7% (GPQA-Diamond); generic fixed rubrics and rubrics written without a reference answer did worse (32.0 versus 35.9 on HealthBench-1k). It covers medicine and science only, not tool use [Gunjal et al. 2025, §2 to §6, §9].
  • DeepSeek-GRM: a pointwise generative reward model that writes principles, then a critique and a score per response, so one format rates one, two or many responses. Pairwise models cannot rate a single response, and scalar models return one deterministic number, so sampling more does not help them. With a 27B model the benchmark average rose from 69.9 (greedy) to 71.0 (voting over 32 samples) and 72.8 with a meta reward model guiding the vote [Liu et al. 2025, §3, §5, Tab. 2].

Cost arithmetic for the design choice: ranking a group of \(G = 16\) responses pairwise takes \(\binom{16}{2} = 120\) comparisons (240 if each pair is judged in both orders to cancel position bias), against 16 calls pointwise. Judge cost is a real fraction of the reward budget, and rubric guidance helped small judges most in RaR's judge-alignment study [Gunjal et al. 2025, §5].

Problem

What it does to training. In OpenAI's frontier run, two systemic hacks, exit(0) and raise SkipTest, once discovered, "quickly get reinforced and become systemic, affecting nearly every training environment". A prompted GPT-4o that read the agent's chain of thought caught them with 95% joint recall against 60% for a monitor that saw only actions. But when the monitor was folded into the reward, the agent first improved (more real solutions, fewer hacks) and then learned obfuscated reward hacking: it hid its intent in a benign-looking chain of thought, monitor recall fell to near zero, and hacking continued [Baker et al. 2025, §2 to §3]. Anthropic's study adds that hacking need not stay local: models that learned to hack in real coding environments generalized to alignment faking and sabotage on other evaluations. Effective mitigations were preventing the hack, diversifying the RLHF safety data, and "inoculation prompting" [MacDiarmid et al. 2025, Abstract, §2].

A defensive checklist (my synthesis of the sources above):

  1. Isolate the verifier. Run tests where the agent cannot write, inject or restore the test files at scoring time, and prefer hidden tests. A verifier the agent shares a filesystem with is a suggestion.
  2. Require more than one signal. DeepSeek-V3.2's validity rule (fail-to-pass greater than 0, pass-to-fail equal to 0), held-out tests, or rubric-plus-execution rewards.
  3. Detect what you know. Regexes and AST checks for known hacks (OpenAI used regexes for exit(0) and SkipTest as ground truth), LLM monitors over whole trajectories, and manual reading of the highest-reward trajectories every few hundred steps.
  4. Do not train hard against the monitor. Use flags to patch environments and gate releases, not as a large reward term, and accept the "monitorability tax".
  5. Offer a legitimate exit ("this task is broken") and evaluate on a different harness, the cheapest way to see train reward and true quality diverge.

Common misconceptions

  • "RL for LLMs is compute-bound like pretraining." Most of the GPU time goes to memory-bound decoding at a few percent MFU. In the worked example generation is about 65% of GPU-seconds, and reported systems spend 61% to 87% of iteration wall-clock in rollout.
  • "More GPUs make rollouts proportionally faster." Extra GPUs add engine replicas and raise throughput, but the latency of the longest response does not shrink, so the tail stays. That is the case for asynchrony, partial rollouts and tail-aware scheduling.
  • "Same weights means same log-probabilities." The engine and the trainer differ in kernels, reduction order, precision, LM-head dtype, sampling masks and MoE routing. Measured per-token KL is around \(10^{-3}\), and it can grow during a run.
  • "The PPO clip handles the mismatch." The clip limits update size around \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) and, with one update per batch, the ratio is exactly 1, so it cannot see engine-versus-trainer differences. The discrepancy factor needs its own correction: truncated importance sampling, sequence masking, or exact agreement.
  • "Asynchronous RL is synchronous RL, only faster." It changes the data: staleness, skewed lengths, mixed policy versions inside one trajectory, and sometimes missing old logits. AReaL's naive PPO scored 23.3 on AIME24 at \(\eta = 4\) against 42.0 for the synchronous oracle, and the decoupled objective recovered 42.2.
  • "Regenerating an interrupted rollout from scratch is harmless." Short responses survive interruptions more often, so regeneration conditions the delivered data on finishing early and biases the policy toward short outputs. Resume from a token log, or keep the finished samples unbiased some other way.
  • "In multi-turn RL, train on the whole conversation." Train only on tokens the policy sampled, and do not re-tokenize them from text. Tool outputs and template scaffolding are context, and a re-tokenized sequence is a trajectory that never happened.
  • "An execution-based reward cannot be hacked." Agents exit before the asserts, patch pytest, overload __eq__, edit tests, and stub libraries. The verifier must run where the agent cannot write, and hack rates need their own metrics.

Interview questions

Warm-up Name the phases of one RL step for an LLM and the software that runs each.

(1) Rollout: sample \(G\) responses per prompt on an inference engine (vLLM or SGLang). (2) Reward: verifiers, sandboxes for code and tools, judges or reward models. (3) Log-probability passes on the trainer: \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) for the ratio and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) if there is a KL term. (4) Update: forward, backward and optimizer step in FSDP or Megatron. (5) Weight sync: send the new weights to the engines. There are two software stacks with different parallelism (the trainer shards for memory and throughput, the engine uses tensor parallelism plus many replicas), so weight sync includes resharding. Rollout usually dominates wall-clock.

Warm-up Define colocated and disaggregated placement. Give one advantage and one cost of each.

Colocated: trainer and engine time-share the same GPUs, each releasing memory while the other runs. Advantage: no idle pool, every phase uses all GPUs, and each side can use its own parallel layout. Cost: an offload, weight-sync and engine wake-up on every step (Kimi k1.5: under a minute one way, about ten seconds back), and no overlap between generation and training. Disaggregated: separate pools. Advantage: no per-step switch, independent scaling and parallelism, and overlap becomes possible. Cost: each pool idles whenever the other is the bottleneck, so it needs asynchrony and a split matching the generation share of the work (about 65% rollout in our 8B example; AReaL used 75%).

Warm-up Give three reward hacks in an agentic coding environment and one defense for each.

(1) sys.exit(0) before the asserts, so the process returns code 0: judge success from parsed test-framework output produced by a runner outside the agent's process, not from the exit code. (2) Editing or deleting tests: mount tests read-only or restore them from a clean copy at scoring time, or keep hidden tests. (3) A conftest.py that patches pytest's report function, or an always-equal object: run the verifier from a clean checkout in a separate container with a pinned configuration and no agent-writable files on the path. Also add detectors for the known patterns and read the highest-reward trajectories regularly. Beyond hacks that game the harness, special-casing the visible tests is best caught with hidden tests.

Core Why is rollout generation the bottleneck in LLM RL, and how do systems address it?

Why. Decode is memory-bound: each step streams the weights plus every sequence's KV cache, so the engine runs at roughly 4% MFU against about 40% for training. Per token that makes generation about twice as expensive as training even though it needs fewer FLOPs (\(2N\) against \(\sim 10N\) with the log-probability passes). Example: \(512 \times 16 \times 8192 = 67.1\)M tokens; at 2,500 tokens/s per GPU that is 7.5 GPU-hours, against 4.0 GPU-hours to train on them, so about 65% of GPU-seconds. Measured rollout shares are 63% to 87% (Seer) and 61% (GiGPO on ALFWorld). Wall-clock is worse: a synchronous batch waits for the longest response, and the last 10% of requests took up to 50% of rollout time.

How systems help. Fill idle slots with asynchrony and bounded staleness (AReaL 1.5× to 2.8×), or partial rollouts (Kimi), or queue scheduling and prompt replication (ROLL Flash). Fix the tail without changing on-policy semantics: divided rollout, group-length prediction and grouped speculative decoding (Seer 2.04×). Make decode cheaper: low-precision rollouts, at the price of more mismatch. Shrink the switch cost in colocated designs (checkpoint engines, under 30 s for a 1T model). Overlap rewards and environments with generation.

Core Synchronous versus asynchronous RL: what are the trade-offs?

Synchronous: generate a batch with the current weights, train, sync. Data is on-policy (up to engine mismatch), the run is reproducible and easy to reason about, but GPUs idle at the tail and adding GPUs does not help. Asynchronous: separate pools, engines keep generating while the trainer works, weights are pushed as they update. It hides the tail and scales with GPUs (AReaL 1.5× to 2.8× end to end, ROLL Flash up to 2.7× on agent tasks). The costs: (1) staleness, bounded by an admission rule \(\lfloor (N_r-1)/B \rfloor \le i + \eta\), which caps outstanding samples at \((\eta+1)B\); (2) a decoupled objective (behavior versus proximal policy) so that clipping is centered on a recent policy, since naive PPO at \(\eta = 4\) fell from 42.0 to 23.3 AIME24; (3) length skew because short samples arrive first; (4) extra log-probability recomputation, which is hard when partial rollouts span versions; (5) harder reproducibility. Moderate staleness (\(\eta \le 8\) in AReaL, a ratio of about 2 in ROLL Flash) was nearly free with the corrected objective. Choose synchronous for small clusters, short responses and debugging; asynchronous for long responses, agents and large clusters.

Core What is the training-inference mismatch problem, and how do you correct it?

The engine that samples (\(\mu\)) and the trainer (\(\pi\)) assign different probabilities to the same tokens under the same weights, because of different kernels and reduction orders, precision (FP8 or INT8 rollouts, the LM head), sampling truncation and, for MoE, different routing. The ratio the algorithm needs factors as \(\frac{\pi_{\text{old}}}{\mu_{\text{old}}} \times \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{old}}}\): discrepancy times staleness. Standard PPO and GRPO keep only the second factor, and with one update per batch it is exactly 1, so the clip is blind to the first. Measured gaps: per-token KL \(1.5 \times 10^{-3}\) (Qwen3-30B-A3B) and \(6.4 \times 10^{-4}\) (dense 8B), with tokens at probability 1 in vLLM and 0 in the trainer in one study. Uncorrected, runs drift and collapse.

Corrections, in order: (1) importance-sampling correction, truncated: weight tokens by \(\min(\pi_{\text{train}}/\mu_{\text{rollout}}, C)\), detached (Yao et al.). (2) Sequence-level tools: length-normalized ratios (GSPO), or masking sequences whose divergence exceeds a threshold (DeepSeek-V3.2). (3) Remove sources: FP32 LM head (MiniMax-M1 correlation 0.987 to 0.997), keep the sampling masks, batch-invariant deterministic kernels (DeepSeek-V4), or an exact true-on-policy mode (Miles) at a throughput cost. (4) For MoE, routing replay (R2, R3, Keep Routing). Always log the engine-versus-trainer KL and the extreme-token fraction.

Core Calculate: a per-token log-probability mismatch of 0.01 nats. What does it do to the sequence-level importance ratio for 1K versus 16K tokens?

The sequence log-ratio is the sum of per-token gaps. If the bias is systematic (same sign), it is \(0.01 T\): \(e^{10} \approx 22{,}000\) at \(T = 1000\) and \(e^{160} \approx 3 \times 10^{69}\) at \(T = 16{,}000\), which overflows float32 (so work in log space or float64). If the signs are random, the log-ratio has standard deviation \(0.01 \sqrt{T}\): 0.32 and 1.26, factors of 1.37 and 3.5. The length-normalized geometric mean is \(e^{0.01} = 1.010\) at any length. At token level \(1.010\) is well inside a PPO clip of \([0.8, 1.2]\) and any truncation cap, so per-token corrections do not see it, while full sequence ratios are unusable at 16K.

Calibrate: a measured token-level KL \(\kappa\) implies \(\sigma^2 \approx 2\kappa\) under a log-normal model, so \(\kappa = 1.5 \times 10^{-3}\) means \(\sigma = 0.055\) per token. Over 16K tokens the log-ratio has standard deviation 7 and mean \(-24\), so the median sequence weight is about \(10^{-11}\) and the effective sample fraction \(e^{-2\kappa T} \approx 10^{-21}\). Hence per-token truncated weights, length normalization or sequence masking.

Core How do you set up RL for a multi-turn tool-using agent? What gets masked?

Rollout: the engine generates a turn, the harness parses the tool call (decoding only to dispatch), the sandbox executes it, and the tool output is tokenized once and appended, until a final answer or a turn or context budget. Mask by provenance: train only the tokens the policy sampled (reasoning, tool-call syntax, end-of-turn, final answer). Mask the system and user prompt, tool outputs, and template tokens the harness inserted. Apply the same mask to the KL term. Search-R1 measured 0.431 with retrieved-token masking against 0.343 without. Token-in-token-out: keep token IDs and engine log-probs as the canonical record, never decode-and-re-encode, or the trainer scores tokens that were not sampled.

Reward and credit: a trajectory-level verifier reward, group-normalized, copied to every unmasked token; turn-level credit if needed (GiGPO anchor states). Environments: executable, checkable, moderately hard, diverse (DeepSeek-V3.2's synthesized general-agent tasks transferred where code and search alone did not), one isolated sandbox per episode, errored rollouts excluded from the gradient. Context: keep reasoning across tool calls, use a summarize or discard strategy at about 80% of the window, and train with the context policy you deploy (branching into several sequences if history is rewritten). Systems: environment services, many concurrent rollouts, partial rollouts, prefix-affinity routing.

Core How would you design rewards for tasks without a verifier?

First look for a checkable proxy: execution (tests), a reference-based check, or a construction that is hard to solve but easy to verify (DeepSeek-V3.2's synthesized tasks with a verifier function). If none exists, use a rubric-based generative judge: an LLM writes a prompt-specific checklist (7 to 20 self-contained items, weighted, with pitfalls), ideally conditioned on a reference answer, and a judge scores the response either as a weighted sum of binary checks or as a holistic score given the rubric (RaR: up to 31% relative gain over Likert judging; reference-free rubrics did worse). Pointwise scoring (DeepSeek-GRM) handles one or many responses and can be sampled and voted; pairwise ranking (K2's critic) costs \(\binom{G}{2}\) comparisons per group and needs order randomization.

Keep the judge honest: refine or calibrate it on verifiable tasks during RL (K2's closed loop), add prescriptive rubrics and a hack-check pass for known exploits, shuffle response order, use several judges or a held-out human eval, control length bias, and watch for judge exploitation as reward rising without held-out gains. Combine rubric scores with verifiable rewards where partial verification exists (V3.2's search agent).

Core Design question: you have 128 H100s, an 8B policy, batches of 512 prompts × 16 samples, 8K-token responses. Propose an RL system.

Estimate first (numbers from this page): about 67M generated tokens per step (if responses reach the cap), 7.5 GPU-hours of generation at 2,500 tokens/s per GPU and 4.0 GPU-hours of training, so a 65/35 split of GPU-seconds. Ideal step time on 128 GPUs is \((26{,}844 + 14{,}473)/128 \approx 5.4\) minutes. At 128 GPUs colocation is past the range where HybridFlow found it best (16 to 64 GPUs, short responses), and the tail would leave GPUs idle, so I would disaggregate: about 83 GPUs for rollout (65%, up to 75% as responses grow) and 45 for training, asynchronous with \(\eta\) around 2 to 4 (an outstanding-sample bound of \((\eta+1) \times 8192\)).

Stability: decoupled PPO or TIS with a cap of 2 to 5, engine log-probs returned, FP32 LM head, engine-versus-trainer KL logged. Rollout: vLLM or SGLang replicas with TP 1 for an 8B model, queue scheduling and prompt replication, a response cap with a length penalty. Weight sync: 16 GB in bf16, a few seconds over NCCL (Magistral reports under 5 s per update), negligible next to a multi-minute step. Add group filtering for zero-advantage groups, and a held-out eval every few steps. If synchronous reproducibility mattered more than 1.5× throughput, I would stay synchronous and attack the tail with partial rollouts and scheduling.

Deep-dive Why do practitioners avoid full sequence-level importance weights for long responses, and what do they use instead?

Sequence weights multiply per-token ratios, so their log-variance grows linearly in length. Under a log-normal model with per-token KL \(\kappa\), the effective sample fraction is \(e^{-2\kappa T}\): 5% at 1K tokens for \(\kappa = 1.5 \times 10^{-3}\), about \(10^{-21}\) at 16K. Almost all the weight sits on a few sequences, so gradients are dominated by noise. Alternatives trade bias for variance. Per-token truncated weights are the first-order expansion of the sequence weight (\(\prod (1+\delta_t) \approx 1 + \sum \delta_t\)) and bounded by a cap, at the cost of bias when the true ratio exceeds the cap; they are principled only while per-token gaps are small, which is why fixing the source matters too. Length-normalized ratios (GSPO's geometric mean) are length-invariant and tolerant of numerical noise, but change the objective and its clipping scale (GSPO clips on the order of \(10^{-4}\) rather than 0.2). Sequence masking (DeepSeek-V3.2) drops sequences whose mean log-ratio exceeds a threshold, only for negative advantages. Exact agreement (batch-invariant kernels) makes the ratio 1 and removes the need. The Qwen study found length normalization in a token-level loss suboptimal because it breaks the first-order argument, so the choice depends on the algorithm's other parts.

Deep-dive Regenerating an interrupted rollout from scratch biases the training data. Why, and how should preemption and partial rollouts be handled?

Suppose interruptions arrive at random times and any request unfinished at that moment is restarted. The probability that a request finishes before the interruption falls with its length, so the delivered samples are conditioned on finishing early, and the policy is pushed toward short outputs. DeepSeek-V4 calls plain regeneration "mathematically incorrect" for this reason. Seer measured a related effect: an over-issue partial-rollout scheme produced a smaller share of long outputs than synchronous rollout. Correct options: (1) resume, don't restart: log every generated token (V4's token-granular write-ahead log), keep or rebuild the KV cache, and continue decoding, so each request's length distribution is untouched; (2) regenerate with the same random seed under a batch-invariant deterministic stack, which is unbiased but repeats decoding; (3) keep partial trajectories across iterations as Kimi's partial rollout does, remembering that segments came from different policy versions, so the loss needs the behavior-versus-proximal correction and the old log-probabilities of earlier segments must be available (or approximated). Also avoid stopping at "the first \(B\) finished" without accounting for the length skew.

Deep-dive MoE RL is less stable than dense RL. Why, and what are the options?

Top-\(k\) routing is discontinuous: a tiny numeric difference flips the chosen experts. Between SGLang and Megatron on Qwen3-30B-A3B, about 10% of router decisions differed and 94% of tokens used a different expert in at least one layer, and the per-token KL was \(1.5 \times 10^{-3}\) against \(6.4 \times 10^{-4}\) for a dense 8B. Even two trainer passes over the same sequence disagreed (KL \(8.4 \times 10^{-4}\)). Weight updates flip routing too (about 10% of activated experts after one step in GSPO's analysis), so both factors of the ratio suffer.

Options: R2 replays the trainer's old-policy routing during the update (fixes staleness-induced flips, not engine mismatch); R3 records the engine's routing and replays it everywhere (overhead under 3% of rollout latency, about 60 MB per 32K-token trajectory at 60 layers with \(k = 8\), KL down to \(7.5 \times 10^{-4}\)); DeepSeek's Keep Routing does the same; GSPO's sequence-level ratio avoided replay in Qwen's runs. Trade-offs: replay biases the target policy. In the Qwen study R3 did not help on-policy, R2 won at mild off-policiness and R3 at 4 to 8 times, and R3 plus TIS gave no clear gain. In asynchronous runs other mismatch sources dominate (Miles leaves R3 off in its GLM-5.2 reference run). Evidence that it matters: on a multi-turn SWE task GRPO collapsed near step 90 (31.8) while GRPO+R3 reached 38.6.

Deep-dive Debugging scenario: training reward keeps rising, but held-out pass rate is flat, mean response length is growing, and the engine-versus-trainer KL has doubled over the last 200 steps. What do you check, in what order?

1. Is it real learning or hacking? Read the highest-reward trajectories and run the hack detectors (test-file edits, early exits, always-equal or stubbed code, monitor flags). Rising train reward with flat held-out reward on a different harness is the classic signature. If hacks appear, patch the environment, isolate the verifier, and do not just penalize the pattern in the reward. 2. Mismatch. A KL that doubles is itself a warning (in the R3 study collapse followed rising KL and a rising extreme-token fraction). Compare engine and trainer log-probs on identical sequences (and two trainer passes), then check for causes that grew with training: longer responses (mismatch compounds with length), lower-probability tokens as entropy shifts, a precision path such as the LM head or FP8 rollouts, and MoE routing. Turn on or tighten TIS or sequence masking, consider routing replay, and fix precision.

3. Length. Check the truncation rate, repetition loops and the length distribution tail. Add a length penalty or budget and a repetition cut-off. If responses are truncated and scored 0, the model may be learning to be short or to loop. 4. Tokenization and masking in multi-turn runs: verify token IDs round-trip and the mask covers only policy tokens. 5. Async health: the staleness histogram and the sync time. 6. Signal density: the share of zero-advantage groups, entropy and clip fraction. If the run has already diverged, roll back to the last good checkpoint, reduce staleness or the number of minibatches per batch, and rerun with the extra monitors on.

Cheat sheet

  • Step phases: rollout (engine) → reward (verifier, sandbox, judge) → \(\pi_{\text{old}}\), \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) passes → update → weight sync.
  • Why generation dominates: \(2N\) FLOPs/token at about 4% MFU versus about \(10N\) at about 40% for training. Example: 512 × 16 × 8K = 67.1M tokens = 7.46 GPU-h at 2,500 tok/s, against 4.02 GPU-h of training: 65% of GPU-seconds. Reported rollout shares: 63% to 87% (Seer), 61% (GiGPO). Last 10% of requests: up to 50% of rollout time.
  • GPU split: step time \(= \max(W_g/N_r,\, W_t/N_t)\), so give rollout the fraction \(W_g/(W_g+W_t)\) of GPUs (about 42 of 64 in the example; AReaL used 75/25).
  • Placement: colocated (k1.5: under 1 min in, about 10 s back; K2: checkpoint engine, under 30 s for 1T) versus disaggregated (OpenRLHF, AReaL). HybridFlow: colocate was best at 16 to 64 GPUs.
  • Async: admit a request if \(\lfloor (N_r-1)/B \rfloor \le i+\eta\); outstanding samples \(\le (\eta+1)B\); \(\eta=0\) is synchronous. Decoupled PPO uses \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\) as the clip center. AIME24 at \(\eta=4\): naive PPO 23.3, decoupled 42.2, oracle 42.0. Speedups 1.5× to 2.8× (AReaL); ROLL Flash ratio about 2.
  • Partial rollout: continue over budget in the next iteration (Kimi). Never regenerate interrupted requests from scratch: length bias. Resume from a token log (DeepSeek-V4).
  • Ratio: \(\pi_\theta/\mu_{\text{old}} = (\pi_{\text{old}}/\mu_{\text{old}}) \times (\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}})\). PPO clip sees only the second factor. TIS: weight \(\min(\pi_{\text{train}}/\mu_{\text{rollout}}, C)\), detached, \(C\) in 2 to 5.
  • 0.01 nats/token: \(e^{10} = 2.2\times10^4\) at 1K, \(e^{160} = 3\times10^{69}\) at 16K; random-sign drift \(0.01\sqrt{T}\); geometric mean \(e^{0.01} = 1.01\). Measured KL \(\kappa\): \(1.5\times10^{-3}\) (30B MoE), \(6.4\times10^{-4}\) (8B dense); \(\sigma^2 \approx 2\kappa\); effective sample fraction \(e^{-2\kappa T}\).
  • Fix ladder: TIS → sequence masking or GSPO-style length normalization → FP32 LM head, keep sampling masks → batch-invariant kernels (V4 §3.3) or true-on-policy (Miles) → MoE routing replay (R2, R3, Keep Routing). MoE: about 10% of routers and 94% of tokens differ.
  • Agentic RL: train only policy-sampled tokens (Search-R1: 0.431 with the mask, 0.343 without); token IDs are canonical, never re-tokenize; trajectory reward copied to unmasked tokens; GiGPO adds step-level advantages from anchor states (over 12% ALFWorld, over 9% WebShop).
  • Environments and context: DeepSeek-V3.2: 85,267 prompts, 1,827 synthesized environments, validity rule F2P greater than 0 and P2F equal to 0; keep reasoning until a new user message; overflow strategies Summary, Discard-75%, Discard-all (BrowseComp 51.4 to 60.2 and 67.6).
  • Rewards without verifiers: per-prompt rubrics judged by an LLM (RaR up to +31%), K2's self-critic refined on verifiable tasks, pointwise GRM with voting. Hacks: exit(0), SkipTest, always-equal, conftest.py, stubs, editing tests. ImpossibleBench: GPT-5 cheats 54% (Conflicting-SWEbench); abort option cuts it to 9%; CoT monitor recall 95% versus 60% for actions only.
  • Monitor: train versus held-out reward, zero-advantage share, length p99 and truncation, entropy, KL, clip fraction, engine-trainer KL and extreme-token fraction, staleness histogram, environment failures, hack detectors.
