DeepSeek I: LLM, MoE, Math, V2

P0 ~60 min Prereqs: 07 MLA, 08 MoE, 13 Scaling laws, 24 GRPO

Between November 2023 and September 2024 DeepSeek went from a LLaMA-shaped 67B dense model to a 236B mixture-of-experts model whose KV cache is an order of magnitude smaller, and almost every step was justified by the same argument: spend fewer FLOPs, bytes or GPU-hours per unit of quality. This page walks through that lineage paper by paper, with the numbers, the reasons and the caveats, so you can discuss DeepSeek's research style fluently and audit its headline claims.

What interviewers probe
  • Lineage fluency: which paper introduced which idea (hyperparameter scaling laws, fine-grained plus shared experts, GRPO, MLA), what each one was for, and how V2 combines them.
  • Scaling-law method: what DeepSeek LLM changed (the model-scale variable \(M\), the batch-size and learning-rate power laws, data quality moving the allocation), and whether you can evaluate the laws at a given compute on the spot.
  • Reading headline numbers critically: V2's "42.5% cheaper, 93.3% smaller KV cache, 5.76× throughput" compare specific things on specific hardware and precision. Expect "how would you check that claim?"
  • Parameter accounting: reconcile 236B total and 21B activated from a config, and say which embedding convention you used.
  • Post-training as one family: the "unified paradigm" from DeepSeekMath (data source, reward function, gradient coefficient), why GRPO drops the critic, and why online beat offline in V2.
  • Systems co-design: why V2 trains without tensor parallelism, what device-limited routing buys, and how the H800 interconnect shaped these choices.

Key idea: one recipe, reused

These are not separate projects. DeepSeekMoE 16B, DeepSeekMath and V2 all use DeepSeek LLM's optimizer (AdamW, \(\beta = (0.9, 0.95)\), weight decay 0.1), its 2,000-step warmup and its step schedule with two drops of \(\times 0.316\), and all are trained with the in-house HAI-LLM framework. DeepSeek-Coder states that it "adapt[s] batch sizes and learning rates by the scaling laws suggested in DeepSeek LLM" [Coder, §3.4]. A shared, well-understood recipe is what lets a small team publish an architecture paper, a domain paper and a flagship within four months.

DeepSeek LLM (January 2024): a LLaMA-shaped model and the scaling study behind it

The report opens with a complaint: earlier scaling-law papers reached "varying conclusions" and "inadequately addressed hyperparameter discussions", so nobody could tell whether models at each compute budget were tuned [DeepSeek LLM, §1]. The team therefore ran the scaling study first, used it to set hyperparameters, and then trained a 7B and a 67B on 2T tokens of English and Chinese. The laws themselves are introduced on page 13. This section covers what DeepSeek did with them and why.

Architecture: LLaMA on purpose, with two departures

The micro design "largely follows" LLaMA: pre-norm RMSNorm, a SwiGLU FFN of width \(\tfrac{8}{3} d\), and RoPE (page 04). The stated reason: LLaMA had become "the de facto benchmark for architecture", so holding it fixed keeps the study about data and scaling [§1, §2.2]. The tokenizer is byte-level BPE with a 102,400-entry model vocabulary (page 02).

The ten-times-larger model has the smaller cache. Remember 380 KiB: it is the baseline for V2's "93.3% smaller KV cache" (below, page 07).

The multi-step learning rate: chosen for continual training

After 2,000 warmup steps the learning rate stays at its peak, drops to 31.6% after 80% of the tokens, and to 10% after 90% (\(0.316 = 1/\sqrt{10}\), so two drops multiply to 0.1). On a 1.6B model with 100B tokens the final performance was "essentially consistent" with cosine, and the schedule "allows for the reuse of training from the first phase, offering a unique convenience for continual training" [§2.3, Fig. 1]. A cosine curve is a function of the total budget \(T\), so a new budget means a new run; a step schedule's 80% constant stage can be extended. Other stage proportions were "slightly better", but 80/10/10 was kept to balance reuse against performance. V2 moved its first drop to about 60% (page 14 relates this family to warmup-stable-decay).

Scaling law 1: batch size and learning rate as power laws of compute

DeepSeek grid-searched batch size and learning rate at \(10^{17}\) FLOPs and found the error "stable across a wide range" of both: a broad basin. It then trained a grid at budgets from \(10^{17}\) to \(2 \times 10^{19}\), reusing the first stage of the step schedule to make this cheap, called settings within 0.25% of the best error near-optimal, and fitted power laws [§3.1, Fig. 2-3]:

\[ \eta_{\text{opt}} = 0.3118\,C^{-0.1250}, \qquad B_{\text{opt}} = 0.2920\,C^{0.3271} \]
\(C = M D\) in FLOPs (\(M\) defined below); \(B\) in tokens; \(\eta\) is the peak learning rate

A check at \(10^{20}\) FLOPs found the fitted point "centered in the optimal parameter space". The paper's own caveats: the optimum shifts slightly with the model-versus-data split at fixed \(C\), and the fit is inconsistent with earlier work that modeled the optimal batch size through the loss alone (page 13 covers critical batch size and an independent refit).

Scaling law 2: the model scale is FLOPs per token, not parameters

For the model-versus-data allocation, DeepSeek replaced the parameter count \(N\) by the non-embedding FLOPs per token \(M\), and \(C \approx 6ND\) by \(C = MD\) [§3.2, Eq. 2]:

\[ 6N_1 = 72\,L\,d^2, \qquad 6N_2 = 72\,L\,d^2 + 6\,V\,d, \qquad M = 72\,L\,d^2 + 12\,L\,d\,l_{\text{seq}} \]

\(N_1\) is Kaplan's non-embedding count, \(N_2\) Chinchilla's count including vocabulary matrices. The first term is \(6 \cdot 12Ld^2\) (\(4d^2\) attention plus \(8d^2\) SwiGLU). The second is our derivation of the attention-score term: \(2ld\) for \(QK^\top\) plus \(2ld\) for the value average per token per layer, times 3 for forward plus backward, counting the full \(l \times l\) matrix. \(M\) drops the vocabulary (it "contributes less to the model's capacity") and adds the sequence term that neither \(6N\) count has.

The paper's explanation is an "intuitive speculation": high-quality data has "logical clarity and less predictive difficulty after sufficient training", so extra compute is better spent on model size. The practical corollary: refit the allocation whenever the data recipe changes. As a cross-check (our arithmetic), at the 67B's compute the fitted optimum is \(M = 6.5 \times 10^{11}\) with 1.5T tokens, while the 67B has \(M = 5.0 \times 10^{11}\) and saw 2T: DeepSeek picked the conventional 67B size, 24% below the optimum and 32% longer, on the flat part of the valley.

Alignment: SFT, then DPO

The alignment set has about 1.5M instances in English and Chinese: 1.2M helpfulness (31.2% general language, 46.6% math, 22.2% code) and 300K safety [§4]. The 7B was fine-tuned for 4 epochs and the 67B for 2 ("the overfitting problem is serious on the 67B model"). As math SFT data grew, so did a repetition failure (responses that never terminate): 2.0% of 3,868 test prompts for the 7B. Two fixes worked: a second SFT stage on conversational data only (repetition 2.0% to 1.4%, HumanEval unchanged at 48.2; Tab. 12) and DPO, one epoch at learning rate \(5 \times 10^{-6}\), batch 512, on helpfulness and harmlessness pairs sampled from DeepSeek's own chat models. DPO "can strengthen the model's open-ended generation skill" and moved standard benchmarks little (67B Chat: MMLU 71.1 to 70.9, GSM8K 84.1 to 85.2, MATH 32.6 to 30.2; Tab. 17). Mechanics: page 21.

Base-model results against Llama 2 at the same 2T tokens, in DeepSeek's harness [Tab. 5]: DeepSeek 67B versus Llama 2 70B scores MMLU 71.3 versus 69.0, GSM8K 63.4 versus 58.4, MATH 18.7 versus 13.5, HumanEval 42.7 versus 28.7 and BBH 68.7 versus 62.9. Harness matters: V2's report re-measures the same 67B at HumanEval 45.1.

DeepSeekMoE (January 2024): specialization as an explicit design goal

Six days after the DeepSeek LLM report came the architecture paper that V2 would build on. DeepSeekMoE starts from two diagnoses of the conventional 8 to 16 expert MoE layer [Dai et al. 2024, §1]. Knowledge hybridity: with few experts, the tokens sent to one expert cover very different knowledge, which one FFN must store together. Knowledge redundancy: tokens sent to different experts still need common knowledge, so several experts learn copies. Both stop experts from specializing, so conventional MoE falls short of its "upper bound", a dense model with the same total parameters.

Each diagnosis gets one fix, at constant parameters and activated FLOPs. Fine-grained segmentation splits each expert into \(m\) smaller ones and activates \(m\) times as many, multiplying the expert combinations a token can use. Shared expert isolation reserves a few always-on experts for common knowledge so routed experts can specialize. Mechanics and combinatorics (120 choices become \(4.4 \times 10^9\) in the paper's example) are on page 08. Here the questions are how DeepSeek validated the idea and what the evidence supports.

What the 16B model got wrong

DeepSeekMoE 16B beat its dense comparators on language modeling and knowledge tasks (Pile, HellaSwag, TriviaQA) but "exhibits limitations in addressing multiple-choice tasks": MMLU 45.0 versus 48.2 for DeepSeek 7B, CMMLU 42.5 versus 47.2 [§5.2.1, Tab. 3]. The explanation is limited attention capacity: "only about 0.5B attention parameters" (\(28 \cdot 4 \cdot 2048^2 = 0.47\)B by our count) out of 2.8B activated, and an earlier DeepSeek 7B variant with multi-query attention "also struggled in MMLU-like tasks". V2's attention, at \(d = 5{,}120\) with 128 heads, holds 8.95B parameters, about 44% of its activated non-embedding parameters (below), against about 20% for the 16B (0.47B of 2.41B). It is tempting to read MLA as the answer to "keep attention strong but make the cache small".

DeepSeek-Coder and DeepSeekMath (January to February 2024): data engineering, then GRPO

DeepSeek-Coder in one paragraph

DeepSeek-Coder trains 1.3B, 6.7B and 33B models from scratch on 2T tokens: 87% source code in 87 languages (public GitHub repositories created before February 2023), 10% English code-related text and 3% Chinese [Guo et al. 2024, §2]. Two ideas carry forward. Repository-level data: import dependencies between files are parsed and the files topologically sorted, so a file's dependencies precede it in the context; the authors say this can "significantly boost the capability of cross-file code generation". Fill-in-the-middle (FIM): documents are split into prefix, middle and suffix and rearranged as prefix-suffix-middle with sentinel tokens, at a 50% rate. The ablation fixed that rate: 100% FIM scored best on the infilling benchmark but gave the weakest ordinary completion. The window is 16K (RoPE linear scaling factor 4, base raised from 10,000 to 100,000, 1,000 extra steps). The 33B uses GQA, and batch size and learning rate follow the DeepSeek LLM laws.

DeepSeekMath: mining a math corpus from Common Crawl

DeepSeekMath's first contribution is data: "the publicly accessible Common Crawl data contains valuable information for mathematical purposes", if you can find it [Shao et al. 2024, §1-2]. The pipeline is an iterated classifier bootstrap:

  1. Seed and classifier. OpenWebMath pages are positives (500,000) and random Common Crawl pages negatives (500,000) for a fastText classifier. Run it over a deduplicated crawl of 40B HTML pages and keep the top-ranked; pre-training runs on the top 40B, 80B, 120B and 160B tokens choose the cut, and the first iteration keeps 40B.
  2. Discover domains. The seed lacks diversity, so many math pages are missed. Call a domain math-related if over 10% of its pages were collected (for example mathoverflow.net) and have humans annotate its mathematical URL paths.
  3. Grow the seed, retrain, repeat. After four iterations the corpus is 35.5M pages and 120B tokens; in iteration 4 "nearly 98%" had already been collected in iteration 3, so collection stops. Contamination is removed by exact 10-gram matching against benchmarks.

The evidence is a controlled comparison: the same 1.3B model trained for 150B tokens on each candidate corpus. GSM8K and MATH (few-shot chain of thought) are 2.9% and 3.0% with no math training, 11.5% and 8.9% on OpenWebMath, 14.3% and 11.2% on Proof-Pile-2, and 23.8% and 13.6% on the 120.2B-token DeepSeekMath corpus [Tab. 1]. The corpus is about 9 times larger than OpenWebMath, the model keeps improving where repeated smaller corpora plateau, and it is multilingual, which lifts Chinese benchmarks where English-only corpora do not.

This is signal-versus-background selection with a data-driven signal sample: a clean, narrow seed as "signal", Common Crawl as background, and each round retraining on what the last round selected. The failure mode you know applies. The classifier recalls only what resembles its positives, so DeepSeek adds a second mechanism (domain statistics plus human URL annotation) to broaden the signal class, and watches saturation (the 98% overlap) to know when to stop. Selection also biases the corpus toward the seed's style, which the paper does not quantify.

Two findings from the study (1.3B models) are worth remembering. Code first helps math. 400B code tokens then 150B math tokens beat the same schedule with 400B general tokens first: GSM8K 21.9% versus 19.1%, MATH 15.3% versus 14.4%, and with Python 17.4% versus 14.3% and 9.4% versus 6.7% [Tab. 6]. That is why DeepSeekMath-Base 7B starts from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 and trains for 500B tokens (56% math corpus, 4% AlgebraicStack, 10% arXiv, 20% GitHub code, 10% natural language). arXiv papers do not help. Adding them "brings no notable improvements on all mathematical benchmarks adopted in this paper" (GSM8K 3.3% against 2.9% without math training). The base model reaches 64.2% on GSM8K and 36.2% on MATH, above Minerva 540B (58.8% and 33.6%): in a narrow domain, data quality can substitute for parameters.

GRPO enters, briefly

For RL, DeepSeekMath starts from DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B (SFT on 776K examples in chain-of-thought, program-of-thought and tool-integrated formats). PPO's value function "is typically another model of comparable size as the policy model", and it is hard to fit at every token when only the last token of a long solution is rewarded. Group Relative Policy Optimization 组相对策略优化 removes the critic: sample \(G\) outputs per question, score them, and use the group's own statistics as the baseline,

\[ \hat A_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}{\operatorname{std}(r_1, \dots, r_G)} \quad \text{for every token } t \text{ of output } i, \]

inside a PPO-style clipped objective, with the KL penalty to the reference policy in the loss (via the unbiased estimator \(\pi_{\text{ref}}/\pi_\theta - \log(\pi_{\text{ref}}/\pi_\theta) - 1\)) instead of in the reward [§4.1]. The paper argues that the group-relative advantage matches the comparative nature of reward models, which are trained on comparisons between outputs for the same question. The full objective is on page 24, together with the normalization variants later criticized (Dr. GRPO and others) and R1's rule-based rewards (R1).

The recipe: 144K chain-of-thought questions related to GSM8K and MATH, a reward model trained from the 7B base, policy learning rate \(10^{-6}\), KL coefficient 0.04, \(G = 64\), maximum length 1,024, batch 1,024, one policy update per exploration stage. GSM8K goes from 82.9% to 88.2%, MATH from 46.8% to 51.7%, and the out-of-domain CMATH from 84.6% to 88.8% [Tab. 5].

GRPO: a group baseline instead of a critic
Problem
PPO for LLMs needs a value model about the policy's size, and a per-token value is hard to learn from one terminal reward.
Idea
Sample \(G\) outputs per prompt and normalize each reward by the group mean and standard deviation; apply that advantage to every token of the output.
Why it works
The group mean is a per-prompt baseline from samples you already have, so no value network is trained.
Evidence
Instruct to RL 7B: GSM8K +5.3, MATH +4.9, CMATH +4.2 points; GRPO beats online rejection sampling in a controlled comparison (Fig. 5) [DeepSeekMath, §4.2, §5.2].
Cost
\(G\) rollouts per prompt; groups with identical rewards have zero advantage; std and length normalization introduce biases studied later (page 24).
Used by
V2 and Coder-V2 alignment, then R1 and V3 (page 28); now a default RLVR algorithm.

  • Data source: offline versus online. On a 1.3B model, online RFT matches RFT early and "gains an absolute advantage in the later stage": early on the policy still resembles the SFT model, later its own samples are the relevant distribution.
  • Gradient coefficient: sign and magnitude. Online RFT weights every correct answer 1 and never pushes down a wrong one. The paper also notes that all these methods "fully TRUST the signal of the reward function", while even the carefully annotated PRM800K has about 20% incorrect labels. Both points return in the debate on page 24 about whether RL adds capability.

Pitfall: 236B is total, 21B is per-token compute

Memory scales with the 236B (weights in bf16 are 471 GB; FP8 halves that), FLOPs per token with the 21B. That is why V2 trains cheaply per token yet still needs a full 8-GPU node of 80 GB cards to serve in bf16 (the model card asks for exactly that), and why MoE serving is memory-first (page 08).

V2 in training: data, schedule, layout, and alignment

Data and hyperparameters

The corpus has 8.1T tokens with the 67B's 100K byte-level BPE tokenizer. Relative to the 67B corpus it adds volume, "especially Chinese data" (Chinese tokens are about 12% more than English ones), recovers data that earlier cleaning "mistakenly deleted", improves the quality filter, and removes "contentious content" to reduce regional-culture bias [V2, §3.1.1]. No mixture proportions are given, so the data recipe cannot be reproduced from the report. Optimization is DeepSeek LLM's recipe plus a batch ramp: AdamW (\(\beta = (0.9, 0.95)\), weight decay 0.1), gradient clip 1.0, peak learning rate \(2.4 \times 10^{-4}\) after 2,000 warmup steps, multiplied by 0.316 at about 60% and again at about 90% of tokens, sequence length 4K, and a batch growing from 2,304 to 9,216 sequences (9.4M to 37.7M tokens) over the first 225B tokens [§3.1.2]. The ramp follows the critical-batch-size logic of page 13: small batches early are sample-efficient, large batches later cut the step count. Figure 2 places these settings against the fitted laws.

Layout, and the decision not to use tensor parallelism

V2 trains with HAI-LLM using "16-way zero-bubble pipeline parallelism, an 8-way expert parallelism, and ZeRO-1 data parallelism" on H800 nodes (8 GPUs on NVLink and NVSwitch, InfiniBand between nodes). The report's reason for omitting tensor parallelism is one sentence: "Given that DeepSeek-V2 has relatively few activated parameters, and a portion of the operators are recomputed to save activation memory, it can be trained without the necessity of tensor parallelism, thereby decreasing the communication overhead" [§3.1.3]. Shared-expert compute is also overlapped with the expert-parallel all-to-all, custom CUDA kernels handle communication, routing and fused expert matmuls, and MLA is built on an improved FlashAttention-2.

No tensor parallelism for V2 training
Problem
Tensor parallelism pays collectives inside every layer, on the critical path, over the fastest link in the machine (page 15). On H800s that link is NVLink at 400 GB/s per GPU, down from the H100's 900 GB/s [Zhao et al. 2025, §4.1].
Idea
Make the model small enough per GPU that TP is unnecessary: layers by pipeline (16 stages), experts by expert parallelism (8 ranks), optimizer state by ZeRO-1, and recompute cheap operators.
Why it works
Our arithmetic: routed experts (222.7B) over \(16 \times 8 = 128\) GPUs is 1.74B each, plus the 13.0B non-routed parameters over 16 stages, 0.81B each (replicated across EP ranks): about 2.55B parameters per GPU, 46 GB of training state at 18 bytes per parameter before ZeRO-1 shards the optimizer part. That fits in 80 GB. A dense 67B on the same 16-stage pipeline would hold 4.2B per GPU (76 GB), too tight without TP.
Evidence
The stated rationale and the achieved 172.8K GPU-hours per trillion tokens. For V3, the "Insights" paper says TP is avoided "due to its inefficiency under limited NVLink bandwidth" [§4.2]. V2 reports no with-versus-without-TP measurement.
Cost
Pipeline bubbles (zero-bubble scheduling), expert all-to-all (device-limited routing, overlap), and no headroom if the per-GPU model grows. Attention is not sharded, so it must fit on one GPU.
Used by
V3 (page 15), with DualPipe and 64-way EP. The reasoning does not carry to inference: V3's decode uses TP4 for attention (page 07).

The per-GPU figures are illustrations (the report gives no data-parallel degree or memory use). The DeepSeek LLM report lists tensor parallelism among HAI-LLM's tools without degrees, so "the 67B used TP" is an inference.

Long context

YaRN extends the window from 4K to 128K, applied only to the decoupled key \(k^R_t\) "as it is responsible for carrying RoPE" (the content path is position-free; page 07). Settings: \(s = 40\), \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 32\), target 160K (the config's max_position_embeddings is \(4096 \times 40 = 163{,}840\)), and an attention-entropy factor \(\sqrt t = 0.0707 \ln s + 1 = 1.26\) chosen to minimize perplexity. Training runs only 1,000 steps at 32K with 576 sequences per batch, about 18.9B tokens or 0.23% of 8.1T, yet needle-in-a-haystack tests pass up to 128K [§3.1.4, Fig. 4]. Page 10 covers YaRN and why a needle test is a weak criterion.

Alignment: SFT, then two-stage online GRPO

SFT uses 1.5M instances (1.2M helpfulness, 0.3M safety) for 2 epochs at \(5 \times 10^{-6}\) [§4.1]. A data-quantity finding (§4.4): earlier work argued fewer than 10K instances suffice, but "we observe a significant performance decline on the IFEval benchmark if we use fewer than 10K instances". RL uses GRPO (above) to avoid a critic as large as the policy, in two stages, because RL on reasoning data behaves differently from RL on general data: "the mathematical and coding abilities of our model can keep improving over a longer period of training steps".

  1. Reasoning alignment. A reward model for code and math scores outputs; code preferences come from compiler feedback, math preferences from ground-truth labels.
  2. Human preference alignment. A multi-reward sum \(r_i = c_1 RM_{\text{helpful}} + c_2 RM_{\text{safety}} + c_3 RM_{\text{rule}}\), with reward models initialized from V2 Chat (SFT).

Two findings shape what came after. Online beats offline: "the online approach significantly outperforms the offline approach", so the team built an online RL framework (a hybrid engine with separate parallel layouts for training and generation, vLLM rollouts, CPU-offload scheduling). This is the data-source lever of the unified paradigm. There is an alignment tax: preference RL raised open-ended scores (V2 Chat RL: 38.9 length-controlled AlpacaEval 2.0, 8.97 MT-Bench, 7.91 AlignBench) but "can negatively impact the performance on some standard benchmarks such as BBH". The contrast with DeepSeek LLM is instructive: offline DPO on a fixed preference set there, online GRPO with reward models here (page 21 covers online DPO).

"Saves 42.5% of training costs"

The inputs are 300.6K GPU-hours per trillion tokens for the dense 67B and 172.8K for V2, so \((300.6 - 172.8)/300.6 = 42.5\%\). Four checks:

  1. It is a rate, not a total. V2 trained on 8.1T tokens and the 67B on 2T, so V2's whole pre-training costs \(172.8\text{K} \times 8.1 = 1.40\)M GPU-hours against \(300.6\text{K} \times 2 = 0.60\)M (our arithmetic, assuming constant cost per token): 2.3 times more. "Cheaper" means cheaper per token, at a much stronger model (MMLU 78.5 versus 71.3).
  2. It falls short of the FLOP saving. With \(6N\) on activated parameters, V2 needs \(21/67.4 = 31\%\) of the 67B's FLOPs per token, a 69% cut, yet hours fell 42.5%. Against a 989 TFLOP/s BF16 peak (V2's training precision is not stated; we assume BF16), the 67B runs at \(6 \cdot 67.4\text{B} \cdot 10^{12} / (300.6\text{K} \cdot 3600\ \text{s}) = 374\) TFLOP/s per GPU, about 38% MFU, and V2 at \(6 \cdot 21\text{B} \cdot 10^{12} / (172.8\text{K} \cdot 3600) = 203\) TFLOP/s, about 20%. With attention included, using the "Insights" paper's 155 GFLOPs per token for V2 at length 4,096 [Zhao et al. 2025, Tab. 2], it is 249 TFLOP/s, about 25%. The gap is the price of sparsity: all-to-all traffic, small expert matmuls (width 1,536), load imbalance with token dropping, and pipeline bubbles, which is why device-limited routing and balance losses exist.
  3. Conditions differ beyond architecture. Different layouts, data and code maturity, all DeepSeek's own measurements. Seven months later V3 reports 180K GPU-hours per trillion tokens at 1.76 times V2's activated size [V3, §1], so part of the ratio is 2024 infrastructure rather than MoE.
  4. Exclusions. Pre-training tokens only: not the 32K extension (about 19B tokens), SFT, RL, ablations or failed runs.

"Reduces the KV cache by 93.3%"

The 67B (GQA, 8 KV heads, 95 layers) caches \(95 \cdot 2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 = 194{,}560\) elements per token, 380 KiB in bf16. V2 caches \(60 \cdot (512 + 64) = 34{,}560\). Then:

\[ \underbrace{1 - \frac{34{,}560 \cdot 2}{194{,}560 \cdot 2}}_{\text{MLA alone, both bf16}} = 82.2\%, \qquad \underbrace{1 - \frac{34{,}560 \cdot 6/8}{194{,}560 \cdot 2}}_{\text{MLA plus 6-bit cache}} = 1 - \frac{25{,}920\ \text{B}}{389{,}120\ \text{B}} = 93.3\%. \]

The second line reproduces the abstract to three digits, and Figure 1(b) of the report draws V2's bar near 25 KB on a 0 to 400 KB axis. So the 93.3% multiplies an architecture (a cache 5.6 times smaller than GQA-8) by a serving technique: the deployed V2 compresses "each element in its KV cache into 6 bits on average" [§3.2.3]. The baseline is already GQA (against a multi-head model of the same shape, \(2 \cdot 64 \cdot 128 \cdot 95 = 1{,}556{,}480\) elements, MLA would save 97.8%), and quantization could equally be applied to the 67B: a 6-bit 67B cache is 142.5 KiB per token, leaving MLA an advantage of \(142.5/25.3 = 5.6\times\) rather than 15×. This is no criticism of MLA, whose quality is tested in the ablations on page 07; it is a reminder to ask what is in the numerator.

"5.76 times the maximum generation throughput"

For serving, V2's parameters are converted to FP8 and its KV cache to about 6 bits, and on "a single node with 8 H800 GPUs" it generates over 50K tokens per second, 5.76 times the 67B's maximum, using "the prompt and generation length distribution from the actually deployed DeepSeek 67B service" [§3.2.3]. The implied 67B figure is about 8.7K tokens per second or more. Three ingredients are confounded. Compute: 21B versus 67B activated parameters is 3.2 times fewer FLOPs per token. Weight traffic is different from FLOPs: at the large batches a throughput maximum measures, a decode step touches nearly every expert, so bytes streamed per step approach the full 236B (about 236 GB in FP8, against 135 GB for the 67B in bf16) and the "activated parameters" bandwidth advantage is gone (page 17). MoE gains because that traffic is amortized over a much larger batch. Cache capacity: that batch fits because the cache is 15 times smaller per token. On 8 GPUs of 80 GB (our arithmetic, ignoring activations), V2's 236 GB of FP8 weights leave \((640 - 236)\,\text{GB} / 25.3\,\text{KiB} \approx 15.6\)M cached tokens, while the 67B in bf16 leaves \((640 - 135)\,\text{GB} / 380\,\text{KiB} \approx 1.3\)M, 12 times fewer. The baseline's precision is not stated, and a throughput maximum describes the large-batch regime, not per-request latency. No ablation attributes the 5.76× to its parts.

How to answer "evaluate this claim"

State the ratio's numerator and denominator, reconstruct it from config and arithmetic, list what differs between the two sides (model, tokens, precision, hardware, software, workload), and name what is excluded. Then say what you would run to isolate the component you care about: an MLA-versus-GQA ablation at equal tokens and cache precision, a same-precision serving benchmark at matched latency, a utilization comparison normalized by FLOPs.

Coder-V2, V2.5, and the hand-off to V3

DeepSeek-Coder-V2 (17 June 2024) shows how cheaply the V2 architecture could be reused. It is "further pre-trained from an intermediate checkpoint of DeepSeek-V2 with additional 6 trillion tokens": 60% code (1,170B tokens from GitHub and Common Crawl, mined with the DeepSeekMath pipeline), 10% math (221B tokens, about double the DeepSeekMath corpus) and 30% natural language sampled from V2's own corpus, to preserve general ability [Coder-V2, §1-2]. Total exposure is 10.2T tokens (4.2T from V2's data plus the 6T). Languages grow from 86 to 338, the window from 16K to 128K, and there are two sizes: 236B with 21B activated, and a 16B with 2.4B activated (the V2-Lite shape). Alignment reuses the recipe: SFT on code and math from the Coder and Math projects plus V2's general data, then GRPO with compiler and test-case feedback and a reward model. The report claims parity with GPT-4-Turbo on coding (HumanEval 90.2%) and 75.7% on MATH, on the authors' own evaluations. The design lesson is the mixture: a code-heavy continuation that keeps 30% general text preserves general ability (MMLU 79.2 under the simple-evals pipeline, against V2's 78.5 in a different harness).

DeepSeek-V2.5 (5 September 2024) merges the two lines: "an upgraded version that combines DeepSeek-V2-Chat and DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct" per the model card, with both API names (deepseek-chat and deepseek-coder) now reaching it and function calling, FIM and JSON output unchanged. The model card moves AlpacaEval 2.0 from 46.6 (V2-0628) and 44.5 (Coder-V2-0724) to 50.5, ArenaHard from 68.3 and 66.3 to 76.2, and HumanEval (Python) from 84.5 and 87.2 to 89.0. V2.5-1210 (10 December 2024) reports MATH-500 from 74.8% to 82.8% and LiveCodeBench from 29.2% to 34.4%. No new pre-training is reported for either.

What changed architecturally? We diffed the released configs. V2.5 and the 236B Coder-V2 are identical to each other and differ from V2 only in the YaRN entries mscale and mscale_all_dim (0.707 in V2, 1.0 in both) plus an ep_size field. In the released code the attention logits are scaled by \((0.1 \cdot \text{mscale} \cdot \ln s + 1)^2\): V2's 0.707 reproduces the report's coefficient 0.0707 (\(\sqrt t = 1.26\) at \(s = 40\)), while 1.0 restores the original YaRN coefficient 0.1 (1.37; V3's config also uses 1.0, page 10). So the V2.5 generation appears to have retuned the long-context attention temperature and nothing else in the architecture.

What stayed fixed from V2 to V3

MLA's constants (\(d_c = 512\), \(d_c' = 1{,}536\), \(d_h^R = 64\), 128 heads of dimension 128, per the released V2 and V3 configs) and the fine-grained plus shared expert recipe. V3 scales width to 7,168 and experts to 1 shared plus 256 routed with 8 active, replaces the three balance losses with bias-based balancing, and adds multi-token prediction, FP8 training and DualPipe: page 27. The line then continues through R1, V3.1, V3.2 (sparse attention) and V4 (compressed attention, mHC residuals, Muon): page 28. What persists longest from this page: MLA, fine-grained plus shared experts, GRPO, and designing around the interconnect.

The DeepSeek research style

Four habits recur across these papers. Each is stated with its source and its limit, because "DeepSeek is efficient and open" is a sentence an interviewer will ask you to back up.

The pattern behind the choices

Ask what is scarce, then design against it. Memory capacity and bandwidth at decode gave GQA at 67B and then MLA. FLOPs per token in training gave DeepSeekMoE. Interconnect bandwidth gave device-limited routing, no tensor parallelism and overlap of shared experts with all-to-all. Critic memory in RL gave GRPO. Compute wasted on a wrong hyperparameter or a wrong model size gave the scaling laws. When an interviewer asks "why did DeepSeek do X", the answer usually starts with which resource X saves and ends with the number that shows it.

Common misconceptions

  • "V2 is 42.5% cheaper to train than DeepSeek 67B." It needs 42.5% fewer GPU-hours per trillion tokens. V2 trained on 8.1T tokens against 2T, so its whole pre-training took about 1.40M GPU-hours against 0.60M, 2.3 times as many. And a 69% cut in FLOPs per token became a 42.5% cut in GPU-hours because utilization fell (about 38% to 20 to 25% MFU by our estimate).
  • "MLA alone gives V2 a 93.3% smaller cache." MLA against the 67B's GQA-8 cache is 82.2% at equal precision. The rest is the 6-bit KV quantization used in deployment. The baseline is GQA, not MHA.
  • "V2 has 21B parameters." It has 235.7B parameters, of which about 20.3B to 21.4B are activated per token depending on whether the LM head and input embedding are counted. Reports do not state their convention, and DeepSeek's own reports are not consistent with each other.
  • "DeepSeek's batch-size and learning-rate laws are universal." They were fitted on dense models between \(10^{17}\) and \(2 \times 10^{19}\) FLOPs as functions of \(C\) only, then extrapolated by five orders of magnitude. The paper itself notes the optimum shifts with the model-versus-data split, an independent refit differs in the learning-rate law by a factor of 2 to 3 (page 13), and V2's own settings sit off the line.
  • "6ND is exact enough for scaling studies." DeepSeek showed \(6N\) mis-states cost by up to about 50% for small models, and that the sign of the error flips depending on whether embeddings are counted. Their \(M\) fixes this for MHA dense models and needs adapting for GQA and MoE.
  • "GRPO was invented for R1." It was introduced in DeepSeekMath in February 2024, with a learned reward model, and used for V2's alignment. Rule-based verifiable rewards and the reasoning-model recipe came with R1 (page 24).
  • "Fine-grained plus shared experts always win." DeepSeekMoE 16B lagged its dense comparator on multiple-choice tasks, which the authors tie to its 0.5B attention parameters, and the 145B run trailed a dense 67B trained on the same 245B tokens on MMLU (39.4 versus 45.1). Later models split on shared experts (page 08).
  • "A step schedule is a worse cosine." On the one comparison reported (1.6B parameters, 100B tokens) the two matched. The step schedule was chosen because its first stage can be reused for longer runs and for scaling-law experiments.

Warm-up How does DeepSeek LLM 67B differ from Llama 2 70B?

The skeleton is the same: pre-norm RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, and GQA with 8 KV heads at this size. The differences: shape, 95 layers with FFN width 22,016 (\(2.69d\)) against 80 layers and 28,672 (\(3.5d\)), a move from width to depth that the report justifies as "aiming for better performance" and easier pipeline partitioning, without an ablation; tokenizer, 102,400 bilingual byte-level BPE against 32,000; data, 2T English and Chinese tokens with deduplication across 91 Common Crawl dumps; schedule, multi-step (31.6% at 80% of tokens, 10% at 90%) against cosine; hyperparameters from its own laws (batch 4,608 sequences, about 18.9M tokens, peak learning rate \(3.2 \times 10^{-4}\)); and post-training with SFT plus DPO. Parameter counts are close (67.4B against 69.0B from the configs). In DeepSeek's harness the base model scored MMLU 71.3 against 69.0, GSM8K 63.4 against 58.4 and HumanEval 42.7 against 28.7.

Warm-up State DeepSeek-V2's headline numbers and what each one is compared with.

236B total and 21B activated parameters, 8.1T training tokens, 128K context. Core What did DeepSeek LLM contribute to scaling-law methodology?

Four things. (1) Hyperparameters as laws of compute. Grid searches over batch size and learning rate at budgets from \(10^{17}\) to \(2 \times 10^{19}\) FLOPs, treating settings within 0.25% of the best error as near-optimal, gave \(\eta_{\text{opt}} = 0.3118\,C^{-0.1250}\) and \(B_{\text{opt}} = 0.2920\,C^{0.3271}\), validated at \(10^{20}\) and used to set the 7B and 67B (both within 10% of the laws). This makes each point of a scaling study properly tuned. (2) Model scale \(M\). Non-embedding FLOPs per token, \(72Ld^2 + 12Ldl_{\text{seq}}\), with \(C = MD\), replacing \(N\) and \(6ND\): \(6N_1\) and \(6N_2\) are off by up to about 50% for small models, in opposite directions, and refits in \(M\) predicted the 7B and 67B losses best. (3) Data quality moves the allocation. The model exponent was 0.450, 0.524 and 0.578 on three datasets of rising quality, against 0.49 for Chinchilla and 0.73 for Kaplan, offered as a candidate explanation for their disagreement. (4) Cheap experiments through a multi-step learning rate whose first stage is reused across budgets. They predicted the 7B and 67B losses from runs with about 1,000 times less compute.

Limits: dense models only; fitted below \(2 \times 10^{19}\) and applied at \(10^{24}\); \(C\) alone does not determine the optimum (the paper says so); the data-quality explanation is a hypothesis without a controlled test.

Core Using DeepSeek LLM's laws, what batch size and learning rate would you start from for a \(10^{24}\)-FLOP run? How does V2 compare, and how far do you trust the answer?

\(\eta = 0.3118 \cdot (10^{24})^{-0.125} = 0.3118 \cdot 10^{-3} = 3.1 \times 10^{-4}\). \(B = 0.2920 \cdot 10^{0.3271 \cdot 24} = 0.2920 \cdot 10^{7.850} = 2.07 \times 10^{7}\) tokens, about 5,050 sequences of 4,096. For a 2T-token run that is about 97,000 steps. Each 10× in compute multiplies \(B\) by 2.12 and \(\eta\) by 0.75.

V2's compute, taking \(C \approx 6 N_{\text{act}} D = 6 \cdot 20.3\text{B} \cdot 8.1\text{T} \approx 9.9 \times 10^{23}\), is almost exactly this. V2 used a peak rate of \(2.4 \times 10^{-4}\) (0.77× the law) and a batch ramping from 9.4M to 37.7M tokens (1.8× the law at the end). The report does not say the laws were used, and \(C\) for a MoE is a modeling choice. Trust: as a starting point for a sweep, yes. The laws were fitted on dense models, extrapolated about 4.7 orders of magnitude beyond the fit, ignore the model-versus-data split, and an independent refit finds a learning-rate exponent 0.11 different, a factor of 2 to 3 in the predicted rate at large scale. I would run a coarse \(2\times\) grid of \(B\) and \(\eta\) at a small proxy and check that the optimum has the predicted shape, and use \(\mu\)P or a per-width check if the architecture differs from a plain dense transformer (page 14).

Core Why did DeepSeek use a multi-step learning-rate schedule instead of cosine?

Cosine depends on the total token budget, so any change of budget restarts the run. A step schedule holds the peak for 80% of tokens, then drops to 31.6% and 10% of the peak at 80% and 90%. Its first stage is therefore shared by all runs with larger budgets: continued training extends stage one, and the scaling-law grid of many small runs was trained by "reusing the first stage". On a 1.6B model with 100B tokens the final performance matched cosine ("essentially consistent"), and other stage splits were slightly better, but 80/10/10 was kept to balance reuse against performance. The factor 0.316 is \(1/\sqrt{10}\). V2 moved the first drop to about 60%. It is the same idea as warmup-stable-decay (page 14). Caveat: one small-scale comparison, so "matches cosine" is weak evidence at 67B or 236B.

Core Summarize DeepSeekMoE's two ideas and the evidence for them.

Diagnosis: with 8 to 16 large experts, each expert mixes unrelated knowledge (hybridity), and experts duplicate common knowledge (redundancy). Fine-grained segmentation: split each expert into \(m\) smaller ones and activate \(m\) times as many, at constant parameters and FLOPs, which multiplies the number of expert combinations (from 120 to \(4.4 \times 10^9\) in the paper's example). Shared expert isolation: a few always-on experts hold common knowledge, so routed experts can specialize.

Evidence: at 2B parameters and 100B tokens, Pile loss 1.808 against GShard's 1.867, matching GShard with 1.5× the expert parameters and approaching the dense-with-16×-FFN upper bound (1.806); disabling the shared expert raises the loss to 2.414. At 16B on 2T tokens it matches dense DeepSeek 7B and Llama 2 7B at about 40% of the FLOPs. At 145B on 245B tokens it matches a dense 67B trained on the same tokens at 28.5% of the FLOPs and beats GShard 137B (1.876 against 1.961).

Limits: baselines re-implemented by the same team; 16B is weak on multiple-choice benchmarks (MMLU 45.0 against 48.2), tied to its 0.5B attention parameters; the 145B run is preliminary; whether shared experts still help at hundreds of experts is contested (Qwen3 dropped them; page 08).

Core What is DeepSeekMath's "unified paradigm" view of post-training methods?

Every method updates \(\theta\) along \(\mathbb E_{(q,o) \sim \mathcal D}\big[\tfrac{1}{|o|}\sum_t GC(q,o,t,\pi_{rf})\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_t \mid q, o_{<t})\big]\). The methods differ in three components: the data source \(\mathcal D\) (fixed SFT data, samples from the frozen SFT model, or samples from the live policy), the reward function (none, a rule such as answer correctness, or a reward model), and the gradient coefficient. SFT: coefficient 1. RFT: \(\mathbb 1[\text{correct}]\) on offline samples. Online RFT: the same on online samples. DPO: a sigmoid of the implicit-reward gap, positive on the chosen and negative on the rejected output, on offline pairs. PPO: the GAE advantage, online. GRPO: the group-normalized advantage \((r_i - \bar r)/\text{std}\) plus a KL term \(\beta(\pi_{\text{ref}}/\pi_\theta - 1)\), online.

The paper's experiments vary one axis at a time: online beat offline sampling, graded and signed coefficients (GRPO) beat binary ones that never penalize wrong answers (online RFT), step-level (process) rewards beat outcome-only, and iterative RL helped mostly in its first round. RL improved Maj@K but not Pass@K, so the paper reads it as sharpening rather than adding capability. Interview use: given a new algorithm, say what its data source, reward and coefficient are and you can predict its failure modes.

Core Walk through DeepSeek-V2's architecture and explain each efficiency choice.

60 layers, \(d = 5{,}120\), pre-norm RMSNorm. Attention is MLA with 128 heads: the KV is compressed to a 512-dimensional latent, queries to 1,536, RoPE lives in a separate 64-dimensional decoupled key shared by all heads. The cache is \(512 + 64 = 576\) numbers per token per layer, equal to GQA with 2.25 groups, while quality matches or beats MHA in the ablations. Weight absorption makes decode an MQA over the latent (page 07). The FFN is DeepSeekMoE in layers 2 to 60 (layer 1 is dense): 2 shared plus 160 routed experts of width 1,536, top-6 with softmax gates. Only 21B of 236B parameters are active, so training FLOPs per token are that of a 21B model, and the activated FFN width \((6+2) \cdot 1{,}536 = 12{,}288\) equals a dense layer's.

Systems choices follow. Device-limited routing (each token's experts on at most 3 of 8 device groups) caps all-to-all traffic. Three balance losses (expert, device, communication) plus device-level token dropping keep load even. Training uses 16-way zero-bubble pipeline parallelism, 8-way expert parallelism and ZeRO-1 with recomputation and no tensor parallelism, and overlaps shared experts with all-to-all. Long context comes from YaRN on the decoupled key only, 1,000 steps at 32K. Serving uses FP8 weights and a 6-bit KV cache. Alignment is SFT then two-stage online GRPO. The question behind each: what is scarce (cache bytes, FLOPs, interconnect) and what is the number that shows the saving.

Core Why did DeepSeek avoid tensor parallelism in V2 training?

The report's reason: V2 "has relatively few activated parameters" and recomputes some operators to save activation memory, so it "can be trained without the necessity of tensor parallelism, thereby decreasing the communication overhead". Tensor parallelism puts all-reduces inside every layer, on the critical path, over NVLink, and the H800's NVLink is 400 GB/s against the H100's 900 GB/s. The later V3 hardware paper says outright that TP is avoided "due to its inefficiency under limited NVLink bandwidth".

Arithmetic that shows it is feasible: routed experts (222.7B) over 16 pipeline stages times 8 expert-parallel ranks is 1.74B parameters per GPU, plus 13.0B of other parameters over 16 stages (0.81B each), about 2.55B per GPU, 46 GB of training state at 18 bytes per parameter before ZeRO-1 sharding. A dense 67B with a 16-way pipeline would hold 4.2B per GPU (76 GB), too tight without TP. The price is pipeline bubbles and expert all-to-all, which is why V2 uses zero-bubble scheduling, device-limited routing and shared-expert overlap. TP is not banned everywhere: at inference V3 uses TP4 for attention.

Core Reconcile DeepSeek-V2's 236B total and 21B activated parameters from its config.

Config: 60 layers, \(d = 5{,}120\), 128 heads, \(d_c = 512\), \(d_c' = 1{,}536\), \(d_h = 128\), \(d_h^R = 64\), dense FFN 12,288 in layer 1, 59 MoE layers with 160 routed and 2 shared experts of width 1,536, top-6, vocabulary 102,400 untied. Expert: \(3 \cdot 5{,}120 \cdot 1{,}536 = 23.59\)M. Routed per MoE layer: \(160 \times 23.59\text{M} = 3.775\)B, so 222.7B in 59 layers. Shared: 2.78B. MLA per layer: 149.2M (\(7.86 + 37.75 + 2.95 + 16.78 + 83.89\)M plus norms), 8.95B in 60 layers. Dense FFN: 189M. Embeddings: \(2 \cdot 102{,}400 \cdot 5{,}120 = 1.05\)B. Sum: 235.74B, matching the checkpoint metadata exactly.

Activated: attention 8.95B + dense layer 0.19B + shared 2.78B + \(59 \cdot 6 \cdot 23.59\text{M} = 8.35\)B routed + routers 0.05B = 20.33B without embeddings, 20.85B with the LM head, 21.38B with both matrices. The reported 21B is inside that range and the report does not say which convention it uses, so state yours.

Deep-dive How would you evaluate the claim "V2 saves 42.5% of training cost compared with DeepSeek 67B"?

Reconstruct it: \(1 - 172.8/300.6 = 42.5\%\), where both numbers are GPU-hours per trillion tokens on the H800 cluster. Then interrogate the denominator and conditions. (1) It is a rate. V2 trained on 8.1T tokens and the 67B on 2T, so whole pre-training is \(172.8\text{K} \times 8.1 = 1.40\)M against \(300.6\text{K} \times 2 = 0.60\)M GPU-hours. What was saved is cost per token, at a much stronger model (MMLU 78.5 against 71.3). (2) Compare with the FLOP ratio. Activated parameters 21B against 67.4B imply 69% fewer FLOPs per token, but only 42.5% fewer hours, so utilization dropped: at a 989 TFLOP/s BF16 peak, about 38% MFU for the 67B and 20 to 25% for V2 depending on how attention FLOPs are counted (\(6N\) versus the 155 GFLOPs per token in DeepSeek's later hardware paper). Plausible reasons are all-to-all communication, small expert matmuls, load imbalance with token dropping, and pipeline bubbles, which the report does not break down. (3) Confounds: different data, parallel layouts and code maturity (V3, seven months later, reports 180K GPU-hours per trillion tokens at 1.76× V2's activated size). (4) Exclusions: context extension, SFT, RL, ablations.

To isolate MoE from everything else I would run both models with the same framework, precision and sequence length on the same cluster, report MFU alongside hours, and compare at matched loss rather than matched tokens.

Deep-dive Design question: you must pretrain a 200B-class MoE on GPUs whose intra-node interconnect is weak and whose memory is 80 GB. Which V2 choices would you carry over, and what would you verify first?

Carry over, each tied to a constraint. (1) No tensor parallelism: with about 20B activated parameters and about 2.5B parameters per GPU under PP16 by EP8, TP's per-layer all-reduces on a slow link cost more than they save. Check per-GPU memory first (state at 18 bytes per parameter plus activations, with recomputation of norms and up-projections). (2) Fine-grained plus shared experts with device-limited routing (\(M \approx 3\) of 8 groups): the all-to-all volume is proportional to the number of devices a token touches, so bound it. Measure the fraction of tokens whose unrestricted top-\(k\) would violate the limit and the loss cost of the restriction at small scale. (3) Balance: expert- and device-level losses, plus a communication-balance term if receive-side skew shows up in profiles; in a newer design consider bias-based balancing (page 08). (4) Overlap shared-expert compute with all-to-all. (5) MLA if long-context serving is a goal, otherwise GQA-8 is a simpler baseline (page 07).

Deviate or verify: token dropping trades quality for stragglers, so try dropless kernels first. Fit batch size and learning rate on a proxy rather than borrowing DeepSeek's dense-fit constants. Compare a zero-bubble pipeline with an interleaved one under your bubble budget. And log MFU per phase from day one, because V2's 42.5% saving came with a large drop in utilization that a design review should expect. What I would ask before committing: which link is slowest, what fraction of step time is exposed communication, and how does that scale if I double experts.

Deep-dive How would you build a domain corpus the way DeepSeekMath did, and what could go wrong?

Pipeline: pick a clean seed set (OpenWebMath) as positives and random web pages as negatives; train a cheap classifier (fastText, 256-d, word 3-grams); score a deduplicated crawl (40B pages); keep the top-ranked pages; find domains where over 10% of pages were selected, annotate math URL paths by hand, add those pages to the positives, retrain; repeat until new rounds add little (in iteration four 98% was already collected). Result: 35.5M pages, 120B tokens. Validate with a controlled test: train the same small model (1.3B for 150B tokens) on each candidate corpus and compare downstream scores (GSM8K 23.8% against 11.5% for OpenWebMath and 14.3% for Proof-Pile-2), and choose the cut-off size from such runs (top 40B, 80B, 120B, 160B tokens).

Failure modes: the classifier recalls only what resembles its seed, so the corpus inherits the seed's style and misses different math; the fix here (domain statistics plus human URL annotation) adds labor and bias. Saturation is measured against the classifier's own recall, not the true coverage. Contamination: the paper removes exact 10-gram matches, which does not catch paraphrases. Ablations can surprise: code before math helped (21.9% against 19.1% GSM8K against a general-data first stage) and arXiv text did not, so verify each source separately rather than assuming "more math-looking text" is better.

Cheat sheet

  • Timeline (arXiv v1): DeepSeek LLM 5 Jan 2024 (weights Nov 2023) · DeepSeekMoE 11 Jan · Coder 25 Jan · Math 5 Feb · V2 7 May · Coder-V2 17 Jun · V2.5 5 Sep (V2.5-1210 on 10 Dec).
  • DeepSeek LLM: 7B = 30 layers, \(d\) 4,096, MHA; 67B = 95 layers, \(d\) 8,192, GQA-8, FFN 22,016 (Llama 2 70B: 80 layers, 28,672). 2T tokens; batch 2,304 and 4,608 sequences of 4,096; peak LR \(4.2\times10^{-4}\) and \(3.2\times10^{-4}\); multi-step LR (31.6% at 80%, 10% at 90%, 2,000 warmup steps); SFT 1.5M instances, then DPO.
  • Laws: \(\eta = 0.3118\,C^{-0.1250}\), \(B = 0.2920\,C^{0.3271}\) tokens, \(C = MD\), \(M = 72Ld^2 + 12Ldl\); fitted \(10^{17}\) to \(2\times10^{19}\). At \(10^{22}\): 4.59M tokens, \(5.5\times10^{-4}\). At \(10^{24}\): 20.7M tokens, \(3.1\times10^{-4}\). Per 10× compute: batch ×2.12, LR ×0.75. Allocation: \(M_{\text{opt}} = 0.1715\,C^{0.5243}\), \(D_{\text{opt}} = 5.8316\,C^{0.4757}\); model exponent 0.450, 0.524, 0.578 as data quality rises.
  • DeepSeekMoE: fine-grained (\(m\times\) more, smaller experts) plus shared experts. 2B: Pile loss 1.808 vs GShard 1.867. 16B (2 + 64, top-6, 16.4B/2.8B): about dense 7B at 40% FLOPs, weak on multiple choice (0.5B attention). 145B (4 + 128, top-12, 144.6B/22.2B): about dense 67B (same 245B tokens) at 28.5% FLOPs.
  • DeepSeekMath: corpus by 4 fastText iterations, 35.5M pages, 120B tokens; base from Coder-v1.5 + 500B tokens: GSM8K 64.2, MATH 36.2. Code before math helps, arXiv does not. GRPO: \(G = 64\), KL 0.04, LR \(10^{-6}\); \(\hat A = (r - \bar r)/\text{std}\). Unified paradigm: gradient \(= \mathbb E_{\mathcal D}[\tfrac1{|o|}\sum_t GC\,\nabla\log\pi]\); SFT 1, RFT \(\mathbb 1[\text{correct}]\), DPO sigmoid, PPO \(A_t\), GRPO \(\hat A + \beta(\pi_{\text{ref}}/\pi - 1)\). RL raises Maj@K, not Pass@K.
  • V2: 60 layers, \(d\) 5,120, 128 heads, \(d_c\) 512, \(d_c'\) 1,536, \(d_h^R\) 64 (576 cached per token per layer); layer 1 dense (12,288), then 2 shared + 160 routed experts (1,536), top-6, \(M = 3\) of 8 device groups; \(\alpha = 0.003, 0.05, 0.02\); token dropping (capacity 1.0). 8.1T tokens; peak LR \(2.4\times10^{-4}\); batch 2,304 to 9,216 sequences over 225B tokens; PP16 zero-bubble + EP8 + ZeRO-1, no TP; YaRN \(s = 40\) on \(k^R\), 1,000 steps at 32K. 1.5M SFT, two-stage online GRPO.
  • V2 parameters: total 235,741,434,880 (routed experts 222.7B = 94.5%); activated 20.33B non-embedding, 20.85B with LM head, 21.38B with both matrices; attention 8.95B = 44% of activated. Expert = 3 × 5,120 × 1,536 = 23.59M.
  • Claims: 42.5% = \(1 - 172.8/300.6\) GPU-hours per trillion tokens (whole run: 1.40M vs 0.60M GPU-hours; implied MFU about 38% vs 20 to 25%). 93.3% = \(1 - 25{,}920/389{,}120\) (MLA alone 82.2%; 6-bit cache does the rest; baseline is GQA-8, 380 KiB/token). 5.76× = over 50K tokens/s on 8×H800, V2 in FP8.
  • After V2: Coder-V2 = 6T more tokens on a V2 checkpoint (60/10/30 code/math/text); V2.5 = merge, configs differ from V2 only in YaRN mscale (0.707 to 1.0). MLA constants persist into V3.
  • Style: design around scarce resources (H800 NVLink 400 GB/s vs H100 900), lead with cost ratios, release weights and configs, report failures (MC data excluded, arXiv no gain, PRM and MCTS failed).
