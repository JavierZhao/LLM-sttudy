DeepSeek-V3 (December 2024) is a 671B-parameter mixture-of-experts model that activates 37B parameters per token and was trained on 14.8T tokens in 2.788M H800 GPU-hours. This page walks the technical report in order (architecture, multi-token prediction, infrastructure, FP8 training, data and hyperparameters, long context, post-training, cost) and gives the reason behind each decision, linking to the mechanism pages instead of re-deriving them.

V3 at a glance: what was inherited, what is new

DeepSeek-V3 is the DeepSeek-V2 recipe (MLA for attention, DeepSeekMoE for the feed-forward layers, both "thoroughly validated" in V2 according to the report; see page 26) scaled up and hardened with four additions [DeepSeek-V3, §1, §2.1]. First, auxiliary-loss-free load balancing 无辅助损失负载均衡. Second, a multi-token prediction objective 多词元预测. Third, FP8 mixed-precision training 混合精度训练 at a scale where the report says it is validated "for the first time". Fourth, a training system built around the cost of moving expert activations between nodes: the DualPipe schedule for pipeline parallelism 流水线并行 plus custom all-to-all kernels. The report calls the result "our optimized co-design of algorithms, frameworks, and hardware", the right frame for interviews: nothing in V3 is a stand-alone trick, and each part answers a specific bottleneck (KV cache: MLA; FLOPs: sparse MoE; router collapse: bias balancing; signal per token: MTP; matmul throughput and memory: FP8; all-to-all: node-limited routing and DualPipe).

Key idea: three levers

Read every V3 decision as pulling one of three levers. Compute: sparse activation (37B of 671B), FP8 matmuls, and the extra training signal of MTP. Communication: node-limited routing, FP8 dispatch, DualPipe overlap and hand-written kernels, because MoE turns each layer into an all-to-all across a cluster whose scale-out network is 3.2× slower than its scale-up network. Memory: MLA, recomputation, FP8 and BF16 storage, and a parallel layout that needs no tensor parallelism. When a question starts with "why did they...", name the lever first.

Architecture: each component and the reason for it

V3 keeps V2's block: pre-norm transformer layers with MLA for attention and DeepSeekMoE for the feed-forward layers [DeepSeek-V3, §2.1]. The report treats this as a recap. For an interview the useful question is what each part is for.

MLA: the cache is the constraint

Serving cost at long context is dominated by KV bytes read per generated token (page 06). MLA caches one 512-dimensional latent plus one 64-dimensional RoPE key per token per layer, 576 values, which is 70,272 bytes per token across 61 layers, less than Llama-3-8B's 131,072 although V3 has over 80 times as many total parameters (page 07). The query is compressed too (\(d_c' = 1536\)), but only "to reduce the activation memory during training", since queries are never cached. RMSNorm layers follow the latents, with extra scaling factors at the width bottlenecks, as in V2 [§4.2]. Position lives only in the 64-dimensional decoupled key, so YaRN touches only that key (below).

DeepSeekMoE: 671B stored, 37B computed

Each MoE layer has one shared expert used by every token and 256 routed experts of which the router picks 8, each a SwiGLU network of width 2048 (\(3 \times 7168 \times 2048 = 44{,}040{,}192\) parameters). The nine active experts do \(9 \times 2048 = 18{,}432\) units of hidden width of work, exactly the \(d_{ff}\) of the three dense layers, so an MoE layer costs the FLOPs of a dense layer of that width while holding 257 experts. Experts hold 656.5B of the 671.0B parameters (97.8%), only \(9/257 = 3.5\%\) of them are active per token, and the always-active parts bring the activated total to 5.3% to 5.6% of the model (page 08). Four other choices:

  • Three dense layers first. The report gives no reason. Circumstantial evidence on page 08: balance converged slowest in the first layer of DeepSeekMoE, and V4 later replaces these layers with hash-routed MoE layers.
  • Sigmoid affinities, normalized over the selected experts. V2 used a softmax over all experts. V3 computes \(s_{i,t} = \sigma(u_t^\top e_i)\) per expert and divides the 8 selected scores by their sum [§2.1.2]. The report gives no rationale. Our reading: each affinity stays in \((0,1)\), the units in which the balancing bias is added, and renormalization keeps the selected gates summing to 1.
  • Node-limited routing, \(M = 4\). The 256 experts form 8 groups of 32 (one node each in training). A group scores as the sum of its top \(K_r/M = 2\) biased affinities, the best 4 groups survive, and the top 8 experts are taken inside them. NVLink offers 160 GB/s and IB 50 GB/s, so a token crosses IB to at most 4 nodes and NVLink forwards it onward. A token can use about 3.2 experts per node at no extra NVLink cost, so the same communication would support \(4 \times 3.2 = 12.8\), about 13, routed experts [§3.2.2] (page 08 has the routing-freedom cost).
  • No token dropping in training or inference [§2.1.2]: no capacity factor to tune and no train-inference mismatch from dropping.

Load balancing: a bias controller plus a tiny sequence-wise loss

Auxiliary balance losses fight the language-modeling loss: too small and routing collapses, too large and the interference gradients hurt quality. V3 removes the balance term from the gradient. Each routed expert has a bias \(b_i\) added to its affinity only when choosing the top-\(k\); the gate multiplied into the expert output still comes from the unbiased \(s_{i,t}\) [§2.1.2, Eq. 16]. After every step, an expert overloaded over the whole batch has its bias lowered by \(\gamma\) and an underloaded one raised by \(\gamma\): a sign-based integral controller on load error (derived on page 08).

Why keep a loss at all? The controller balances a batch. A sequence made entirely of code can still send most of its tokens to a few experts while the batch average stays flat, and at inference the traffic can drift toward one domain (the report's "domain-shift-induced load imbalance"). So V3 adds a sequence-wise loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{Bal}} = \alpha \sum_i f_i P_i\) with \(\alpha = 10^{-4}\), "just to avoid extreme imbalance within any single sequence" [§2.1.2, §4.2]. Batch-wise scope is deliberate: it lets experts specialize by domain (expert-load plots on the Pile), and a batch-wise auxiliary loss matched the bias method's validation loss (2.253 versus 2.253 at 1B, 2.080 versus 2.080 at 3B, against 2.258 and 2.085 for sequence-wise) [§4.5.3, Fig. 9]. The report names the two costs of batch-wise balance: imbalance inside small batches, absorbed by large micro-batches from large-scale expert and data parallelism, and domain shift at inference, absorbed by redundant experts (below).

Multi-token prediction: extra signal in training, a free draft head at inference

Standard training gives position \(i\) one target, \(x_{i+1}\). V3 adds a second: a small module predicts \(x_{i+2}\), trained jointly and weighted by \(\lambda\) [DeepSeek-V3, §2.2]. The report's two motivations are hedged: the objective "densifies the training signals and may improve data efficiency", and it "may enable the model to pre-plan its representations". Neither is shown causally. Equations and the comparison with Gloeckle et al.'s parallel heads are on page 11; what matters end to end:

  • Wiring (Figure 1). Module \(k\) takes the previous depth's hidden state \(h^{k-1}_i\) and the embedding of token \(t_{i+k}\), RMS-normalizes each, concatenates them (\(2d = 14{,}336\)), projects back to \(d\) with \(M_k \in \R^{d \times 2d}\), and runs one transformer block. Its output goes through the shared output head. Because module \(k\) sees the true next tokens, the causal chain is kept at every depth, unlike independent parallel heads.
  • Loss. \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}} = \frac{\lambda}{D}\sum_{k=1}^{D}\mathcal{L}^k_{\text{MTP}}\) with \(D = 1\); \(\lambda = 0.3\) for the first 10T tokens and \(0.1\) for the last 4.8T [§4.2], changing when the learning-rate cosine decay starts.
  • Cost. 11.5B unique parameters (1.7%), almost all in the block's 257 experts, and about 3.2 GFLOP per token forward against 74 for the main model, roughly 4% (page 11). The extra vocabulary head alone is \(2 \times 129{,}280 \times 7168 = 1.85\) GFLOP, 1.6 times an activated MoE layer (1.17 GFLOP).
  • Placement. DualPipe puts the shallowest layers (with the embedding) and the deepest (with the head) on one pipeline rank, so the shared tensors' "parameters and gradients" are physically shared with the MTP module [§3.2.3].

Ablation. Two MoE baselines differ only by a depth-1 module: 15.7B parameters on 1.33T tokens and 228.7B on 540B [Tab. 4]. Of 20 benchmark cells, 17 improve, one ties and two get worse. Gains concentrate on code and math (large model: HumanEval 44.5 to 53.7, GSM8K 72.3 to 74.0), Pile bits-per-byte barely moves (0.658 to 0.657), and large-model MMLU falls from 67.5 to 66.6. Single runs by the adopting lab at a tenth or less of the final token count: encouraging, not proven.

At inference: discard it, or use it as a draft

The module exists mainly to improve the main model, so it can be dropped, leaving inference cost identical to a model trained without it. Alternatively its prediction of \(x_{i+2}\) is a one-token draft for speculative decoding 推测解码 (page 17). The report measures a second-token acceptance rate of 85% to 90% across topics and a resulting 1.8× tokens per second [§5.4.3]. The two numbers are consistent, as a whiteboard check shows. With one draft accepted with probability \(p\), each step emits \(1 + p\) tokens: 1.85 at \(p = 0.85\), 1.90 at \(p = 0.90\). A measured 1.8× then implies a step with a draft costs \((1+p)/1.8 - 1 = 2.8\%\) to \(5.6\%\) more than a plain step, plausible when decode is memory-bound and the module is one block (my inference; the report does not state the overhead). This is the simple one-draft model; page 17 has the general formula and the extra expert reads an MoE incurs when verifying two tokens.

Infrastructure: 671B parameters on 2,048 H800s, without tensor parallelism

The cluster and the numbers that shape the design

V3 trains on 2,048 NVIDIA H800 GPUs in nodes of 8, joined by NVLink and NVSwitch inside a node and InfiniBand across nodes [DeepSeek-V3, §3.1]. Two figures drive most of the systems design: NVLink delivers 160 GB/s and IB 50 GB/s, a factor of 3.2, and each H800 has 132 streaming multiprocessors (SMs), of which the communication kernels take 20 [§3.2.2, §3.5.1]. The H800 is the export-market Hopper part: its tensor-core throughput matches the H100 SXM and its NVLink is capped at 400 GB/s against the H100's 900 GB/s, so the links, more than the tensor cores, set the pace. That is why FP8, node-limited routing and computation-communication overlap appear in the same report.

The parallel layout, and why there is no tensor parallelism

The layout is 16-way pipeline parallelism, 64-way expert parallelism 专家并行 spanning 8 nodes, and ZeRO-1 data parallelism [§3.2]. That is 128 GPUs per pipeline stage, with routed experts sharded 64 ways inside a stage (the leftover factor of 2 is our inference). Page 15 works the memory arithmetic: about 97% of parameters are routed experts, leaving about 1.7B parameters per GPU. The report's stated goal is to optimize memory "making it possible to train DeepSeek-V3 without using costly tensor parallelism" [§1]. Three reasons make tensor parallelism 张量并行 (page 15) a bad fit here (our reading; the report says only "costly"):

  • Its collectives sit on the critical path of every layer. Tensor-parallel all-reduces block, while MoE all-to-all and pipeline transfers can be overlapped with other chunks' compute (DualPipe, next).
  • The GEMMs are already small. An expert has width 2048, and splitting its matrices further would shrink per-GPU tiles and tensor-core efficiency.
  • The memory it would save is not needed. Expert parallelism shards 97% of the weights, ZeRO-1 shards optimizer states, recomputation trims activations, and BF16 Adam moments shrink the rest.

DualPipe: hiding the all-to-all inside the pipeline schedule

Cross-node expert parallelism makes the ratio of computation to communication "approximately 1:1", which the report calls inefficient [§3.2.1]. Three ideas combine to fix it.

  1. Split each chunk into components. A forward chunk is attention, all-to-all dispatch, MLP and all-to-all combine. As in Zero Bubble, the backward chunk splits attention and MLP each into backward-for-input (needed by the previous layer) and backward-for-weights (needed only by the optimizer), with pipeline transfers as a fifth component. The weight-gradient parts have no communication dependency, so they are ideal filler.
  2. Pair a forward chunk with a backward chunk so each all-to-all runs under compute from the other chunk (Figure 2a). The SM split between communication and computation is tuned by hand, and "both all-to-all and PP communication can be fully hidden".
  3. Feed micro-batches from both ends of the pipeline. Each device holds two stages, one per direction, so fill and drain overlap and the bubble roughly halves. This is why DualPipe needs two parameter copies.

Cross-node all-to-all kernels: 20 SMs for communication

The kernels are co-designed with the gating rule and the network. A token first travels over IB to the GPU with the same in-node index on each of its (at most 4) target nodes, and is then forwarded over NVLink to the GPUs hosting its experts without waiting behind later tokens. Combine reverses the path and accumulates on the way. The two stages overlap, and 20 of the 132 SMs saturate both IB and NVLink. The 20 SMs form 10 channels, and warp specialization gives dispatch three kinds of warps (IB send, IB-to-NVLink forward, NVLink receive) and combine three (NVLink send, NVLink-to-IB forward with accumulation, IB receive with accumulation), with counts adjusted dynamically; custom PTX and an auto-tuned chunk size limit L2 use and interference with compute SMs [§3.2.2]. The open-source implementation is DeepEP (FP8 dispatch, BF16 combine, per its README). The price is real: 20 of 132 SMs is 15.2% of the chip moving data, which the report says "will limit the computational throughput" (below).

Memory savings that cost almost nothing

Three techniques complete the no-TP story [§3.2.3]. Recompute 重计算 RMSNorm and the MLA up-projections in backward rather than store their outputs: the up-projected \(k^C\) and \(v^C\) alone are \(128 \times (128 + 128) = 32{,}768\) values per token per layer, 64 times the 512-value latent that regenerates them (page 07 prices the rebuild at 33.5M FLOPs per token per layer). Keep the parameter EMA in CPU memory, updated asynchronously, for an early read on quality after learning-rate decay at no GPU cost. Share the embedding and output head with the MTP module through pipeline placement. Low-precision storage is the next section.

FP8 training: the full recipe

Page 15 introduces FP8 formats and summarizes V3's scheme. This is the detailed version: interviewers follow up on which tensors are FP8, why the groups have those shapes, and what "promotion" means. The motivation is twofold. FP8 tensor cores have twice the peak of BF16 (about 1,979 versus 989 dense TFLOP/s on an H100, half of the sparse figures on NVIDIA's H100 page; the report says FP8 "theoretically doubles the computational speed"), and FP8 storage halves the bytes of cached activations and MoE dispatch. The obstacles are twofold too: FP8 has very little range and precision, and Hopper's FP8 tensor cores accumulate imprecisely.

What E4M3 is. One sign bit, 4 exponent bits (bias 7), 3 mantissa bits: largest finite value 448, smallest normal \(2^{-6} = 0.0156\), smallest subnormal \(2^{-9} = 0.00195\), no infinities, and 253 distinct finite values (127 non-negative including zero, counted in the drill). In a binade \([2^e, 2^{e+1})\) values are \(2^{e-3}\) apart, so relative rounding error is at most 6.25% (about 2.6% RMS on Gaussian data). The format spans \(\log_2(448/2^{-9}) = 17.8\) binades, about \(2.3 \times 10^5\) from smallest subnormal to maximum: generous, not unlimited.

Fine-grained quantization: one scale per small group

Standard FP8 training scales a whole tensor so its largest absolute value (amax) lands on 448. One outlier then sets the scale for everything: if amax is \(10^5\) times the typical magnitude, typical values land near \(0.005\) on the scaled axis, inside the subnormal region where spacing is fixed at \(0.00195\), and are quantized with tens of percent error or flushed to zero. V3 confines each outlier to a small group 细粒度量化 [§3.3.2]:

  • Activations: one scale per 1×128 tile, that is, per token per 128 channels.
  • Weights: one scale per 128×128 block, per 128 input channels and 128 output channels.
  • Rule: \(s = \mathrm{amax}_{\text{group}}/448\), computed online from the current values, and \(q = \mathrm{round}_{\text{E4M3}}(x/s)\). The report contrasts this with the "delayed quantization" of tensor-wise frameworks, which infers the scale from the amax history of earlier iterations.
From your jet tagger

Jet constituents have heavy-tailed inputs: transverse momentum spans orders of magnitude, so you log-transformed or standardized each feature before the network saw it. FP8 faces the same heavy tail inside the network, where you cannot preprocess. Per-group scaling is that normalization done on the fly, per token and per 128 channels, with the "standardization constant" recomputed from the current values at every step.

Scale overhead is small: an FP32 scale per 128 activations is \(32/128 = 0.25\) bits per element and \(32/16{,}384 = 0.002\) bits per weight, assuming FP32 scales (the report does not state the dtype; DeepGEMM takes FP32 scales on Hopper, [README]).

Why tiles for activations but blocks for weights? The report does not spell out the rationale, but Appendix B.2 gives the evidence. Quantizing all tensors of Dgrad (the activation gradients) in 128×128 blocks made a 16B MoE diverge within about 300B tokens, and the authors hypothesize that activation gradients "are highly imbalanced among tokens, resulting in token-correlated outliers" that a block spanning 128 tokens cannot isolate [App. B.2]. A 1×128 tile holds one token, so a loud token cannot damage its neighbors. Our reading of the other half: weights are static and their outliers are not token-correlated, so blocks amortize scale storage, and a block-quantized tensor transposes for the backward pass without re-quantizing (as the report notes).

Backward regrouping. Scales must run along each GEMM's contraction dimension. In Fprop that is the channel dimension \(K\), so activations are grouped 1×128. Wgrad contracts over tokens, so the same activations (and output gradients) must be grouped 128×1: the forward tiles are "read out, dequantized, transposed, re-quantized into 128x1 tiles, and stored in HBM" [§3.5.2], an extra memory pass the report lists as a hardware wish (below).

Accumulation precision 累加精度: why partial sums are promoted every 128 elements

The report found that "the accumulation precision of FP8 GEMM on NVIDIA H800 GPUs is limited to retaining around 14 bits". Per §3.5.2: after aligning 32 mantissa products by right-shifting to the maximum exponent, the tensor core keeps only the top 14 bits of each product and truncates the rest, and accumulation into registers is also 14-bit. Error grows with the inner dimension \(K\); for random matrices with \(K = 4096\) a preliminary test gave a maximum relative error of nearly 2% [§3.3.2]. The fix is promotion: the tensor cores run a short MMA chain, and at each interval of \(N_C\) elements the partial result is copied to FP32 registers on the CUDA cores and accumulated in full precision. The report picks \(N_C = 128\), "equivalent to 4 WGMMAs" (so 32 elements of \(K\) each), as the smallest interval that significantly improves precision without substantial overhead.

Promotion also makes per-group scaling along \(K\) feasible. A standard FP8 GEMM takes one scale per operand and cannot rescale each 128-wide slice of \(K\). But if each MMA chain covers exactly one scale group, the tensor core can work on raw codes and the scales are multiplied in during promotion: dequantization at almost no extra cost. Tile width, block width and promotion interval are all \(N_C = 128\) by design. One output element is

\[ y_{ij} \;=\; \sum_{g=0}^{K/128-1} s^{A}_{i,g}\; s^{W}_{g,\lfloor j/128\rfloor} \Bigl(\sum_{k=128g}^{128g+127} a^{q}_{ik}\, w^{q}_{kj}\Bigr) \]
Inner sum: tensor core on E4M3 codes. Outer sum: CUDA-core FP32 accumulation with both scales applied per chunk

For \(K = 7168\) that is 56 promotions per output tile, and 16 for an expert's down-projection (\(K = 2048\)). The cost is partial results moving between tensor cores and CUDA cores, which lowers one warpgroup's MMA issue rate. The report notes that H800 typically keeps two WGMMAs in flight, so while one warpgroup promotes the other multiplies.

E4M3 on every tensor

Earlier FP8 recipes use E4M3 forward and E5M2 for gradients, since gradients need range [Micikevicius et al. 2022]. V3 uses E4M3 everywhere for its extra mantissa bit ("mantissa over exponents"). The report's explanation is that per-group scaling effectively shares exponent bits among a group's elements [§3.3.2]: the range E5M2's extra exponent bit would give across a tensor is supplied by the group scales, so the element format spends its bits on precision. The same logic says why the recipe fails when groups get large (the block-wise Dgrad divergence).

What was validated, and what was not reported

Validated. On roughly 16B parameters trained on 1.33T tokens and roughly 230B on about 0.9T (the main text says "approximately 1 trillion tokens"), FP8 loss stays within 0.25% relative error of BF16 throughout [§3.3, App. B.1, Fig. 10]. Not reported: measured FP8-versus-BF16 throughput, MFU, and ablations of promotion interval or group size. Independent evidence fills part of the gap:

  • Ling 2.0 used the same layout (activations and gradients in [1,128] groups, weights in [128,128] blocks, E4M3, FP32 scales) at 1T parameters: loss within 0.25% of BF16 (averaging about 0.1%) over 900B tokens, and about +15% MFU over BF16. It also needed QKNorm, because outliers in the QKV projection grew with depth and amplified quantization error [Ling 2.0, §5.1].
  • Kimi K2 uses only a storage variant: FP8-E4M3 in 1×128 tiles with FP32 scales for cached MoE and SwiGLU inputs, with no FP8 computation because of "potential risks of performance degradation" seen in preliminary study [Kimi K2, §2.4.3].
  • Meta states Llama 4 Behemoth was pre-trained in FP8 on 32K GPUs at 390 TFLOPs per GPU, with no recipe [Llama 4 blog]. DeepGEMM reports up to 1550 TFLOPS on an H800 [README, 2026], about 78% of the 1,979 dense FP8 peak.

Read together: quality parity is well supported. The only independent end-to-end speed figure here (Ling 2.0's +15% MFU over BF16) is far below 2×, unsurprising when attention, the output head, normalization and communication stay outside FP8 and quantization and promotion add memory traffic and CUDA-core work.

Fine-grained FP8 training
Problem
Per-tensor FP8 scaling is defeated by outliers, and Hopper's FP8 tensor cores accumulate in only about 14 bits, so errors grow with the inner dimension.
Idea
Quantize activations in 1×128 tiles and weights in 128×128 blocks with online amax scales, use E4M3 for every tensor, promote partial sums to FP32 on CUDA cores every 128 elements of \(K\), and keep sensitive operators, master weights and optimizer math in BF16 or FP32.
Why it works
An outlier damages only its own group, the group scale plays the role of a shared exponent, and the promotion step is where the scales can be applied cheaply.
Evidence
Relative loss error under 0.25% versus BF16 at two scales (about 16B and 230B parameters), then a 14.8T-token run [DeepSeek-V3, §3.3, App. B]; Ling 2.0 reproduces it at 1T scale.
Cost
Custom kernels (DeepGEMM), scale tensors, an extra dequantize-transpose-requantize pass in backward, CUDA-core promotion work, no measured end-to-end speedup in the report, and dependence on Hopper's accumulator behavior.
Used by
DeepSeek-V3 (V4's stack keeps the FP8 layers, and NVIDIA's NVFP4 study uses the same scaling for its FP8 baseline, per page 15); Ling 2.0.

What transfers is the mechanism: error grows with the length of the narrow chain, and cutting it to 128 terms buys nearly an order of magnitude at every width.

3. The end-to-end GEMM. A \(64 \times 1024\) activation matrix in 1×128 tiles times a \(1024 \times 256\) weight matrix in 128×128 blocks, through the promoted GEMM, has 3.7% relative error against FP32: the two operands' 2.6% noise floors in quadrature (\(\sqrt{2} \times 2.6\% \approx 3.7\%\)). With this recipe, accumulation is no longer the dominant error.

import torch

E4M3_MAX = 448.0

def round_to_e4m3(x):
    ax = x.abs().clamp(max=E4M3_MAX)
    e = torch.floor(torch.log2(ax)).clamp(min=-6)           # binade; the clamp gives subnormal spacing 2**-9
    step = torch.exp2(e - 3)                                # 3 mantissa bits: 8 steps per binade
    return torch.sign(x) * torch.round(ax / step) * step    # ties to even, saturating at 448

def quantize_tiles(x, tile=(1, 128)):                       # tile (1,128) for activations, (128,128) for weights
    R, C = x.shape; gr, gc = tile
    amax = x.abs().reshape(R // gr, gr, C // gc, gc).amax(dim=(1, 3))
    s = amax / E4M3_MAX                                     # one scale per group (assumes amax > 0)
    return round_to_e4m3(x / s.repeat_interleave(gr, 0).repeat_interleave(gc, 1)), s

def fp8_matmul(a_q, a_s, w_q, w_s, chunk=128):              # a_q (M,K), a_s (M,K/128), w_q (K,N), w_s (K/128,N/128)
    out = torch.zeros(a_q.shape[0], w_q.shape[1])           # the FP32 registers on the CUDA cores
    for g in range(a_q.shape[1] // chunk):
        k = slice(g * chunk, (g + 1) * chunk)
        partial = a_q[:, k] @ w_q[k]                        # "tensor core": raw E4M3 codes, no scales
        out += partial * a_s[:, g:g + 1] * w_s[g].repeat_interleave(128)   # promote: rescale, add in FP32
    return out

Pretraining: data, tokenizer, hyperparameters, stability

Data and tokenizer

The corpus is 14.8T tokens (counted in V3's own tokenizer). Compared with V2 the report raises the share of mathematics and programming samples, extends multilingual coverage beyond English and Chinese, and refines the pipeline "to minimize redundancy while maintaining corpus diversity" (page 12). Documents are packed into 4K sequences "for data integrity", with no cross-sample attention masking during training, the opposite choice from Llama 3 (page 10) [DeepSeek-V3, §4.1]. Fill-in-the-middle is applied at a rate of 0.1 in the prefix-suffix-middle layout, <|fim_begin|> f_pre <|fim_hole|> f_suf <|fim_end|> f_middle <|eos_token|>, at the document level during pre-packing. The justification is inherited from DeepSeekCoder-V2: FIM did not harm next-token prediction while teaching the model to fill in from context.

The tokenizer is byte-level BPE with a "128K" vocabulary (129,280 entries in the config; page 02), with the pretokenizer and tokenizer training data modified for multilingual compression. One change has a side effect worth knowing: the new pretokenizer produces tokens that combine punctuation and line breaks, which compresses well but creates token-boundary bias when a multi-line prompt ends without a final line break (few-shot prompts are the typical case), because the merged token that normally covers the final punctuation and line break cannot form when the prompt stops before the line break (our gloss on the bias the report cites). V3's fix is to randomly split a proportion of such combined tokens during training, exposing the model to both forms.

The constants are AdamW with \(\beta_1 = 0.9\), \(\beta_2 = 0.95\), weight decay 0.1, gradient-norm clipping 1.0, sequence length 4K, initialization standard deviation 0.006, node limit \(M = 4\), and sequence-wise balance weight \(\alpha = 10^{-4}\) [§4.2]. Stability, as reported

The report claims twice that "we did not experience any irrecoverable loss spikes or perform any rollbacks" during the entire run [abstract, §1]. That sentence is careful: it does not say the loss curve was free of spikes, only that none required a restart, and it attributes the stability to nothing in particular. Our reading of the plausible contributors, none of them ablated: a moderate peak learning rate (\(2.2\times10^{-4}\)) with \(\beta_2 = 0.95\) and clipping at 1.0 (page 14), a batch that starts small (3,072 sequences) and ramps to 15,360, RMSNorm on the MLA latents and scaling at width bottlenecks, no balance-loss gradient in the router (page 08), and the FP8 safeguards of high-precision sensitive operators. Compare how other reports describe the same problem: Kimi K2 credits MuonClip's QK-clip, and DeepSeek-V4 added anticipatory routing against outlier-driven spikes (page 11, page 08). Treat V3's clean run as an outcome worth explaining, not a recipe that has been shown to transfer.

Long-context extension

After the 14.8T tokens at 4K, V3 applies YaRN and trains two phases of 1,000 steps each, 4K to 32K and then 32K to 128K. The YaRN settings are identical in both phases (scale \(s = 40\), \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 32\), attention factor \(0.1\ln s + 1\)), the learning rate is \(7.3\times10^{-6}\), the final pretraining rate, and batch sizes are 1,920 sequences of 32K then 480 of 128K [§4.3]. YaRN is "applied exclusively to the decoupled shared key" \(k^R\), because that is the only place position lives (page 07; page 10 gives the details, including that the code rotates \(q^R\) with the same frequencies).

Both phases process \(1{,}920 \times 32{,}768 = 480 \times 131{,}072 = 62.9\)M tokens per step, the same token batch as the end of pretraining, so the extension is about \(2000 \times 62.9\)M = 126B tokens, 0.85% of pretraining, yet it costs 119K GPU-hours, 4.3% of the total: \(119{,}000 / 126 \approx 945\) GPU-hours per billion tokens against 180 in pretraining, a 5.3× premium per token (my arithmetic; attention cost per token grows with sequence length). That is one reason to extend context late and briefly. The report's evidence is the needle-in-a-haystack grid to 128K after SFT (its Figure 8) and LongBench v2 accuracy of 48.7 against GPT-4o's 48.1 and Claude-3.5-Sonnet's 41.0 (its Table 6).

Post-training: SFT, distilled reasoning data, and GRPO

Post-training costs 5K GPU-hours, 0.18% of the total, and is where V3 imports the reasoning behavior of a separate model by knowledge distillation 知识蒸馏 [DeepSeek-V3, §5]. The pipeline, step by step:

  1. Expert models. The reasoning data come from an internal DeepSeek-R1 model, whose output is accurate but suffers from "overthinking, poor formatting, and excessive length" (page 28 covers R1 itself). So the team first builds, for each domain (code, mathematics, general reasoning), an expert model with a combined SFT and RL pipeline, and that expert becomes the data generator for the final model. The goal is a balance between R1's accuracy and the concision of regularly formatted answers.
  2. Two sample types per problem. One pairs the problem with its original response, the other adds a carefully designed system prompt that induces reflection and verification and pairs it with the R1 response. During RL the expert samples at high temperature and, without any system prompt, learns to mix both patterns; "after hundreds of RL steps" it has absorbed R1's style.
  3. Rejection sampling 拒绝采样 with the finished experts as generators curates the reasoning SFT data, keeping R1's strengths and concise answers (page 19). Non-reasoning data (creative writing, role-play, simple question answering) comes from DeepSeek-V2.5 with human verification. The SFT set is 1.5M instances.
  4. SFT. Two epochs, cosine learning rate from \(5\times10^{-6}\) to \(1\times10^{-6}\). Samples are packed but masked so they are mutually invisible, unlike pretraining, where packed documents can attend to each other.
  5. Reinforcement learning. Rule-based rewards where an answer can be checked (a final answer in a box for math, compiler and test cases for LeetCode problems), which the report says are "resistant to manipulation or exploitation". Otherwise a model-based reward model trained from V3 SFT checkpoints, whose preference data include the chain of thought leading to the reward, to reduce reward hacking (page 21). The optimizer is GRPO (page 24; introduced in DeepSeekMath, page 26), with no critic and a group baseline \(A_i = (r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r))/\mathrm{std}(r)\) over \(G\) samples per prompt, a clipped policy ratio and a KL penalty to a reference model, on prompts from coding, math, writing, role-play and question answering.
  6. Self-rewarding for open-ended prompts. Where no verifier exists, V3 follows the constitutional-AI approach, using the voting results of V3 itself as feedback. As a judge V3 scores 87.0 on RewardBench (Claude-3.5-Sonnet-1022: 88.7, GPT-4o-0806: 86.7), and 89.6 with majority voting over 6 samples [§5.3.4, §5.4.2, Tab. 8].

Pitfall: two ways to misuse the number

It is a fair marginal compute price of the final run at market rental rates, and it is checkable (2.788M GPU-hours, \(3.29\times10^{24}\) FLOPs, about 35% BF16 MFU) and comparable across labs that use the same convention. It is not the cost of building a frontier model, and it is not reproducible by someone who rents the same GPUs without the systems stack (FP8 kernels, DualPipe, DeepEP, MLA kernels): 35% MFU on H800s is the output of engineering that sits in the excluded research and development. A good interview answer states both halves.

The hardware wish list (§3.5)

The report ends its systems section with concrete asks to chip vendors, each traceable to a cost paid above [§3.5]:

  • Offload communication from the SMs. The 20 SMs forward data between IB and NVLink, move data between RDMA buffers and input and output buffers, run the combine reductions and manage memory layout. The ask is a co-processor (like NVIDIA SHARP) and one interface unifying the IB and NVLink networks with simple read, write, multicast and reduce primitives.
  • Higher accumulation precision in FP8 tensor cores, replacing the 14-bit truncating adder that forced promotion to CUDA cores.
  • Native tile- and block-wise scaling: let tensor cores receive scale factors and finish accumulation and dequantization internally, avoiding the frequent tensor-core-to-CUDA-core copies.
  • Online quantization support: today 128 BF16 values are read from HBM, quantized, written back as FP8 and read again for the MMA. The ask is a cast fused with TMA access (or near-memory), cutting off-chip traffic by roughly 50%.
  • Transposed GEMM reads from shared memory, to remove the dequantize-transpose-requantize pass of the backward step.

The report remarks that its tile scaling is "highly consistent with the idea of microscaling formats" and that Blackwell's tensor cores announced support for finer-grained microscaling (page 15): hardware moving toward what V3 did in software.

  • Architecture as a template. Common misconceptions

  • "V3 cost $5.6M to build." $5.576M is the rental value of the GPU time of the final run (pretraining, context extension, post-training) at an assumed $2 per H800-hour. The report itself excludes prior research and ablations. Data, staff, the R1-series models that generated post-training data, and cluster ownership are also outside it.
  • "FP8 training means every tensor is FP8." Only the inputs of the Linear-layer GEMMs (plus some stored activations and the MoE dispatch) are FP8. Embedding, output head, gating, normalization and the attention core stay BF16 or FP32, master weights and weight gradients are FP32, and Adam moments are BF16.
  • "Fine-grained scaling and FP32 promotion are the same trick." They fix different problems. Group scales handle outliers and range, and promotion handles the tensor core's roughly 14-bit accumulator. The tile width, block width and promotion interval coincide (\(N_C = 128\)) so that the scales can be applied at the promotion step, but neither mechanism makes the other unnecessary.
  • "Auxiliary-loss-free means no balancing loss at all." V3 still has a sequence-wise loss with \(\alpha = 10^{-4}\). The bias controller balances a batch, and the small loss stops single sequences from collapsing onto a few experts.
  • "MTP is how V3 generates several tokens at once." The module is a training objective first. At inference it is discarded or used as a one-token draft for speculative decoding, where it predicts one extra token, verified by the main model, giving 1.8× TPS at 85% to 90% acceptance.
  • "DualPipe wins by pipelining better." With no communication to hide, DualPipe's bubble is barely below ZB1P's (14 versus 15 at 16 stages in our illustration); splitting weight gradients from input gradients does most of that work. Its distinct win is overlapping the all-to-all, and it costs two parameter copies per device.
  • "No tensor parallelism means the model is not sharded." V3 shards with 16 pipeline stages, 64-way expert parallelism and ZeRO-1. It avoids only the per-layer tensor-parallel collectives.
  • "Top-8 routing sends a token to 8 nodes." Routing is node-limited: at most 4 nodes per token, 32 experts per node, and NVLink forwarding makes extra experts on a reached node nearly free.

Interview questions

Warm-up Walk me through DeepSeek-V3's architecture and the reason for each component.

61 pre-norm transformer layers, width 7168, 671B parameters of which 37B are activated per token, trained on 14.8T tokens. Attention is MLA (128 heads, KV latent 512, decoupled RoPE key 64): it caches 576 values per token per layer, 70,272 bytes per token, because serving is bounded by KV bytes. Feed-forward is DeepSeekMoE in 58 of the layers (the first 3 are dense): 1 shared and 256 routed experts of width 2048 with 8 routed active, so each token pays for 9 small experts while the model stores 257. Affinities are sigmoids normalized over the selected experts. Routing is node-limited to 4 nodes because IB is 3.2× slower than NVLink. Balancing is auxiliary-loss-free (a per-expert bias steering top-k), plus a tiny sequence-wise loss, and no tokens are dropped, so balance costs no gradient interference. MTP (depth 1) adds training signal and a draft head. The systems side is part of the design: FP8 GEMMs with fine-grained scales for throughput and memory, DualPipe and custom all-to-all kernels to hide communication, and PP16 × EP64 with ZeRO-1 and no tensor parallelism. The through-line is compute, communication and memory levers under one co-design.

Warm-up Calculate the activated parameters of V3 from its config, and the fraction of parameters that are routed experts.

One expert is a SwiGLU network with three \(7168 \times 2048\) matrices: 44,040,192 parameters. Routed experts: \(58 \times 256 \times 44.04\text{M} = 653.9\)B, shared: \(58 \times 44.04\text{M} = 2.55\)B, so experts hold 656.5B of 671.0B, 97.8%. Active per token: 8 routed plus 1 shared = 9 experts per MoE layer, \(58 \times 9 \times 44.04\text{M} = 22.99\)B. Always-active parts: MLA \(61 \times 187.1\text{M} = 11.41\)B, three dense FFNs \(3 \times 3 \times 7168 \times 18{,}432 = 1.19\)B, routers \(58 \times 7168 \times 256 = 0.11\)B, and the two vocabulary matrices \(2 \times 129{,}280 \times 7168 = 1.85\)B. Total 37.55B with both matrices; 36.6B counting only the output head, 35.7B with neither. The reported "37B" sits inside that range, which is why the embedding convention matters at the 5% level. Only \(9/257 = 3.5\%\) of expert parameters are used per token, which is why compute follows the activated count while memory follows the total.

Warm-up What is MTP in V3, and how is it used at inference?

MTP adds a second training target: a module predicts the token after the next one. Module \(k\) RMS-normalizes the previous depth's hidden state and the embedding of the next token, concatenates them (\(2d\)), projects back to \(d\), runs one transformer block, and uses the main model's shared output head. The loss is weighted by \(\lambda = 0.3\) for the first 10T tokens and 0.1 for the last 4.8T. It keeps the full causal chain, unlike independent parallel heads. Ablation: 17 of 20 benchmark cells improve, mostly code and math, at 15.7B and 228.7B scale.

At inference there are two options. Drop it: the model is then exactly as expensive as one trained without MTP. Or use it as a one-token drafter for speculative decoding: with the reported second-token acceptance of 85% to 90%, each step emits \(1+p\) = 1.85 to 1.90 tokens on average, and the reported 1.8× TPS implies a per-step overhead of only about 3% to 6%.

Warm-up Which operations run in FP8 in V3, and which do not?

All three GEMMs of every Linear operator (Fprop, Dgrad, Wgrad) take FP8 (E4M3) inputs and output BF16 or FP32. Kept in BF16 or FP32: the embedding, the output head, MoE gating, normalization and the attention operators. Master weights and weight gradients are FP32 and the Adam moments are BF16. Cached activations are stored in FP8 (the input to the Linear after attention uses a custom E5M6 format, and SwiGLU inputs are cached in FP8 with the output recomputed). MoE dispatch is FP8 and combine is BF16. The rule of thumb: FP8 wherever the tensor feeds a big matmul, high precision wherever the operation is cheap or numerically sensitive.

Core How does auxiliary-loss-free balancing work, and why add a sequence-wise loss anyway?

Each routed expert has a bias \(b_i\). Top-\(k\) selection uses \(s_{i,t} + b_i\), but the gate that multiplies the expert output is still the unbiased sigmoid score, normalized over the selected experts. After each step, measured over the whole batch, an overloaded expert's bias falls by \(\gamma\) and an underloaded expert's rises by \(\gamma\) (\(\gamma = 0.001\) for the first 14.3T tokens, then 0). No balance term enters the loss, so no interference gradient reaches the router or backbone, unlike an auxiliary loss where the coefficient trades balance against quality.

The controller only sees batch-level load. A single sequence, say all code, can still pile onto a few experts while the batch average is flat, and serving batches are less diverse than training batches. So V3 adds a sequence-wise loss with \(\alpha = 10^{-4}\), "just to avoid extreme imbalance within any single sequence". Batch-wise scope is intended: it allows domain specialization (a batch-wise auxiliary loss matched the bias method's validation loss, 2.253 versus 2.253 at 1B, against 2.258 for sequence-wise). The costs the report names are imbalance in small batches, handled by big micro-batches, and inference domain shift, handled by redundant experts. No tokens are dropped.

Core How did DeepSeek make FP8 training work? Why fine-grained scaling?

Three ingredients. (1) Fine-grained scales. Per-tensor scaling maps the tensor's amax to 448, so one outlier pushes everything else toward E4M3's subnormal range and flushes small values to zero. V3 uses one scale per 1×128 activation tile (per token per 128 channels) and per 128×128 weight block, computed online as amax/448. An outlier now damages only its group. (2) FP32 promotion. The Hopper FP8 tensor core accumulates in about 14 bits, giving nearly 2% maximum relative error at \(K = 4096\), so every \(N_C = 128\) elements of \(K\) the partial sum is copied to FP32 registers on CUDA cores, where the scales are also applied. (3) Mixed precision. E4M3 everywhere (the group scales act as shared exponents), high precision for embedding, head, gating, norms and attention, FP32 masters, BF16 moments.

Evidence: relative loss error under 0.25% versus BF16 at two scales (about 16B and 230B parameters), then a 14.8T-token run. Not reported: a measured speedup or MFU. Ling 2.0 independently reproduced the recipe at 1T parameters (within 0.25% loss, +15% MFU over BF16), but needed QKNorm to tame outliers.

Core Why promote partial sums to FP32 every 128 elements? What breaks without it?

Without promotion the whole \(K\)-long chain accumulates in the tensor core's narrow (about 14-bit) adder, which aligns products to the largest exponent and truncates low bits, so once the running sum is large, later small products vanish, and error grows with \(K\). Promotion cuts the narrow chain to 128 terms and sums the chunks in FP32. In a toy model (round the running sum to \(b\) bits after each add; \(K = 4096\)), promotion every 128 cut the relative error 5.7× to 9.1× for \(b\) from 10 to 16. It also enables the scaling scheme: a standard FP8 GEMM has one scale per operand, so it cannot apply a different scale to each 128-wide slice of \(K\). Because each MMA chain covers exactly one scale group, the tensor core multiplies raw codes and the CUDA cores multiply the two group scales into each partial sum: \(y_{ij} = \sum_g s^A_{i,g} s^W_{g,\lfloor j/128\rfloor}\,(\text{partial}_g)\). The price is data movement between tensor cores and CUDA cores and a lower MMA issue rate for one warpgroup, mostly hidden because two warpgroups alternate. The report asks hardware vendors to fix it natively.

Core Why no tensor parallelism? What problem does DualPipe solve?

No TP. The report says tensor parallelism is "costly", and V3's layout makes it unnecessary and unattractive (our reading): about 97% of parameters are routed experts, sharded 64 ways by expert parallelism across 16 pipeline stages, leaving roughly 1.7B parameters per GPU (page 15's arithmetic); ZeRO-1 shards optimizer state; recomputation of RMSNorm and MLA up-projections, FP8 activations and BF16 moments cut activation and state memory. Tensor-parallel all-reduces would sit on every layer's critical path on NVLink that the expert all-to-all already needs, and would shrink already small expert GEMMs.

DualPipe. Cross-node expert parallelism makes communication about as long as computation. DualPipe splits chunks into attention, dispatch, MLP and combine (backward split into input and weight gradients), overlaps a forward chunk with a backward chunk so each all-to-all hides behind the other's compute, and feeds micro-batches from both pipeline ends. Bubble \((PP/2 - 1)(F\&B + B - 3W)\) versus \((PP-1)(F+B)\) for 1F1B, at the price of 2× parameter copies and \(PP+1\) activation slots.

Core Is "$5.6M to train V3" a fair statement? What does it include and exclude?

It is a correct statement about one thing: 2.788M H800 GPU-hours (2.664M pretraining, 119K context extension, 5K post-training) times an assumed $2 per hour gives $5.576M, and the numbers reconcile (180K GPU-hours per trillion tokens times 14.8T is 2.664M). It excludes, as the report says, prior research and ablation experiments. Also outside it: the R1-series model and expert models that produced post-training data, data work, staff, evaluation, failed or restarted runs, and owning the cluster instead of renting (one outside estimate puts server capex near $1.6B, unconfirmed). The report's own tables imply at least about 19% of the main run's FLOPs in ablations.

Fair use: a marginal compute price for the final run at market rates, comparable to other labs' figures computed the same way (Llama 3 405B would be about $61.7M at $2 per GPU-hour). Unfair use: the cost of building a frontier model, or a claim that anyone renting H800s could reproduce it for that price, since reaching about 35% MFU took the excluded systems engineering.

Core Back out V3's MFU from the report. State your assumptions.

Throughput: 180K GPU-hours per trillion tokens is \(10^{12}/(180{,}000 \times 3600) = 1{,}543\) tokens per second per GPU. FLOPs per token with \(N = 37\)B activated: \(6N = 222\) GFLOP, so \(222 \times 1543 = 342.6\) TFLOP/s per GPU. Against the H100/H800 dense BF16 peak of 989.4 that is 34.6% (equivalently \(6ND = 3.29\times10^{24}\) over \(2.664\text{M} \times 3600 \times 989.4\text{T}\)). Adding causal attention at 4K (about 30.7 GFLOP per token for forward plus backward over 61 layers) and the MTP module (about 9.7 GFLOP) gives about 262 GFLOP per token, 405 TFLOP/s, 40.9%. Against the FP8 peak (1,979) it is 17% to 20%; against a blended peak (about 84% of FLOPs in FP8 GEMMs) about 24%.

Assumptions to state: the peak (H800 assumed equal to H100 SXM), which parameters count (37B versus 37.55B), whether the 180K figure includes restarts, MFU counts model FLOPs not recomputation, and the attention convention. Sanity check: Llama 3 405B implies 34.5% by the same method from its 30.84M GPU-hours, so V3's hardware efficiency is comparable to a good dense run, and its cost advantage is the 11× lower FLOPs.

Core Why tiles (1×128) for activations but blocks (128×128) for weights, and why E4M3 for every tensor?

The report's evidence is Appendix B.2: quantizing the activation gradients of Dgrad in 128×128 blocks made a 16B MoE diverge within about 300B tokens, and the authors attribute it to token-correlated outliers, since activation gradients are highly imbalanced across tokens. A 1×128 tile contains one token, so a loud token cannot degrade its neighbors. Weights are static, and (our reading) their outliers are not token-correlated, so blocks amortize scale storage and can be transposed for the backward pass without re-quantization. Activations do need regrouping to 128×1 in the backward pass because Wgrad contracts over tokens, which costs an extra dequantize-transpose-requantize pass.

E4M3 everywhere, instead of E5M2 for gradients: per-group scaling makes each group's scale act as a shared exponent, so the element format does not need the extra exponent bit for range and can spend it on a third mantissa bit. That precision is lost again if groups get large, which is the block-wise divergence.

Deep-dive If you had to cut V3's training cost by another 2×, what would you try?

Start from the identity cost = \(6 N_{\text{act}} D / (\text{MFU} \times \text{peak})\) times price per GPU-hour, and where the money is: 95.6% is pretraining. Levers, with expected gain and risk:

  • Utilization (about 1.3× to 1.5×). V3 sits at roughly 35% to 41% of BF16 peak and about 17% to 24% of FP8 peak. Levers: get the 20 communication SMs (15% of the chip) back through hardware offload or fused kernels, native group-scaled MMA to remove promotion overhead, better overlap. Risk: engineering effort with diminishing returns, and independent teams saw limited gains from overlap.
  • Sparser MoE (about 1.15×). Kimi K2's sparsity scaling law says sparsity 48 needs 1.15× fewer FLOPs than 32 for the same validation loss (V3 is 256/8 = 32). Risk: more communication and serving complexity.
  • Token or optimizer efficiency (uncertain, up to 2× if it holds). Muon (Moonlight: about 52% of AdamW's FLOPs at their scale; but Wen et al. found only 1.1× at 1.2B under careful tuning), better or synthetic data, more MTP depth. Risk: gains often shrink at scale, so validate on a scaling-law ladder first (page 13).
  • Lower precision (small today). FP4 experts need hardware support and quantization-aware training to pay off; V4's FP4 gain is currently memory, not FLOP/s (page 15).
  • Long-context stage (about 4%). It costs 5.3× more per token; pretrain with long sequences natively only if the recipe saves elsewhere.

Multiplying sparsity, utilization and a partial optimizer gain gets close to 2× but none is guaranteed. I would first spend on small-scale proxies (as V3 did with 16B and 230B ablations), reserving the large run for the combination. Price per GPU-hour is a non-algorithmic lever.

Deep-dive Compute the pipeline bubble at 16 stages for 1F1B, ZB1P and DualPipe with F = 2, B = 4, W = 1 and F&B = 3. When does DualPipe stop paying off?

Using V3's Table 2 with \(PP = 16\): 1F1B \((16-1)(2+4) = 90\); ZB1P \((16-1)(2+4-2) = 60\); DualPipe \((8-1)(3+4-3) = 28\), in units where a forward chunk is 2. These times model an all-to-all as long as the compute it serves, exposed in serial schedules (\(F\), \(B\)) but hidden in the overlapped pair (\(F\&B = 3 < F + B = 6\)). If there is no communication to hide (\(F = 1, B = 2, W = 1, F\&B = 3\)), the numbers are 45, 15 and 14: DualPipe barely improves on ZB1P.

It stops paying off when (a) the expert-parallel group is small or the compute-to-communication ratio is far from 1:1 (nothing to overlap; Ling 2.0 saw limited end-to-end gains), (b) memory is tight, because it holds two parameter copies (Kimi K2 rejected it above 1T parameters), (c) routing imbalance makes the slowest rank dominate, or (d) stages or micro-batches cannot be divisible by 2. Also note the report has no ablation, so the size of the real-world win is asserted through the analytical table.

Deep-dive Design question: you port V3's FP8 recipe to hardware with native microscaling (MXFP8). What becomes unnecessary, what carries over, and what would you re-validate?

The report's own wish list (§3.5.2) asks for tensor cores that take scale factors and finish accumulation and dequantization internally, and for higher accumulation precision. MXFP8 hardware puts a shared power-of-two (E8M0) scale on each block of 32 elements (page 15). If the hardware also accumulates at adequate precision, the CUDA-core promotion loop, and its data movement, can go away. That last condition must be measured with a \(K = 4096\)-style test, not assumed.

What changes: groups shrink from 128 to 32, which isolates outliers better, but power-of-two scales cannot map a group's amax exactly to 448, so the group's largest value lands between 224 and 448 and up to one binade of range is wasted (our reasoning). What carries over: E4M3 on every tensor, online scaling, keeping embedding, head, gating, norms and attention core in high precision, FP32 masters, and the backward-pass regrouping logic (Wgrad still contracts over tokens). What I would re-validate before committing: a BF16-versus-FP8 loss comparison at 16B and a larger scale (the report's Appendix B.1 protocol, target under 0.25% relative loss error), Dgrad's sensitivity to token-correlated outliers (Appendix B.2), and actual speedup, because the report never measured one.

Deep-dive Read the V3 report critically: which claims are evidence and which are assertions?

Well supported: the cost arithmetic (it reconciles), the FP8 loss parity at two scales (Fig. 10, under 0.25%), the config and parameter counts (checkable against the released config), and reported benchmark results (the authors' own runs, capped at 8K output tokens). Supported by single ablations from the adopting lab, at a small fraction of the final token count: MTP (17 of 20 cells improve, but MMLU falls 0.9 on the large model and gains concentrate on code and math), aux-loss-free balancing (consistent small gains, large-model MMLU down 1.1), and the batch-wise versus sequence-wise finding. Asserted without measurement: DualPipe's practical bubble and overlap (only an analytical table), FP8's speedup ("theoretically doubles"), any MFU, why the run was stable (no spikes needing rollback, no attribution), why the first 3 layers are dense, why sigmoid, and why the bias freezes for the last 500B tokens. Unknowable from the report: the full cost of research and ablations. A strong answer names what independent replication has added (Ling 2.0 reproduced FP8; Kimi K2 rejected DualPipe and FP8 compute) and proposes the missing experiment, for example an MFU and bubble measurement with DualPipe on and off.
