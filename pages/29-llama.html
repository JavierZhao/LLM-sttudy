Four generations in one figure

Llama's public history is short: four generations between February 2023 and April 2025, each attacking a different bottleneck. Llama 1 asked which model is cheapest to serve for a target quality and answered with a small model trained on far more tokens than compute-optimal. Llama 2 made that base usable as an assistant. Llama 3 scaled the same block to 15T tokens and a 405B flagship on 16K GPUs, and documented the engineering. Llama 4 changed the block: mixture of experts 混合专家, native multimodality, and interleaved RoPE and position-free layers for context length.

Two threads run through the page: serving cost (Llama 1 chose tokens over parameters, Llama 4 attacks FLOPs per token with sparsity) and risk (Llama 3 avoided MoE and RL to keep a 405B run stable; Llama 4 took both).

Llama 1 (February 2023): the overtraining bet and the standard block

The bet: pay for tokens now, save on inference forever

The Chinchilla scaling law 缩放定律 says to spend a training budget at roughly 20 tokens per parameter (page 13). Llama 1 argued that this answers the wrong question for a model served millions of times: the objective "disregards the inference budget", and "a smaller one trained longer will ultimately be cheaper at inference" [Touvron et al. 2023, §1]. Their evidence: "although Hoffmann et al. (2022) recommends training a 10B model on 200B tokens, we find that the performance of a 7B model continues to improve even after 1T tokens." So the 7B and 13B saw 1.0T tokens and the 33B and 65B saw 1.4T, with a 4M-token batch [Llama 1, Tab. 2]. Using exact parameter counts (the drill reproduces them):

The block: three borrowed ideas

The Llama block: pre-norm RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE
Problem
The original transformer normalizes after each sublayer, uses a ReLU FFN of width \(4d\) and adds absolute position embeddings once at the input. Deep post-norm stacks are hard to train, and absolute positions are tied to the training length.
Idea
Pre-normalization with RMSNorm 均方根归一化 [credited to GPT-3]; SwiGLU with hidden width \(\tfrac23\cdot 4d\) [PaLM]; rotary embeddings 旋转位置编码 at every layer instead of absolute positions [GPT-Neo] [Llama 1, §2.2].
Why it works
Pre-norm keeps an identity path through the residual stream, which makes depth trainable (page 04); RMSNorm is cheaper than LayerNorm. A gated FFN has three matrices, so width \(\tfrac83 d\) gives \(3 \cdot d \cdot \tfrac83 d = 8d^2\) parameters, the same as the \(2 \cdot d \cdot 4d\) it replaces. RoPE makes attention depend on relative offset at every layer (page 05).
Evidence
No ablation in the paper, only citations of each idea's source. The evidence is that the models are strong and most open dense models since use the same block.
Cost
Nothing measurable here. Full multi-head attention costs 512 KiB of KV cache per token in the 7B (next section).
Used by
Llama 1 to 4, and close variants in Qwen, Mistral, DeepSeek LLM and OLMo 2 (pages 04, 05).

Every linear layer is bias-free and the embedding and output head are untied (a bias-free, untied count reproduces each released checkpoint's parameter total to the digit). The tokenizer is a 32K SentencePiece BPE with byte fallback that splits numbers into digits [Llama 2, §2.2] (page 02). The data was public only: 67% CommonCrawl (CCNet), 15% C4, 4.5% each of GitHub, Wikipedia and books, about 1.4T tokens [Llama 1, Tab. 1] (page 12). The authors blame their MMLU gap to Chinchilla on having only 177 GB of books and papers against up to 2 TB, an early hint that data mattered more than architecture.

Worked numbers: the 65B run

The paper reports about 380 tokens per second per GPU on 2,048 A100-80GB GPUs, so 1.4T tokens take about 21 days [Llama 1, §2.4]. Check: \(380 \times 2048 = 778{,}240\) tokens/s, so \(1.4\times10^{12}/778{,}240\) s \(= 20.8\) days, and the carbon table's 1,022,362 GPU-hours \(/\,2048 = 499\) h \(= 20.8\) days as well [Llama 1, Tab. 15]. Llama 2 (July 2023): more tokens, longer context, GQA and the open RLHF recipe

The base model: almost nothing changed in the block

The paper adopts "most of the pretraining setting and model architecture from Llama 1"; the two architectural differences are doubled context (2,048 to 4,096) and GQA with 8 KV heads on the 34B and 70B only [Touvron et al. 2023b, §2.2, Tab. 1]. The optimizer is unchanged (AdamW, \(\beta = (0.9, 0.95)\), cosine to 10% of peak, 2,000 warmup steps, weight decay 0.1, clip 1.0, 4M-token batch; Llama 2 states \(\epsilon = 10^{-5}\)). The change is data: a new public mix with more cleaning and up-sampling of factual sources, 2.0T tokens at every size (40% more than Llama 1's 1.4T), and 3.3M A100-80GB GPU-hours in total, 1,720,320 of them for the 70B (Llama 1's 65B: 1,022,362). The 34B was trained but withheld: "we are delaying the release of the 34B model due to a lack of time to sufficiently red team."

The context change has its own ablation, two models on 150B tokens each. On SCROLLS, whose inputs average 3.5K tokens, the 2K model is near zero (NarrativeQA F1 0.21 against 17.26 for the 4K model) while short-context tasks are within noise (HellaSwag 75.1 against 74.8) [Llama 2, App. A.2.1, Tab. 16-17]. A model cannot use context it was not trained on, which is why the staged extension of page 10 exists.

GQA only where the cache hurts

Grouped-query attention 分组查询注意力 is there for "inference scalability for our larger models". The evidence is a 30B ablation on 150B tokens, where GQA "performs comparably to the MHA baseline on most evaluation tasks and is better than the MQA variant on average" (FFN widened by 1.3 for GQA and 1.33 for MQA to hold parameters equal), plus a serving argument: the largest models run on 8 A100s with tensor parallelism, an MQA head cannot be split across 8 GPUs, so it would be duplicated and the cache would be no smaller than GQA's [Llama 2, App. A.2.1, Tab. 18] (mechanism: page 07). The stakes per token, with 128-dim heads in bf16:

  • Llama 2 7B (MHA): \(32 \times 2 \times 32 \times 128 \times 2\text{ B} = 524{,}288\) B = 512 KiB, so 2 GiB per 4K-token sequence.
  • Llama 2 70B (GQA-8): \(80 \times 2 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 327{,}680\) B = 320 KiB, 1.25 GiB per sequence. As MHA (64 KV heads) it would be 2.5 MiB per token, 8 times more (10 GiB per sequence).

The paper does not say why the 7B and 13B kept MHA; the likely reason (our reading) is that their caches mattered less. Llama 3 adopted GQA-8 at every size, including the 8B (below).

Code Llama and the RoPE base

Code Llama (August 2023) continued Llama 2 on code, then ran a long-context stage on 16,384-token sequences in which "we increase the base period \(\theta\) from 10,000 to 1,000,000" (10,000 steps, stable to 100,000 tokens) [Rozière et al. 2023, §2.4]. In their ablation, fine-tuning without changing the base already failed to retrieve far keys at 8,000 tokens, and linear position interpolation failed to extrapolate (App. G.4). The lever needs no architecture change and reappears in Llama 3, which sets a base of 500,000 and cites Xiong et al. [Xiong et al. 2023] (page 05, page 10).

Llama 3 and 3.1 (2024): the same block, ten times the data, 16K GPUs

Llama 3 shipped in two steps: 8B and 70B with 8K context in April 2024, then 3.1 (8B, 70B, 405B, 128K context) in July with "The Llama 3 Herd of Models" [Llama Team 2024]. Its three "key levers" are data, scale, and managing complexity, and the third explains the design: a dense transformer "rather than a mixture-of-experts model to maximize training stability", and "a relatively simple post-training procedure" of SFT, rejection sampling and DPO "as opposed to more complex reinforcement learning algorithms that tend to be less stable and harder to scale" [Llama 3, §1]. Llama 3 is the "reduce the risk" generation.

Architecture: four small changes to Llama 2

The architecture "does not deviate significantly from Llama and Llama 2"; the gains "are primarily driven by improvements in data quality and diversity as well as by increased training scale" [Llama 3, §3.2]. The four changes:

  1. GQA with 8 KV heads at every size, "to improve inference speed and to reduce the size of key-value caches during decoding". For the 8B that is 128 KiB per token against 512 KiB for a Llama 2 7B-shaped MHA (page 07), and it makes context-parallel all-gathers of K and V cheap.
  2. A document mask stopping attention across documents in a packed sequence: limited impact in standard pre-training, but "important in continued pre-training on very long sequences".
  3. A 128K-token vocabulary (decision card below).
  4. RoPE base 500,000 (from 10,000) "to better support longer contexts"; Xiong et al. showed it effective to 32,768 (page 05, page 10).

The FFN width rule, and where the extra parameters went

Llama 1 used \(\tfrac83 d\); Llama 3's \(\dff/d\) is 3.5. The width comes from Meta's reference code, which the drill asks you to write (page 04 derives it):

def ffn_hidden_dim(d, multiple_of, ffn_dim_multiplier=None):   # llama/model.py, FeedForward
    h = int(2 * (4 * d) / 3)                       # 2/3 of 4d, truncated
    if ffn_dim_multiplier is not None:
        h = int(ffn_dim_multiplier * h)            # truncated again
    return multiple_of * ((h + multiple_of - 1) // multiple_of)   # round UP to a multiple

A 128K-token vocabulary
Problem
Llama 2's 32K vocabulary encodes English at 3.17 characters per token and other languages worse. Every token costs the same FLOPs and KV-cache bytes, so a poor tokenizer wastes compute and context.
Idea
100K tokens from the tiktoken BPE tokenizer plus 28K for non-English languages [Llama 3, §3.2].
Why it works
English compression rises from 3.17 to 3.94 characters per token, so the same text is \(3.17/3.94 = 80.5\%\) as many tokens: 24% more text per training FLOP and per context window. The 28K added tokens improved compression and downstream performance "with no impact on English tokenization".
Evidence
The compression numbers; no isolated quality ablation is reported.
Cost
Embedding and head are \(2 \times 128{,}256 \times 4096 = 1{,}050{,}673{,}152\) parameters in the 8B, 13.1% of the model (3.9% for Llama 2 7B); rare tokens see fewer updates; the logits tensor is 4 times wider.
Used by
Llama 3 onward; other families use even larger vocabularies for the same reason (page 02).

Llama 3 in practice: scaling laws, 16K GPUs, 128K context and post-training

Scaling laws, and small models trained far past optimal

The flagship's size came from a scaling-law fit (method and caveats: page 13): models of 40M to 16B parameters at budgets from \(6\times10^{18}\) to \(10^{22}\) FLOPs, a parabola per IsoFLOP curve, and a power law \(N^\star(C) = A\,C^\alpha\) with \((\alpha, A) = (0.53, 0.29)\). Extrapolated to \(3.8\times10^{25}\) FLOPs it suggested 402B parameters on 16.55T tokens; the curves "become flatter around the minimum as the compute budget increases", so they chose 405B [Llama 3, §3.2.1]. The same runs forecast benchmarks in two steps (log-likelihood against FLOPs, then a sigmoid to accuracy) and chose the data mix.

The flagship sits near Chinchilla (\(15.6\text{T}/405.85\text{B} = 38\) tokens per parameter); the small models do not. The paper trains "smaller models for much longer than is compute-optimal", which "perform better than compute-optimal models at the same inference budget", and uses the flagship to improve them "during post-training" [Llama 3, §1]. The launch post adds that Chinchilla-optimal for an 8B model is about 200B tokens, yet "both our 8B and 70B parameter models continued to improve log-linearly after we trained them on up to 15T tokens" [Meta 2024]. That is Llama 1's argument pushed 12 times further.

The three numbers: Llama 1 7B, \(1.0\text{T}/6.74\text{B} = 148\); Llama 2 70B, \(2.0\text{T}/68.98\text{B} = 29\); Llama-3-8B, \(15\text{T}/8.03\text{B} = 1{,}868\), 93 times Chinchilla's 20. The target moved from "beat GPT-3 at 13B" to "the best model you can run yourself". Over-training costs more training compute and saves inference FLOPs on every request; the break-even is worked out in the design question below and on page 13.

Data and the training recipe

The corpus is "about 15T multilingual tokens", against "1.8T tokens for Llama 2" in the Llama 3 paper (the Llama 2 paper says 2T, so quote the source you cite); the launch post adds seven times Llama 2's data and four times more code. The final mix is roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% math and reasoning, 17% code and 8% multilingual, chosen with a topic classifier and small-model scaling-law runs (predict the large model's results on each candidate mix) and adjusted during training [Llama 3, §3.1, §3.4.1]. Over the last 40M tokens the learning rate anneals 退火 linearly to 0 at 128K context on a mix upsampling high-quality code and math, with checkpoint averaging; on the 8B this lifted GSM8K and MATH by 24.0% and 6.4%, on the 405B the gains were "negligible", and the experiment used those benchmarks' training sets, so it partly measures format learning (page 12).

The 405B recipe is AdamW at peak LR \(8\times10^{-5}\), 8,000 warmup steps, cosine to \(8\times10^{-7}\) over 1.2M steps (page 14). The batch is 4M tokens at length 4,096, doubled to 8M at length 8,192 after 252M tokens and to 16M after 2.87T, "a lower batch size early in training to improve training stability", with "few loss spikes". These stages sum to \(63{,}000 + 327{,}250 + 795{,}625 \approx 1.19\)M steps for 15.6T tokens, consistent with the 1.2M-step horizon (our check).

Now the reliability lesson. In a 54-day snapshot of pre-training there were 466 job interruptions: 47 planned and 419 unexpected. About 78% of the unexpected ones were confirmed or suspected hardware, GPU issues alone were 58.7% (faulty GPUs 30.1%, HBM3 memory 17.2%), the next largest causes were software bugs (12.9%), network switches and cables (8.4%) and unplanned host maintenance (7.6%), only three needed manual intervention, and effective training time stayed above 90% [Llama 3, §3.3.4, Tab. 5]. The arithmetic an interviewer wants:

  • Rate: \(419/54 = 7.8\) unexpected interruptions a day, one every \(54 \cdot 24/419 = 3.1\) hours.
  • Per GPU reliable, cluster not: \(419/(54 \cdot 24 \cdot 16{,}384) = 1.97\times10^{-5}\) per GPU-hour, an MTBF of about 50,700 GPU-hours (5.8 years). In synchronous training one failure restarts the whole job, so the job's failure rate scales with the GPU count: at the same per-GPU rate and independent failures, 100K GPUs would see one every 30 minutes (an extrapolation).
  • What "above 90%" implies: if it holds for this window, at most 10% of \(54 \cdot 1440 = 77{,}760\) minutes was lost across 466 interruptions, so the average interruption cost under \(7776/466 \approx 17\) minutes end to end (detection, restart, rollback). Hence the emphasis on fast diagnosis (NCCL flight recorder, straggler tools), cheap checkpoints and automation over prevention. The paper also notes a 1 to 2% diurnal throughput swing from temperature and power swings of "tens of megawatts".

The table lists "Faulty GPU" as 148 interruptions, 30.1%. From your jet tagger

A tagger trains for hours on a few GPUs, so a crash is an anecdote and you restart from the last epoch. Long context: six stages at the very end

Llama 3 pretrains at 8K and extends to 128K only "in the final stages of pre-training", because "the compute in self-attention layers grows quadratically in the sequence length". The context grows in six stages on about 800B tokens (5% of 15.6T), each until short-context evaluations recover and needle-in-a-haystack is perfect [Llama 3, §3.4.2]. The ingredients are the 500,000 base, the document mask, 16-way context parallelism, and the 3.1 rope_scaling entry (factor 8, low and high frequency factors 1 and 4, original length 8,192; derived on page 10). "Supports 128K" is not "uses 128K": on NoLiMa the effective length is 2K for Llama 3.1 405B and 70B and 1K for the 8B [Modarressi et al. 2025, Tab. 3].

Post-training: rejection sampling, SFT and DPO, six rounds

Each of six rounds [Llama 3, §4]: (1) collect preference pairs from two recent models, rated on four levels, with an edit step (edited \(\succ\) chosen \(\succ\) rejected), keeping only "significantly better" and "better"; (2) train one reward model on all data so far, with no margin term ("diminishing improvements after data scaling"); (3) rejection-sample \(K\) = 10 to 30 answers per prompt and keep the RM's favorite; (4) SFT on that plus synthetic and human data (LR \(10^{-5}\), 8.5K to 9K steps); (5) DPO 直接偏好优化 on the most recent preference batches (LR \(10^{-5}\), \(\beta = 0.1\)), with header and termination tokens masked out of the loss (they otherwise cause "tail repetition or abruptly generating termination tokens") and an NLL term of coefficient 0.2 on the chosen answer; (6) average models across runs.

DPO instead of PPO in Llama 3
Problem
Llama 2's RS+PPO worked at 70B, but PPO puts generation inside the training loop and needs an actor, critic, reference and reward model (page 23); Llama 2 measured 330 s per 70B iteration and a roughly 20 times generation slowdown under FSDP. At 405B on a synchronous 16K-GPU job that is a large, fragile system.
Idea
Do reward maximization offline (best-of-\(K\) plus SFT) and preference learning with DPO, a supervised loss on stored pairs, using data from the previous round's best models.
Why it works
Each round's pairs come from a policy close to the one being trained, so the offline loss is nearly on-policy. DPO needs forward passes only, with a frozen reference whose log-probabilities can be cached, and no critic (page 21). Six rounds restore some of online RL's iteration.
Evidence
One sentence, no numbers: they "explored on-policy algorithms such as PPO, but found that DPO required less compute for large-scale models and performed better, especially on instruction following benchmarks like IFEval" [Llama 3, §4.1.4]. External evidence is mixed: Tulu 3 found PPO comparable and slower, Xu et al. found PPO ahead on code (page 21).
Cost
Offline pairs limit exploration, DPO's failure modes needed patches (token mask, NLL term), and it is weaker where rewards are verifiable: reasoning, code, math.
Used by
Llama 3.x, Zephyr-style open recipes, Tulu 3. Llama 4 keeps only a "lightweight" DPO at the end (below).

Two caveats: it rests on one sentence, not an ablation, and Meta's accounts differ. The April 2024 post lists "supervised fine-tuning (SFT), rejection sampling, proximal policy optimization (PPO), and direct preference optimization (DPO)" [Meta 2024], while the July paper describes SFT, RS and DPO only. We cannot say whether the April models used PPO.

Llama 3.2 (September 2024) made the 1B and 3B by single-shot structured pruning of Llama 3.1 8B, then pretraining with the 8B and 70B models' logits as token-level distillation targets [Meta 2024b]; both tie embedding and head (checked in the drill; page 19). Llama 3.3 (6 December 2024) is a 70B instruct model whose card lists the same 7.0M GPU-hours as 3.1 70B [Llama 3.3 card], suggesting the gain came from post-training (our inference).

iRoPE: interleaved RoPE and position-free layers

Meta describes the idea in three sentences: "A key innovation in the Llama 4 architecture is the use of interleaved attention layers without positional embeddings. Additionally, we employ inference time temperature scaling of attention to enhance length generalization. We call this the iRoPE architecture, where 'i' stands for 'interleaved' attention layers, highlighting the long-term goal of supporting 'infinite' context length, and 'RoPE' refers to the rotary position embeddings employed in most layers." [Meta 2025]. The mechanism is in the released config and the Hugging Face implementation:

  1. Layer pattern. In every group of four layers, three use RoPE and attend only within fixed 8,192-token chunks (attention_chunk_size); the fourth has no positional encoding (NoPE) and attends over the whole context. That is 12 global layers of 48 in both models.
  2. Temperature scaling, on the NoPE layers only. The query at position \(p\) is multiplied by \(s(p) = 1 + 0.1 \ln\!\big(\lfloor (p+1)/8192 \rfloor + 1\big)\) (attn_scale 0.1, floor_scale 8192): exactly 1 below 8,191, then 1.28 at 131,072, 1.49 at 1M, 1.72 at 10.5M. Scaling \(q\) scales all logits, a sharper softmax that counters dilution over more keys. The Hugging Face code cites Scalable-Softmax for it [Nakanishi 2025]. (Scout also applies a parameter-free L2 QK-norm on its RoPE layers; Maverick's config does not.)
iRoPE
Problem
RoPE generalizes poorly to unseen relative distances and patching it (interpolation, YaRN) is a heuristic (page 10); softmax attention dilutes as keys multiply; and a KV cache growing in every layer makes very long contexts unaffordable.
Idea
Split the depth: most layers see a bounded window with RoPE, so their positions stay in the trained range at any total length; a minority see everything with no positional code and a length-dependent temperature.
Why it works
Local layers never meet a relative distance above 8,191, so nothing needs extrapolating; global layers have no rotation phases to extrapolate, and a causal decoder can infer order from the mask (NoPE, page 05). The logarithmic temperature offsets growing attention entropy. Only 12 of 48 layers grow a cache with \(T\).
Evidence
Needle retrieval and cumulative NLL over 10M tokens of code for Scout, "pre-trained and post-trained with a 256K context length" (so 10M is 40 times the trained length). No ablation.
Cost
Information must cross chunk boundaries through the 12 global layers alone; NoPE layers give no explicit order; and a long window is not a long effective context (below).
Used by
Llama 4. The same shape (RoPE on local layers, none on global) appears in the dense Muse Glimmer 30B (below); hybrids are the subject of page 09.

Worked numbers: the KV cache. Each layer stores \(2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 4096\) bytes per token in bf16. All 48 layers global would be 192 KiB per token. From your jet tagger

Your tagger had no positional encoding because a jet is a set: permutation invariance was the goal. A causal language model is the opposite case, yet Llama 4's position-free layers work: the causal mask alone breaks the symmetry, since each token sees a different number of predecessors, and attention can recover position from that. The layers are not order-invariant, they are just not told the order. The 3-to-1 interleaving bets that a few such layers can do long-range retrieval while the RoPE layers handle local order.

Llama 4: training recipe, reception, and what came after

Training: early fusion, MetaP, FP8, distillation, online RL

All from Meta's launch post [Meta 2025], which gives few numbers and no ablations.

  • Early fusion 早期融合: text and vision tokens enter one backbone from the start, jointly pre-trained on text, image and video. The vision encoder is MetaCLIP-based, "trained separately in conjunction with a frozen Llama model". Llama 3's vision models instead attached adapters to a finished text model [Llama 3, §7].
  • MetaP sets "per-layer learning rates and initialization scales" that "transfer well across different values of batch size, model width, depth, and training tokens". Nothing else is disclosed; the goal matches \(\mu\)P (page 14).
  • FP8: Behemoth pre-training "using FP8 and 32K GPUs" reached 390 TFLOPs/GPU. The post does not name the peak: it is 39% of dense bf16 (989) or 20% of dense FP8 (1,979; page 04), so do not read it as an MFU without asking.
  • Data: 200 languages, 10 times Llama 3's multilingual tokens, and a mid-training stage for long-context extension. By the cards, Scout saw about \(40\text{T}/17.17\text{B} = 2{,}330\) tokens per active parameter and Maverick \(22\text{T}/17.18\text{B} = 1{,}280\) (Figure 2).
  • Codistillation (knowledge distillation 知识蒸馏): Maverick was codistilled from Behemoth with "a novel distillation loss function that dynamically weights the soft and hard targets through training", which during pre-training "amortizes the computational cost" of the teacher's forward passes (page 19).
  • Post-training reversed Llama 3: lightweight SFT, then online RL, then lightweight DPO, because "SFT and DPO can over-constrain the model, restricting exploration during the online RL stage and leading to suboptimal accuracy, particularly in reasoning, coding, and math domains". They dropped over 50% of SFT data tagged easy (95% for Behemoth) and ran continuous online RL on medium-to-hard prompts only, with a fully asynchronous framework reported as about 10 times more efficient (page 24, page 25).

A year earlier RL was "less stable and harder to scale"; by April 2025 Meta found the offline stages capped reasoning gains. The turn toward online RL is the field's, not only Meta's.

Reception: what happened, from primary sources

Llama 4 launched on a Saturday and the reception was mixed. Stated in Meta's and LMArena's own words:

  • The post said Maverick "offers a best-in-class performance to cost ratio with an experimental chat version scoring ELO of 1417 on LMArena".
  • LMArena, 8 April 2025: "Meta's interpretation of our policy did not match what we expect from model providers. A preference arena measures style as well as capability, so a tuned variant can outrank the model people download. New architectures were served inconsistently at first, so early third-party numbers can reflect implementation bugs. Which choices other labs adopted

Widely. The block (pre-RMSNorm, gated FFN, RoPE, no biases) is the default of open dense models, with variants in Qwen, Mistral, DeepSeek LLM and OLMo 2 (pages 04, 05; DeepSeek LLM's 67B was "LLaMA-shaped", page 26). So are GQA-8 (page 07) and late, staged long-context training with a raised RoPE base and rescaling (page 10). Reward-model-filtered SFT followed by DPO became a standard open recipe (Llama 3, Tulu 3; page 21), before the field moved toward online RL, as Llama 4 did (page 24). Llama 4 took shared-plus-routed experts from the DeepSeekMoE line (page 08).

Not. A 405B dense flagship found few followers: the large open models that followed are sparse (DeepSeek-V3 with 671B total and 37B active, Kimi K2 with 1.04T and 32.6B, Qwen3-235B-A22B; page 08). What Llama contributed. Almost every ingredient was borrowed: pre-norm from GPT-3, SwiGLU from PaLM, RoPE from Su et al. via GPT-Neo, GQA from Ainslie et al., the RoPE-base change from Code Llama and Xiong et al., sparse experts from the GShard, Mixtral and DeepSeek line. Llama added integration at scale, released openly and documented: the tokens-per-parameter bet (Llama 1), the first open end-to-end RLHF account (Llama 2), and the most complete public account of a frontier run, with failure statistics (Llama 3). The interview frame: for each choice, name where it came from, the problem it solved, and the evidence Meta gave. On architecture that is usually "borrowed, with no ablation"; on systems and data, "measured, at scale".

Common misconceptions

  • "Llama 3 was a big architectural step." The paper says the opposite: the model "does not deviate significantly" from Llama 2, and the gains "are primarily driven by" data quality and scale. The architecture changes are four small ones (GQA-8 everywhere, 128K vocabulary, RoPE base 500,000, document mask).
  • "Llama followed the Chinchilla recipe." Llama 1 was written as a rebuttal of its objective: the 7B saw 148 tokens per parameter and the Llama 3 8B saw about 1,870, against Chinchilla's 20. Only the largest model of each early generation sits near 20.
  • "17B active means it costs like a 17B dense model to host." FLOPs per token do, but memory does not: all 400B parameters must be resident (401 GB in FP8). The "17B" also includes 2.07B of embedding and head parameters.
  • "Tokens per parameter for an MoE is tokens over total parameters." Use activated parameters, as Figure 2 does, or the ratio understates the training by an order of magnitude or more.
  • "A 128K (or 10M) window means the model works at that length." The window is what the architecture accepts. On NoLiMa the effective length of Llama 3.1 405B is 2K, of Scout 1K, of Maverick 2K.
  • "iRoPE is a new RoPE scaling rule." It is a layer-pattern architecture: RoPE with chunked local attention in three of four layers, no positional encoding with global, temperature-scaled attention in the fourth. The scaling of position frequencies (page 10) is a different mechanism.
  • "Llama 3 used PPO like Llama 2." Llama 2 used rejection sampling and then PPO; the Llama 3 paper describes rejection sampling, SFT and DPO, and says it tried PPO and preferred DPO. (Meta's April 2024 launch post still lists PPO for the 8B and 70B.)
  • "Numbers in a big technical report are internally consistent." The Llama 3 paper's Table 5 has a cell that contradicts its own percentage, its vocabulary is 128,000 in one place and 128,256 in the checkpoints, and it says 1.8T tokens for Llama 2 where the Llama 2 paper says 2T. Sanity-check every number you plan to quote.

Interview questions

Warm-up What did Llama 1 change relative to the original transformer and GPT-3, and why?

Three architectural changes, each borrowed, and one change of training regime. Pre-normalization with RMSNorm: normalize the input of each sublayer, not the output, for stability (GPT-3 already used pre-norm; RMSNorm drops LayerNorm's mean subtraction and bias to be cheaper). SwiGLU instead of ReLU or GELU, with hidden width \(\tfrac23 \cdot 4d\) so that three matrices (\(3 \cdot d \cdot \tfrac83 d = 8d^2\)) cost the same as the two-matrix \(4d\) FFN (\(2 \cdot d \cdot 4d = 8d^2\)). RoPE at every layer in place of learned absolute position embeddings, so attention depends on relative offset. Also no biases, and dense causal attention throughout, with no GPT-3-style banded sparse layers.

The bigger change was the regime: 1.0T to 1.4T tokens on public data only, so the 7B trained on 148 tokens per parameter, against GPT-3's 300B tokens for 175B parameters. The motivation is inference cost: Chinchilla-optimal training minimizes training compute, not serving compute, so "a smaller one trained longer will ultimately be cheaper at inference". The paper gives no ablations for the architecture changes: it credits GPT-3, PaLM and GPT-Neo.

Warm-up Compute tokens per parameter for Llama 1 7B, Llama 2 70B and Llama 3 8B. What is the trend and why?

Llama 1 7B: \(1.0\text{T} / 6.74\text{B} = 148\). Llama 2 70B: \(2.0\text{T} / 68.98\text{B} = 29\). Llama-3-8B: \(\approx 15\text{T} / 8.03\text{B} = 1{,}868\). Chinchilla's optimum is about 20, so these are 7.4, 1.4 and 93 times it. The smallest model of each family went 148, 297 (Llama 2 7B, 2T), 1,868: each generation pushed its small models further past the compute-optimal point. The flagships stayed near it (65B: 21, 70B: 29, 405B: 38).

Why: the quantity being optimized is total cost including serving. A small model trained on a lot of data is worse per training FLOP but cheaper on every request, and Llama's target shifted from "beat GPT-3" to "the best model people can run themselves". Llama 3 also reports that its 8B and 70B kept improving log-linearly up to 15T tokens. The limit is data supply and diminishing returns per token.

Warm-up Derive Llama-3-8B's FFN width of 14,336 from \(d = 4096\). Why is it not \(\tfrac83 d\)?

Meta's reference code: \(h = 4d = 16384\); \(h \leftarrow \lfloor 2h/3 \rfloor = 10922\); multiply by the config's ffn_dim_multiplier 1.3 and truncate, \(\lfloor 14198.6 \rfloor = 14198\); round up to a multiple of multiple_of = 1024, which is \(14 \times 1024 = 14336\). The 70B (multiplier 1.3, multiple of 4096) gives 28,672 and the 405B (1.2, 4096) gives 53,248.

\(\tfrac83 d \approx 10{,}923\) would have been rounded up to 11,008 with Llama 1's multiple of 256. Llama 3's \(\dff/d = 3.5\) is 30% wider. The paper does not explain it, but the accounting shows the trade: GQA-8 freed 805M attention parameters, and 1.31B were spent on the wider FFN (with 0.79B on the larger vocabulary, that is exactly the 6.74B to 8.03B step from Llama 2 7B). The round multiples plausibly align with tensor-core tiles and the 8-way TP split (14,336/8 = 1,792 columns per GPU); the paper does not say.

Core Why did Llama 3 use DPO rather than PPO in post-training?

What the paper says: it "explored on-policy algorithms such as PPO, but found that DPO required less compute for large-scale models and performed better, especially on instruction following benchmarks like IFEval", and it wanted "a relatively simple post-training procedure" instead of RL that "tends to be less stable and harder to scale". No numbers are given.

The mechanism behind it: PPO needs sampling inside the training loop and four models (actor, critic, reference, reward). Llama 2 measured 330 seconds per 70B PPO iteration and a roughly 20 times generation slowdown under FSDP, and at 405B on a synchronous 16K-GPU job that is a large source of complexity. DPO is a supervised loss on stored pairs with a frozen reference. Llama 3 keeps it nearly on-policy by training on the latest batches of preference data collected with the previous round's best models, six rounds in all, while rejection sampling (best-of-\(K\), \(K\) = 10 to 30, then SFT) does the reward-maximizing part offline. They also patch DPO's known problems: header and termination tokens are masked out of the loss and an NLL term (coefficient 0.2) on the chosen answer stops its likelihood falling. Caveats: external comparisons are mixed (Tulu 3: PPO comparable, slower; Xu et al.: PPO ahead on code), and Llama 4 later reversed course, arguing that SFT and DPO "can over-constrain the model, restricting exploration during the online RL stage".

Core What does the Llama 3 paper teach about infrastructure reliability at 16K GPUs?

In a 54-day window there were 466 interruptions: 47 planned and 419 unexpected. About 78% of the unexpected ones were hardware (confirmed or suspected), GPU issues alone were 58.7%, and only three needed manual intervention. That is 7.8 unexpected interruptions a day, one every 3.1 hours. Per GPU that is \(419/(54 \cdot 24 \cdot 16384) = 1.97\times10^{-5}\) per hour, an MTBF of about 50,700 GPU-hours (5.8 years), so each GPU is reliable and the job is not: in synchronous training the failure rate scales with the number of GPUs, and at the same rate 100K GPUs would see one every 30 minutes (extrapolation).

Effective training time stayed above 90%, which (if it holds for this window) bounds the average cost per interruption (detection, restart, rollback) to about \(0.1 \cdot 77{,}760/466 \approx 17\) minutes. So the levers are: fast checkpointing and restart, automated diagnosis (NCCL flight recorder, straggler detection), scheduler automation, and a design that avoids extra risk, which is why the paper chose a dense model and simple post-training ("managing complexity"). Also relevant: a 1 to 2% diurnal throughput swing from temperature, power swings of tens of megawatts, and that Table 5's "Faulty GPU" row (148, 30.1%) is internally inconsistent (148/419 is 35.3%), so cross-check numbers before quoting them.

Per layer: attention \(2 \cdot 16384^2 + 2 \cdot 16384 \cdot 1024 = 570{,}425{,}344\); FFN \(3 \cdot 16384 \cdot 53248 = 2{,}617{,}245{,}696\); norms \(32{,}768\); total 3,187,703,808. Core Why did Llama 3 move to a 128K vocabulary, and what does it cost?

Tokenizer efficiency. The new vocabulary is 100K tiktoken BPE tokens plus 28K for non-English languages, and it raises English compression from 3.17 to 3.94 characters per token. The same text becomes \(3.17/3.94 = 80.5\%\) as many tokens: 24% more text per training FLOP, per KV-cache byte and per context window, and cheaper generation per character. The added non-English tokens improved compression and downstream performance with no impact on English tokenization.

Costs: embedding and head are \(2 \cdot 128256 \cdot 4096 = 1.05\text{B}\) parameters in the 8B (13.1% of the model, against 3.9% for Llama 2 7B, an extra 0.79B parameters); the logits tensor and softmax are 4 times wider; rare tokens get fewer gradient updates. The paper reports no isolated quality ablation.

Core Design question: you need a model of a given quality to serve about a trillion tokens. How do you decide between a compute-optimal large model and an over-trained small one?

Minimize total FLOPs: training \(6ND\) plus serving \(2N\) per generated token. For two models of equal quality, \((N_1, D_1)\) compute-optimal and \((N_2, D_2)\) smaller and over-trained, the small one wins once the served tokens \(T\) exceed \(T^\star = 3\,(N_2 D_2 - N_1 D_1)/(N_1 - N_2)\). Illustration with Llama-like numbers: \(N_1 = 70\text{B}\), \(D_1 = 1.4\text{T}\), \(N_2 = 8\text{B}\), \(D_2 = 15\text{T}\) gives \(T^\star = 3 \cdot (1.2\times10^{23} - 9.8\times10^{22})/(62\times10^{9}) \approx 1.06\times10^{12}\) tokens. Above about a trillion served tokens the 8B is cheaper in total FLOPs, and below it the larger model is.

Caveats that a strong answer raises: the equal-quality assumption is the weak link (it must come from a scaling fit, and the two models above are not equal); FLOPs are not dollars (decode is memory-bound, so batch size and hardware matter, page 17); data supply caps \(D\) (repeated epochs give less); latency and on-device constraints can force the small model regardless; and a large teacher can improve the small model in post-training, as Llama 3's flagship did.

Core How did Llama 3.1 reach a 128K context, and why is it staged at the end of pre-training?

Pretraining is at 8K. In the final stage the context grows in six increments from 8K to 128K over about 800B tokens (5% of the total), each run until short-context evaluations have fully recovered and needle-in-a-haystack is solved perfectly at that length. It is late because self-attention cost grows quadratically with length, so long sequences early would waste FLOPs on a model that has not yet learned the basics. Enablers: RoPE base 500,000 from the start (Xiong et al. showed it effective to 32K), a document mask so packed unrelated documents do not attend to each other, GQA so context-parallel all-gathers of K and V are cheap (the 128K stage uses TP8, CP16, PP16, DP8 at 38% MFU), and the 3.1 llama3 frequency rescaling (factor 8 over the original 8,192 positions, page 10).

Caveat: a 128K window is not 128K of usable context. On NoLiMa the 405B and 70B have effective length 2K and the 8B 1K.

Core Describe Llama 2's RLHF recipe and justify two reward models, rejection sampling before PPO, and Ghost Attention.

Pipeline: SFT on 27,540 high-quality annotations; human preference collection in weekly batches (over 1.4M Meta comparisons, four-level ratings); two reward models, helpfulness and safety, initialized from chat checkpoints, with a ranking loss plus a preference-strength margin; then iterative RLHF versions V1 to V5, using rejection sampling only up to V4 and rejection sampling followed by PPO after that.

Why: helpfulness and safety trade off, so a single RM is confused and "optimizing two separate models eases the reward modeling task" (safety RM output takes priority below a 0.15 threshold). Rejection sampling (best of \(K\), fine-tune on it, done with the 70B and distilled down) gives breadth, exploring \(K\) samples per prompt, cheaply, and PPO gives depth, training on its own latest samples with a KL penalty (0.01 or 0.005) against reward hacking. Ghost Attention fixes multi-turn forgetting of a system instruction: prepend the instruction to every user turn when sampling, then train with it only in the first turn and loss zeroed on earlier turns. Cost: PPO iterations of about 330 s at 70B and a large FSDP generation slowdown, the seeds of Llama 3's decision to drop PPO.

Deep-dive What is iRoPE and what problem is it meant to solve?

iRoPE is Llama 4's layer pattern for long context: "interleaved" attention layers, most with RoPE and some with no positional embedding, plus inference-time temperature scaling of attention. In the released config, three of every four layers use RoPE (\(\theta\) = 500,000) with chunked local attention over 8,192-token chunks, and every fourth layer (12 of 48) is a NoPE layer with full attention. On those layers the query is scaled by \(s(p) = 1 + 0.1\ln(\lfloor (p+1)/8192 \rfloor + 1)\): 1 up to 8,190, 1.28 at 131K, 1.72 at 10.5M, sharpening the softmax as the number of keys grows.

Problems addressed: (1) RoPE extrapolation, since the local layers never see a relative distance beyond 8,191 and the global layers have no rotation phases to go out of range, so length generalization no longer depends on interpolating frequencies; (2) attention dilution over millions of keys, countered by the logarithmic temperature (in the spirit of Scalable-Softmax); (3) cache growth, since only 12 layers keep \(T\) tokens. Scout was trained at 256K and claimed to 10M, with evidence from needle retrieval and NLL over code, no ablation, and independent NoLiMa results (effective length 1K, 21.6 at 32K against a base of 81.7). It is not a new frequency-scaling rule.

Deep-dive Why might Meta have moved Llama 4 to MoE? What are the trade-offs?

Meta's stated reason is that MoE is "more compute efficient for training and inference" and, at fixed training FLOPs, gives higher quality than a dense model. The numbers support the efficiency part: Maverick activates 17.2B parameters against 405.9B, and excluding embeddings that is \(2 \times 16.15\text{B} = 32\) GFLOPs per token against \(2 \times 403.75\text{B} = 808\) GFLOPs, about 25 times fewer. The same lever Llama 1 pulled (cost at serving time) applies, with serving targets of one H100 for Scout in Int4 and one 8-GPU host for Maverick in FP8. Llama 3 had argued against MoE for training stability at 405B, but by early 2025 large sparse runs were established (DeepSeek-V3: 671B total, 37B active), and the comparison that mattered was active parameters (Meta compares Maverick with DeepSeek-V3 "at less than half the active parameters").

Trade-offs: memory is set by total parameters (401 GB in FP8, 803 GB in bf16, against 406 GB and 812 GB for a 405B dense), so MoE serving trades FLOPs for memory and interconnect; expert parallelism and load balancing add engineering; batches touch many experts so decode bandwidth is closer to the total than the active size; and it is harder to fine-tune. Our reading of why it happened, as opposed to what Meta said, is the falling risk (external proof of stability) plus the competitive pressure on active-parameter benchmarks.

Deep-dive Design question: you must serve Llama 4 Scout with 10M-token prompts. Size the problem, and say how you would validate the claim.

Memory. Per token each layer stores \(2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 4096\) B in bf16. Only 12 global layers grow with \(T\); the 36 chunked layers hold at most 8,192 tokens. At \(T = 10{,}485{,}760\): \(12 \cdot 4096 \cdot T = 480\) GiB plus \(36 \cdot 4096 \cdot 8192 = 1.1\) GiB, so 481 GiB per sequence (1,920 GiB if all layers were global). Weights are 54 GB in Int4 or 109 GB in FP8. "Fits on one H100" is the weights only: one sequence needs more than six H100s of memory for KV alone in bf16, about half of that with an FP8 cache, and batching multiplies it.

Compute. Prefill on the 12 global layers costs about \(2 T^2 d_{\text{attn}}\) FLOPs each with \(d_{\text{attn}} = 5120\): \(1.13\times10^{18}\) per layer, \(1.35\times10^{19}\) in total, against \(3.4\times10^{17}\) for all the linear layers (\(2 \cdot 16.15\text{B} \cdot T\)). Attention is 97% of prefill, so on 8 H100s at 40% MFU a single 10M-token prefill takes about 73 minutes (our estimate). Decode reads the whole cache each step: 481 GiB across 8 GPUs at 3.35 TB/s is about 19 ms per token from KV alone. Mitigations: KV quantization, prefix caching, chunked prefill, sequence or context parallelism, sparse or retrieval-based attention (pages 09, 17).

Validation. Do not rely on literal needle-in-a-haystack. Test at 32K, 128K, 1M and beyond with tasks that need association without lexical overlap (NoLiMa: Scout's effective length 1K), multi-hop and aggregation tasks (RULER), your own documents, and per-position accuracy curves, with the exact serving stack, since new architectures were served inconsistently at launch. Report the effective length, not the window.
