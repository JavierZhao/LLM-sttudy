Llama 1 to 4

Llama defined the standard recipe of open dense language models, its third paper is the most detailed public account of training a frontier dense model (405B parameters on 16K GPUs), and its fourth generation switched to mixture of experts and had a rocky reception. This page follows the lineage generation by generation, gives the reason for each change and the numbers that let you check it, and links back to the mechanism pages instead of re-deriving them.

Four generations in one figure

Llama's public history is short: February 2023 to April 2025 for language models, four generations. Each generation attacked a different bottleneck, and the design choices follow from it.

  • Llama 1 (Feb 2023) asked which model is cheapest to serve for a target quality, and answered: a small model trained on far more tokens than compute-optimal.
  • Llama 2 (Jul 2023) made that base model usable as an assistant: 2T tokens, 4K context, GQA for the large sizes, and a full open RLHF recipe.
  • Llama 3 (2024) scaled the same block: 15T tokens, a 405B flagship on 16K GPUs, and a paper that documents the engineering. Its theme is "managing complexity".
  • Llama 4 (Apr 2025) changed the block: mixture of experts, native multimodality, and interleaved RoPE and position-free attention layers for context length.

The rest of the page takes the generations in order. The thread to follow is serving cost: Llama 1 chose tokens over parameters to make inference cheap, Llama 3 kept doing so for its small models, and Llama 4 attacks FLOPs per token directly with sparsity. The counter-thread is risk: Llama 3 explicitly avoided MoE and RL to keep a 405B run stable, and Llama 4 took both risks.

Llama 1 (February 2023): the overtraining bet and the standard block

The bet: pay for tokens now, save on inference forever

The scaling-law result of Hoffmann et al. (Chinchilla) tells you how to spend a training budget: roughly 20 tokens per parameter (page 13). Llama 1 argued that this answers the wrong question for a model that will be served millions of times. The paper says the Chinchilla objective "disregards the inference budget", that "given a target level of performance, the preferred model is not the fastest to train but the fastest at inference", and that "a smaller one trained longer will ultimately be cheaper at inference" [Touvron et al. 2023, §1]. Their evidence was empirical: "although Hoffmann et al. (2022) recommends training a 10B model on 200B tokens, we find that the performance of a 7B model continues to improve even after 1T tokens."

So the four sizes were trained for 1.0T (7B, 13B) and 1.4T tokens (33B, 65B), all with a 4M-token batch and a 2,000-step warmup [Llama 1, Tab. 2, §2.3]. Tokens per parameter, using exact parameter counts of the released checkpoints (the drill reproduces them):

The block: three borrowed ideas

Llama 1's architecture section lists three changes to the original transformer, each with its source in brackets [Llama 1, §2.2]. None was new in 2023. The contribution was the combination, trained at scale and released.

The Llama block: pre-norm RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE
Problem
The original transformer normalizes after each sublayer (post-norm), uses a ReLU FFN of width \(4d\) and adds absolute position embeddings once at the input. Deep post-norm stacks are hard to train without careful warmup, and absolute positions are tied to the training length.
Idea
Pre-normalization with RMSNorm 均方根归一化 on the input of each sublayer [credited to GPT-3]; SwiGLU instead of ReLU, with hidden width \(\tfrac23\cdot 4d\) [PaLM]; rotary embeddings 旋转位置编码 at every layer instead of absolute positions [GPT-Neo].
Why it works
Pre-norm keeps an identity path through the residual stream, which is what makes depth trainable (page 04). RMSNorm drops LayerNorm's mean subtraction and bias, so it is cheaper. The gated FFN uses three matrices, so width \(\tfrac83 d\) gives \(3 \cdot d \cdot \tfrac83 d = 8d^2\) parameters, exactly the \(2 \cdot d \cdot 4d\) of the ReLU FFN it replaces. RoPE rotates queries and keys so that attention scores depend on relative offset, at every layer (page 05).
Evidence
The paper reports no ablation of these three choices: it cites the works they come from. The evidence is that the resulting models are strong (below) and that most open dense models since have used the same block.
Cost
Nothing measurable at this scale. Pre-norm is known to leave the deepest layers doing little (page 04). Full multi-head attention means a KV cache of 512 KiB per token for the 7B (next section).
Used by
Llama 1 to 4, and close variants in Qwen, Mistral, DeepSeek LLM and OLMo 2 (pages 04 and 05 list the families).

Two further details are part of the recipe. Every linear layer is bias-free: a bias-free count reproduces the parameter total of each released checkpoint to the digit (for example 6,738,415,616 for the 7B), so no bias tensors exist. And the input embedding and output head are separate matrices (untied). The tokenizer is SentencePiece BPE with byte fallback for unknown UTF-8 characters, a 32K vocabulary, and every number split into single digits [Llama 1, §2.1], [Llama 2, §2.2] (page 02).

Worked numbers: the 65B training run

Llama 1 reports throughput rather than MFU: about 380 tokens per second per GPU on 2,048 A100-80GB GPUs, so that 1.4T tokens take about 21 days [Llama 1, §2.4]. Check it, then convert:

  • Cluster throughput: \(380 \times 2048 = 778{,}240\) tokens/s, so \(1.4\times10^{12} / 778{,}240 = 1.80\times10^{6}\) s \(= 20.8\) days. The paper's carbon table lists 1,022,362 GPU-hours for the 65B, and \(1{,}022{,}362 / 2048 = 499\) hours \(= 20.8\) days [Llama 1, Tab. 15].
  • Per-GPU FLOP rate by the \(6N\) rule: \(380 \times 6 \times 65.29\times10^{9} = 1.49\times10^{14}\) FLOP/s, which is 48% of the A100's 312 TFLOP/s dense bf16 peak (page 16). This is our arithmetic and a floor, since \(6N\) ignores the attention term.

That is a high number for 2023. The paper credits an efficient causal-attention implementation that does not store attention weights, hand-written backward passes that save the expensive activations instead of recomputing them, and overlap of communication with computation [Llama 1, §2.4] (page 15, page 16).

GQA only where the cache hurts

Only the 34B and 70B use GQA, with 8 KV heads. The paper's stated purpose is "inference scalability for our larger models" and it gives two arguments [Llama 2, App. A.2.1, Tab. 18]. First, a 30B ablation on 150B tokens: GQA "performs comparably to the MHA baseline on most evaluation tasks and is better than the MQA variant on average", with the FFN widened by 1.3 (GQA) or 1.33 (MQA) to keep parameter counts equal. Second, the largest models are served on 8 A100s with tensor parallelism, and an MQA head cannot be split across 8 GPUs, so it would be duplicated and the cache would be no smaller than GQA's. The mechanism is the subject of page 07. The stakes, per token with 128-dim heads in bf16:

  • Llama 2 7B (MHA): \(32 \text{ layers} \times 2 \times 32 \text{ heads} \times 128 \times 2\text{ B} = 524{,}288\) B = 512 KiB, or 2 GiB per 4K-token sequence.
  • Llama 2 70B with GQA-8: \(80 \times 2 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 327{,}680\) B = 320 KiB, or 1.25 GiB per sequence. With plain MHA (64 KV heads) it would be \(80 \times 2 \times 64 \times 128 \times 2 = 2{,}621{,}440\) B = 2.5 MiB, 8 times more: 10 GiB per 4K sequence.

The 7B and 13B kept MHA because their caches were small relative to the serving budget. Llama 3 then adopted GQA-8 at every size, including the 8B, once the target became long contexts and cheap serving everywhere (below).

Code Llama and the RoPE base

One Llama 2 offshoot changed the recipe of everything after it. Code Llama (August 2023) continued Llama 2 on code, then added a long-context fine-tuning stage on 16,384-token sequences (4 times Llama 2's 4,096) in which "we increase the base period \(\theta\) from 10,000 to 1,000,000" [Rozière et al. 2023, §2.4]. The fine-tuning used LR \(2\times10^{-5}\) and 10,000 gradient steps by default with a 2M-token batch for the 7B and 13B, and the models stayed stable up to 100,000 tokens. Their ablation on an earlier 7B: fine-tuning on 16K sequences without changing the base failed to retrieve far-away keys already at 8,000 tokens, and linear position interpolation (frequency scaling 1/4) failed to extrapolate [Code Llama, App. G.4, Tab. 18]. The paper notes that a Reddit user had proposed raising the base concurrently, and that the change is cheap: it needs no architecture change. The same lever reappears in Llama 3, which sets a base of 500,000 in the architecture itself and cites Xiong et al. for it [Xiong et al. 2023] (theory and scaling recipes: page 05, page 10).

Llama 3 and 3.1 (2024): the same block, ten times the data, 16K GPUs

Llama 3 shipped in two steps: 8B and 70B with 8K context in April 2024, then the 3.1 family of 8B, 70B and 405B with 128K context in July, together with the paper "The Llama 3 Herd of Models" [Llama Team 2024]. The paper organizes its choices around "three key levers": data, scale, and managing complexity. The third is the one that explains the design: "we opt for a standard dense Transformer model architecture with minor adaptations, rather than for a mixture-of-experts model to maximize training stability", and "a relatively simple post-training procedure based on supervised finetuning, rejection sampling, and direct preference optimization as opposed to more complex reinforcement learning algorithms that tend to be less stable and harder to scale" [Llama 3, §1]. Llama 3 is the "reduce the risk" generation.

Architecture: four small changes to Llama 2

The paper says the architecture "does not deviate significantly from Llama and Llama 2", and that the performance gains "are primarily driven by improvements in data quality and diversity as well as by increased training scale" [Llama 3, §3.2]. The four modifications and why:

  1. GQA with 8 KV heads at every size, "to improve inference speed and to reduce the size of key-value caches during decoding". For the 8B this cuts the cache from 512 KiB per token (Llama 2 7B, MHA) to 128 KiB, a factor 4 (page 07). It also makes context-parallel all-gathers cheap, because K and V are small (below).
  2. A document mask that stops attention across document boundaries within a packed sequence. It had limited impact in standard pre-training but is "important in continued pre-training on very long sequences", where a 128K-token sequence packs many unrelated documents.
  3. A 128K-token vocabulary (decision card below).
  4. RoPE base 500,000, up from 10,000, "to better support longer contexts"; Xiong et al. showed it effective up to 32,768 (page 05, page 10).

The FFN width rule, and where the extra parameters went

Llama 1 used \(\tfrac83 d\). Llama 3 does not: \(\dff/d\) is 3.5 for the 8B and 70B. The width comes from the rule in Meta's reference code, which the drill asks you to write (page 04 derives it):

def ffn_hidden_dim(d, multiple_of, ffn_dim_multiplier=None):   # llama/model.py, FeedForward
    h = int(2 * (4 * d) / 3)                       # 2/3 of 4d, truncated
    if ffn_dim_multiplier is not None:
        h = int(ffn_dim_multiplier * h)            # truncated again
    return multiple_of * ((h + multiple_of - 1) // multiple_of)   # round UP to a multiple

A 128K-token vocabulary
Problem
Llama 2's 32K SentencePiece vocabulary encodes English at 3.17 characters per token and other languages worse. Every token costs the same FLOPs and KV-cache bytes, so a poor tokenizer wastes compute and context.
Idea
A vocabulary of 128K tokens: 100K from the tiktoken BPE tokenizer plus 28K added for non-English languages [Llama 3, §3.2].
Why it works
English compression goes from 3.17 to 3.94 characters per token. The same text is \(3.17/3.94 = 80.5\%\) as many tokens, or equivalently the model "reads" 24% more text for the same training FLOPs and holds 24% more text in the same context window. The 28K added tokens improved both compression and downstream performance "with no impact on English tokenization".
Evidence
The compression numbers above. The paper reports no isolated ablation of the vocabulary's effect on quality.
Cost
Embedding and output head grow: \(2 \times 128{,}256 \times 4096 = 1{,}050{,}673{,}152\) parameters in the 8B, 13.1% of the model, against 3.9% for Llama 2 7B. Rare tokens see fewer updates, and the output softmax and logits tensor are 4 times wider.
Used by
Llama 3 and later Llama models; several other families use even larger vocabularies for the same reason. Trade-offs: page 02.

Llama 3 in practice: scaling laws, 16K GPUs, 128K context and post-training

Scaling laws, and small models trained far past optimal

The flagship's size came from a scaling-law fit (method and caveats: page 13). Llama 3 trained models from 40M to 16B parameters at compute budgets from \(6\times10^{18}\) to \(10^{22}\) FLOPs, fitted a parabola to each IsoFLOP curve, and fitted a power law \(N^\star(C) = A\,C^\alpha\) with \((\alpha, A) = (0.53, 0.29)\) through the minima. Extrapolated to \(3.8\times10^{25}\) FLOPs it suggested 402B parameters on 16.55T tokens. Because the curves "become flatter around the minimum as the compute budget increases", the team chose 405B [Llama 3, §3.2.1]. The second use of the same runs was forecasting: a linear fit of downstream negative log-likelihood against FLOPs (models up to \(10^{22}\)), then a sigmoid from log-likelihood to accuracy, using both the scaling-law models and the older Llama 2 models to cover the accuracy range. On ARC Challenge this extrapolated four orders of magnitude and only slightly underestimated the final 405B result. The same small-model runs chose the data mix (below).

The flagship sits near the Chinchilla ratio: \(15.6\text{T} / 405.85\text{B} = 38\) tokens per parameter. The smaller models do not. The paper says it trains "smaller models for much longer than is compute-optimal", that these "perform better than compute-optimal models at the same inference budget", and that the flagship then improves them "during post-training" [Llama 3, §1]. The launch post adds the observation: the Chinchilla-optimal compute for an 8B model corresponds to about 200B tokens, yet "both our 8B and 70B parameter models continued to improve log-linearly after we trained them on up to 15T tokens", and larger models "can match the performance of these smaller models with less training compute, but smaller models are generally preferred because they are much more efficient during inference" [Meta 2024]. That is Llama 1's argument, pushed 12 times further.

The three numbers the brief asks about, with the arithmetic: Llama 1 7B, \(1.0\text{T}/6.74\text{B} = 148\). Llama 2 70B, \(2.0\text{T}/68.98\text{B} = 29\). Llama-3-8B, \(15\text{T}/8.03\text{B} = 1{,}868\), which is 93 times Chinchilla's 20. The ratio for the smallest model of each family went 148, 297 (Llama 2 7B), 1,868: the target moved from "beat GPT-3 at 13B" to "the best model you can run yourself". When does this pay? Training an over-trained small model costs more compute than a compute-optimal model of the same quality, and saves inference FLOPs on every request; the break-even request volume is worked out on page 13. It stops paying when data runs out (repeated epochs give diminishing returns) or when the deployment is low-volume.

Data and the training recipe

The corpus is "about 15T multilingual tokens", against "1.8T tokens for Llama 2" in the Llama 3 paper's comparison (the Llama 2 paper itself says 2T, so quote the source you cite). The launch post adds that the dataset is seven times larger than Llama 2's, with four times more code, and over 5% non-English data covering more than 30 languages. Meta reports no source-level breakdown like Llama 1's. What the paper does give:

  • The final mix is roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% mathematical and reasoning, 17% code and 8% multilingual tokens, chosen with a knowledge classifier (to downsample over-represented topics such as arts and entertainment) and with scaling-law experiments: train several small models on a candidate mix, predict a large model's benchmark results on it, and repeat [Llama 3, §3.1.2].
  • The mix changed during training: more non-English data, upsampled math, more recent web data late in training, and downsampling of subsets later found to be low quality [Llama 3, §3.4.1].
  • Annealing: over the final 40M tokens the learning rate goes linearly to 0 at 128K context, the mix upsamples very high-quality code and math, and the checkpoints are averaged (Polyak averaging). On Llama 3 8B this lifted GSM8K and MATH validation scores by 24.0% and 6.4%, but for the 405B the gains were "negligible" [Llama 3, §3.1.3]. Note that the 8B experiment used the GSM8K and MATH training sets, so it partly measures format learning (page 12).

The 405B optimizer recipe is AdamW with peak learning rate \(8\times10^{-5}\), 8,000 warmup steps, and cosine decay to \(8\times10^{-7}\) (1% of peak) over 1,200,000 steps. Infrastructure: 4D parallelism and the failure arithmetic

Llama 3 405B trained on up to 16K H100 GPUs (80 GB HBM3, 700 W each) in Meta's Grand Teton servers, eight GPUs per server on NVLink, over RoCE at 400 Gbps between GPUs, out of a 24K-GPU cluster whose top network layer is oversubscribed 1:7 (so the job scheduler and the parallelism layout are topology-aware) [Llama 3, §3.3.1].   • Why TP=8 innermost: tensor parallelism needs the most bandwidth and lowest latency, so it stays inside one NVLink server. DP (implemented as FSDP) is outermost because it can prefetch weights and overlap gradient reduction over a slow network. Llama 3's FSDP shards optimizer states and gradients but does not reshard the weights after the forward pass, trading memory for one fewer all-gather in backward.
  • Pipeline changes: the number of consecutive micro-batches \(N\) is tunable (standard schedules force the batch per DP group to be divisible by the number of stages, which fought the batch-size ramp); the first and last stages hold one fewer transformer layer each, to balance the embedding and the loss; and with tensors freed eagerly, 8K-token training needs no activation checkpointing. The bubble ratio is \((\text{PP}-1)/(V \cdot M)\) for \(V\) interleaved stages per rank and \(M\) micro-batches.
  • Context parallelism for the 128K stage is all-gather based, not ring-based: gather K and V, then attend with the local Q chunk. The exposed all-gather is cheap because GQA makes K and V small (8 KV heads), attention costs \(O(S^2)\) against \(O(S)\) for the gather, and any mask, such as the document mask, is trivial to apply.
  • Numerics: FP32 gradient accumulation across micro-batches and FP32 reduce-scatter across data-parallel ranks, added after comparing losses across parallelism setups.
  • Why MFU fell from 43% to 41%: the paper attributes it to the lower batch per DP group (32 to 16 sequences) needed to keep 16M tokens per batch when the data-parallel size doubles.

Now the numbers that give the reliability lesson. During a 54-day snapshot of pre-training the run had 466 job interruptions: 47 planned (firmware upgrades, configuration or dataset updates) and 419 unexpected. About 78% of the unexpected ones were attributed to confirmed or suspected hardware issues, GPU problems alone were 58.7%, and manual intervention was needed only three times. Effective training time stayed above 90% [Llama 3, §3.3.4, Tab. 5]. The arithmetic an interviewer wants:

  • Rate: \(419 / 54 = 7.8\) unexpected interruptions per day, one every \(54 \cdot 24 / 419 = 3.1\) hours (one every 2.8 hours counting planned ones).
  • Per-GPU reliability is high but the cluster is not: \(419 / (54 \cdot 24 \cdot 16{,}384) = 1.97\times10^{-5}\) per GPU-hour, a mean time between failures of about 50,700 GPU-hours, or 5.8 years per GPU. Failure rate scales linearly with the number of GPUs in a synchronous job (a single failure restarts the whole job), so at the same per-GPU rate and independent failures, 100K GPUs would see an interruption every 30 minutes. That is an extrapolation, not a measurement.
  • What "above 90%" implies: at most 10% of \(54 \cdot 1440 = 77{,}760\) minutes, about 7,776 minutes, was lost across 466 interruptions, so the average interruption cost under \(7776/466 \approx 17\) minutes end to end (detection, restart, rollback to the last checkpoint). This is why the paper stresses fast diagnosis, cheap checkpoints and automation over preventing failures.

The paper says "the complexity and potential failure scenarios of 16K GPU training surpass those of much larger CPU clusters that we have operated", and lists the tools that made it tractable: reduced job startup and checkpoint time, PyTorch's NCCL flight recorder (a ring buffer of collective metadata and stack traces dumped on a timeout) for hang diagnosis, straggler detection (one slow GPU slows thousands), and a diurnal 1 to 2% throughput swing from mid-day temperature effects on GPU clocks. Simultaneous power changes across tens of thousands of GPUs, for example at a checkpoint or restart, swing data-center load by "tens of megawatts". Also, the launch post reported effective training time above 95% and about three times Llama 2's training efficiency [Meta 2024]; the paper's figure for the 405B run is above 90%.

Pitfall: Table 5 does not add up

The root-cause table lists "Faulty GPU" as 148 interruptions, 30.1%. But \(148/419 = 35.3\%\), while \(126/419 = 30.1\%\), and the text's 58.7% for all GPU causes matches 126 (\(126 + 72 + 19 + 17 + 6 + 6 = 246 = 58.7\%\) of 419), not 148 (which would give 64%). The counts as printed do sum to 419, so one of the printed numbers in that row is a typo, and we cannot tell which. Quote the percentages from the text, say "roughly 60%", and never present a table cell as ground truth without a sanity check. The same paper says the vocabulary is 128,000 in Table 3 while the checkpoints have 128,256 rows.

From your jet tagger

A tagger trains for hours on a handful of GPUs, so a crash is an anecdote and you restart from the last epoch. A synchronous run on 16K GPUs for months turns failure into a rate: with one interruption every three hours, the checkpoint interval, restart time and job scheduler are as much a part of the model recipe as the learning rate. The reading habit that transfers is the same one you use for detector performance: ask for the rate and the recovery time, not for the anecdote.

Long context: six stages at the very end

Llama 3 pretrains at 8K and extends to 128K only "in the final stages of pre-training", not earlier "because the compute in self-attention layers grows quadratically in the sequence length". The context grows in six stages, from 8K to 128K, on about 800B tokens (5% of the 15.6T), and each stage runs "until the model has successfully adapted to the increased context length", judged by two tests: short-context evaluations recovered completely, and "needle in a haystack" solved perfectly up to that length [Llama 3, §3.4.2]. Four ingredients are visible in the released material: the 500,000 base in the architecture, the document mask, 16-way context parallelism in the training layout above, and the Llama 3.1 frequency rescaling. The checkpoints' rope_scaling entry has factor 8, low-frequency factor 1, high-frequency factor 4 and original length 8,192 (page 10 derives the rule and notes that Meta does not document whether training used the rescaled frequencies).

"Supports 128K" and "uses 128K" differ. On NoLiMa, which removes literal matches between question and needle, the effective length (score above 85% of the model's short-context base) is 2K tokens for Llama 3.1 405B and 70B and 1K for the 8B, against a claimed 128K [Modarressi et al. 2025, Tab. 3]. The paper's own long-context evaluations (needle retrieval, QuALITY, InfiniteBench) are easier, and page 10 covers why.

Post-training: rejection sampling, SFT and DPO, six rounds

The pipeline, per round, is [Llama 3, §4]:

  1. Preference data. Annotators compare two responses from two different recent models and rate the margin on four levels (significantly better, better, slightly better, marginally better), then edit the winner, giving edited \(\succ\) chosen \(\succ\) rejected. Only "significantly better" and "better" pairs are used.
  2. Reward model. One RM on the pretrained checkpoint, trained on all preference data so far. Compared with Llama 2 the margin term is removed "as we observe diminishing improvements after data scaling", and there is one RM instead of two.
  3. Rejection sampling. For each prompt, sample \(K\) (typically 10 to 30) answers from the latest policy, keep the RM's favorite. PagedAttention and prompt-KV sharing gave over 2 times throughput.
  4. SFT on the rejection-sampled data plus synthetic and human data, LR \(10^{-5}\), 8.5K to 9K steps, loss on target tokens only.
  5. DPO on the most recent preference batches, LR \(10^{-5}\), \(\beta = 0.1\), with two changes: mask the header and termination tokens out of the loss (they appear in both chosen and rejected responses and cause "tail repetition or abruptly generating termination tokens"), and add an NLL term with coefficient 0.2 on the chosen sequence to keep its likelihood from falling.
  6. Model averaging across runs with different data or hyperparameters at each of RM, SFT and DPO. Repeat six times.
DPO instead of PPO in Llama 3
Problem
Llama 2's RS+PPO pipeline worked at 70B, but PPO puts generation inside the training loop and needs an actor, critic, reference and reward model (page 23). Llama 2 measured 330 seconds per 70B iteration and a roughly 20 times generation slowdown under FSDP. At 405B, with a synchronous 16K-GPU job, that is a large and fragile system.
Idea
Move the reward maximization into rejection sampling plus SFT (offline best-of-\(K\)), and do preference learning with DPO, a supervised loss on stored pairs, on data from the previous round's best models.
Why it works
Each round's DPO pairs come from a policy close to the one being trained, so the offline loss is nearly on-policy. DPO needs forward passes only, with a frozen reference whose log-probabilities can be cached, and no critic (page 21). Six rounds restore some of the iteration that online RL provides.
Evidence
One sentence, no numbers: they "explored on-policy algorithms such as PPO, but found that DPO required less compute for large-scale models and performed better, especially on instruction following benchmarks like IFEval" [Llama 3, §4.1.4]. External evidence is mixed: Tulu 3 found PPO comparable and much slower, while Xu et al. found PPO ahead of DPO on code (page 21).
Cost
Offline pairs limit exploration, and DPO's known failure modes had to be patched (the formatting-token mask and NLL term). It is weaker where the reward is verifiable and exploration matters: reasoning, code, math.
Used by
Llama 3.x, Zephyr-style open recipes, Tulu 3. Llama 4 keeps only a "lightweight" DPO at the end (below).

Two caveats matter for an interview. First, the choice is a compute and stability argument backed by one sentence, not an ablation table. Second, Meta's own accounts differ: the April 2024 launch post for the 8B and 70B says post-training is "a combination of supervised fine-tuning (SFT), rejection sampling, proximal policy optimization (PPO), and direct preference optimization (DPO)" [Meta 2024], while the July paper describes SFT, RS and DPO only. We cannot say whether the April models used PPO.

The rest of the Llama 3 family

  • Llama 3.2 (September 2024) added 11B and 90B vision models and the 1B and 3B text models, made by single-shot structured pruning of Llama 3.1 8B, then pretraining with the logits of the 3.1 8B and 70B models as token-level distillation targets, then the same SFT, RS and DPO rounds, with 128K context [Meta 2024b]. Both tie the embedding and output head: 1,235,814,400 and 3,212,749,824 parameters, which the drill checks (distillation: page 19).
  • Llama 3.3 (6 December 2024) is a 70B instruct model only. Its model card lists 7.0M GPU-hours, the same as Llama 3.1 70B, and over 25M synthetic fine-tuning examples [Llama 3.3 model card], which suggests that the improvement came from post-training (our inference).

Mixture of experts: what is active, and why

Llama 4's MoE: one routed expert plus a shared expert
Problem
Serving cost per token scales with the active parameters. Llama 3 405B spends about 0.8 TFLOPs per token, and quality per dollar of inference was the axis on which Llama 1 had competed from the start.
Idea
MoE layers with 128 routed experts (Maverick) or 16 (Scout) and one shared expert, each token going to the shared expert and to one routed expert. Maverick alternates dense and MoE layers (stated in the post [Meta 2025]); Scout's config makes every layer MoE. Mechanics: page 08.
Why it works
Meta's stated reason: "MoE architectures are more compute efficient for training and inference and, given a fixed training FLOPs budget, delivers higher quality compared to a dense model", and the alternation is "for inference efficiency". A useful fact from the config: a routed expert and the shared expert are each 8,192 wide, so a token in an MoE layer uses \(2 \times 8192 = 16{,}384\), the same as the dense layers. Active compute per layer is therefore uniform, which is why both models have 17B active parameters whatever their expert count.
Evidence
Vendor benchmarks only: Meta says Maverick achieves "comparable results to the new DeepSeek v3 on reasoning and coding" at "less than half the active parameters" (17B against 37B). The post reports no MoE-versus-dense ablation.
Cost
All parameters must be stored: 400B parameters are 401 GB in FP8, which is why the post says Maverick "can be run on a single NVIDIA H100 DGX host" (8 × 80 GB = 640 GB), while a bf16 Llama 3 405B is 812 GB. Expert-parallel communication, load balancing and harder fine-tuning follow (page 08).
Used by
Llama 4; the same shared-plus-routed design as DeepSeek-V2/V3 and Kimi K2 (page 26, page 08).

Why might Meta have moved to MoE? The post gives one reason, the compute-efficiency claim above. The rest is our reading. (1) Cost per token is the thread from Llama 1: active parameters set FLOPs, and Maverick's are about 25 times fewer per token than the 405B (\(2 \times 16.15\text{B}\) against \(2 \times 403.75\text{B}\) matmul parameters, embeddings excluded). (2) At equal training FLOPs, MoE scaling studies favor sparse models (page 13). (3) Llama 3's argument against MoE was training stability at 405B; by 2025 sparse runs at hundreds of billions of parameters were established elsewhere (DeepSeek-V3 has 671B total and 37B active, page 08), so the risk had shrunk and the competitive comparison had become active parameters. (4) The single-host targets (Scout on one H100 in Int4, Maverick on one 8-GPU host) are sizes chosen for serving.

iRoPE: interleaved RoPE and position-free layers

Meta's post describes the idea in three sentences: "A key innovation in the Llama 4 architecture is the use of interleaved attention layers without positional embeddings. Additionally, we employ inference time temperature scaling of attention to enhance length generalization. We call this the iRoPE architecture, where 'i' stands for 'interleaved' attention layers, highlighting the long-term goal of supporting 'infinite' context length, and 'RoPE' refers to the rotary position embeddings employed in most layers." [Meta 2025]. The mechanism is in the released config and the Hugging Face implementation:

  1. Layer pattern. In every group of four layers, three use RoPE (\(\theta = 500{,}000\)) and attend only within fixed chunks of 8,192 tokens (attention_chunk_size), and the fourth has no positional encoding at all (NoPE) and attends over the whole context. That is 12 global layers out of 48 in both models.
  2. Temperature scaling, on the NoPE layers only. The query at position \(p\) is multiplied by \(s(p) = 1 + 0.1 \ln\!\big(\lfloor (p+1)/8192 \rfloor + 1\big)\) (config values attn_scale 0.1 and floor_scale 8192). It is exactly 1 below 8,191 and grows only logarithmically: 1.28 at 131,072, 1.49 at 1M, 1.72 at 10.5M. Multiplying \(q\) by \(s\) multiplies all logits by \(s\), a sharper softmax that counters the dilution of attention over more keys. The Hugging Face code cites the Scalable-Softmax paper for it [Nakanishi 2025].
  3. QK-norm on the RoPE layers of Scout (a parameter-free L2 normalization; the Scout config sets it, the Maverick config does not).
iRoPE
Problem
Three linked ones. RoPE generalizes poorly to relative distances never seen in training, and patching it with position interpolation or YaRN is a heuristic (page 10). Softmax attention over a growing context dilutes: with more keys, weights flatten. And a KV cache that grows linearly in every layer makes very long contexts unaffordable.
Idea
Split the depth. Most layers see a bounded window with RoPE, so their positions stay in the trained range at any total length. A minority of layers see everything and carry no positional code, with a length-dependent temperature.
Why it works
The local RoPE layers never meet an out-of-range relative distance (at most 8,191), so there is nothing to extrapolate. The global layers have no rotation phases to extrapolate either, and a causal decoder can infer order from the mask itself (NoPE: page 05). The logarithmic temperature offsets the growth of attention entropy with length. And only 12 of 48 layers grow a cache with \(T\).
Evidence
Meta reports needle-in-a-haystack retrieval and cumulative negative log-likelihood over 10M tokens of code for Scout, which was "pre-trained and post-trained with a 256K context length", so 10M is 40 times the trained length. No ablation is published.
Cost
Information must cross chunk boundaries through the 12 global layers alone. NoPE layers do not encode order explicitly, which may hurt tasks that need precise positions. And a long window is not a long effective context, as the tests below show.
Used by
Llama 4. The same shape (RoPE on local layers, none on global layers) appears in the dense Muse Glimmer 30B (below), and hybrids like it are the subject of page 09.

From your jet tagger

Your tagger had no positional encoding because a jet is a set: permutation invariance was the design goal. A causal language model is the opposite case, and yet the "position-free" (NoPE) layers in Llama 4 work anyway: the causal mask alone breaks the symmetry: each token sees a different number of predecessors, and attention can recover position from that. The layers are not invariant to order, they simply are not told the order explicitly. The 3-to-1 interleaving is a bet that a few such layers can do long-range retrieval while the RoPE layers handle local order.

Llama 4: training recipe, reception, and what came after

Training: early fusion, MetaP, FP8, distillation, online RL

Everything in this list is a claim in Meta's launch post [Meta 2025]; the post gives few numbers and no ablations.

  • Early fusion 早期融合: text and vision tokens go into one backbone from the start, so the model is pre-trained jointly on unlabeled text, image and video data (up to 48 images in pre-training, tested to eight in post-training). The vision encoder is based on MetaCLIP but "trained separately in conjunction with a frozen Llama model". The alternative, used for Llama 3's vision models, is to attach an adapter to a finished text model [Llama 3, §7].
  • MetaP: a technique "that allows us to reliably set critical model hyper-parameters such as per-layer learning rates and initialization scales", with hyperparameters that "transfer well across different values of batch size, model width, depth, and training tokens". Nothing else is disclosed. The goal is the same as \(\mu\)P (page 14): tune small, transfer to large.
  • FP8 and scale: pre-training Behemoth "using FP8 and 32K GPUs, we achieved 390 TFLOPs/GPU". The post does not say which peak that is measured against: it is 39% of the H100's dense bf16 peak (989) or 20% of the dense FP8 peak (1,979; page 04), so do not read it as an MFU without asking.
  • Data: 200 languages (over 100 with more than 1B tokens each), 10 times more multilingual tokens than Llama 3, and a mid-training stage with "long context extension using specialized datasets". Per token counted by the model cards, Scout saw about \(40\text{T}/17.17\text{B} = 2{,}330\) tokens per active parameter and Maverick \(22\text{T}/17.18\text{B} = 1{,}280\) (Figure 2).
  • Codistillation from Behemoth: Maverick was codistilled from the 288B-active teacher during pre-training with "a novel distillation loss function that dynamically weights the soft and hard targets through training". Doing it during pre-training "amortizes the computational cost of resource-intensive forward passes" over most of the student's data, and extra forward passes were run for the new data (distillation basics: page 19).
  • Post-training reversed Llama 3's recipe: lightweight SFT, then online reinforcement learning, then lightweight DPO. The stated reason: "SFT and DPO can over-constrain the model, restricting exploration during the online RL stage and leading to suboptimal accuracy, particularly in reasoning, coding, and math domains". They dropped more than 50% of the SFT data (tagged easy by Llama judges) for Maverick and 95% for Behemoth, and ran continuous online RL that alternated training with filtering to keep only medium-to-hard prompts. For Behemoth they built a fully asynchronous RL framework that allocates models to separate GPUs, reporting about 10 times better training efficiency than previous generations (page 24, page 25).

Read against Llama 3: a year earlier the paper preferred DPO because RL was "less stable and harder to scale"; by April 2025 Meta's own finding was that the offline stages capped reasoning gains. The direction of change, toward online RL on hard verifiable prompts, is the field's, not only Meta's.

Which choices other labs adopted

  • The block. Pre-norm RMSNorm, gated FFN, RoPE and no biases became the default of open dense models, with variants (Qwen, Mistral, DeepSeek LLM, OLMo 2; pages 04, 05, and DeepSeek LLM's "LLaMA-shaped" 67B on page 26).
  • GQA with 8 KV heads is the default for dense models since 2023 (page 07).
  • Staged, late long-context training with a raised RoPE base, then frequency rescaling, is the common recipe (page 10).
  • Post-training in stages: reward-model-filtered SFT followed by DPO became a standard open recipe (Llama 3, Tulu 3; page 21); the field has since moved toward online RL, as Llama 4 did (page 24).
  • Shared plus routed experts in a sparse model: Llama 4 took this design from the DeepSeekMoE line (page 08, page 26), not the other way round.

Which they did not. A 405B dense flagship found few followers: the large open models that followed are sparse (DeepSeek-V3 with 671B total and 37B active, Kimi K2 with 1.04T and 32.6B, Qwen3-235B-A22B; page 08). GQA stayed the default answer to the KV cache in dense models, while DeepSeek's MLA reached a smaller cache (page 07).

What Llama contributed. Almost every ingredient was borrowed: pre-norm from GPT-3, SwiGLU from PaLM, RoPE from Su et al. via GPT-Neo, GQA from Ainslie et al., the RoPE-base change from Code Llama and Xiong et al., sparse experts from the GShard, Mixtral and DeepSeek line. What Llama added was integration at scale, released openly, and documented: the tokens-per-parameter bet (Llama 1), the first open end-to-end RLHF account (Llama 2), and the most complete public account of a frontier training run, with failure statistics (Llama 3). For interviews that is the frame: for each design choice, name where it came from, what problem it solved, and what evidence Meta gave for it. On the architecture, the answer is usually "borrowed, with no ablation"; on the systems and data, "measured, at scale".

Common misconceptions

  • "Llama 3 was a big architectural step." The paper says the opposite: the model "does not deviate significantly" from Llama 2, and the gains "are primarily driven by" data quality and scale. The architecture changes are four small ones (GQA-8 everywhere, 128K vocabulary, RoPE base 500,000, document mask).
  • "Llama followed the Chinchilla recipe." Llama 1 was written as a rebuttal of its objective: the 7B saw 148 tokens per parameter and the Llama 3 8B saw about 1,870, against Chinchilla's 20. Only the largest model of each early generation sits near 20.
  • "17B active means it costs like a 17B dense model to host." FLOPs per token do, but memory does not: all 400B parameters must be resident (401 GB in FP8). The "17B" also includes 2.07B of embedding and head parameters.
  • "Tokens per parameter for an MoE is tokens over total parameters." Use activated parameters, as Figure 2 does, or the ratio understates the training by an order of magnitude or more.
  • "A 128K (or 10M) window means the model works at that length." The window is what the architecture accepts. On NoLiMa the effective length of Llama 3.1 405B is 2K, of Scout 1K, of Maverick 2K.
  • "iRoPE is a new RoPE scaling rule." It is a layer-pattern architecture: RoPE with chunked local attention in three of four layers, no positional encoding with global, temperature-scaled attention in the fourth. The scaling of position frequencies (page 10) is a different mechanism.
  • "Llama 3 used PPO like Llama 2." Llama 2 used rejection sampling and then PPO; the Llama 3 paper describes rejection sampling, SFT and DPO, and says it tried PPO and preferred DPO. (Meta's April 2024 launch post still lists PPO for the 8B and 70B.)
  • "Numbers in a big technical report are internally consistent." The Llama 3 paper's Table 5 has a cell that contradicts its own percentage, its vocabulary is 128,000 in one place and 128,256 in the checkpoints, and it says 1.8T tokens for Llama 2 where the Llama 2 paper says 2T. Sanity-check every number you plan to quote.

Interview questions

Warm-up What did Llama 1 change relative to the original transformer and GPT-3, and why?

Three architectural changes, each borrowed, and one change of training regime. Pre-normalization with RMSNorm: normalize the input of each sublayer, not the output, for stability (GPT-3 already used pre-norm; RMSNorm drops LayerNorm's mean subtraction and bias to be cheaper). SwiGLU instead of ReLU or GELU, with hidden width \(\tfrac23 \cdot 4d\) so that three matrices (\(3 \cdot d \cdot \tfrac83 d = 8d^2\)) cost the same as the two-matrix \(4d\) FFN (\(2 \cdot d \cdot 4d = 8d^2\)). RoPE at every layer in place of learned absolute position embeddings, so attention depends on relative offset. Also no biases, and dense causal attention throughout, with no GPT-3-style banded sparse layers.

The bigger change was the regime: 1.0T to 1.4T tokens on public data only, so the 7B trained on 148 tokens per parameter, against GPT-3's 300B tokens for 175B parameters. The motivation is inference cost: Chinchilla-optimal training minimizes training compute, not serving compute, so "a smaller one trained longer will ultimately be cheaper at inference". The paper gives no ablations for the architecture changes: it credits GPT-3, PaLM and GPT-Neo.

Warm-up Compute tokens per parameter for Llama 1 7B, Llama 2 70B and Llama 3 8B. What is the trend and why?

Llama 1 7B: \(1.0\text{T} / 6.74\text{B} = 148\). Llama 2 70B: \(2.0\text{T} / 68.98\text{B} = 29\). Llama-3-8B: \(\approx 15\text{T} / 8.03\text{B} = 1{,}868\). Chinchilla's optimum is about 20, so these are 7.4, 1.4 and 93 times it. The smallest model of each family went 148, 297 (Llama 2 7B, 2T), 1,868: each generation pushed its small models further past the compute-optimal point. The flagships stayed near it (65B: 21, 70B: 29, 405B: 38).

Why: the quantity being optimized is total cost including serving. A small model trained on a lot of data is worse per training FLOP but cheaper on every request, and Llama's target shifted from "beat GPT-3" to "the best model people can run themselves". Llama 3 also reports that its 8B and 70B kept improving log-linearly up to 15T tokens. The limit is data supply and diminishing returns per token.

Warm-up Derive Llama-3-8B's FFN width of 14,336 from \(d = 4096\). Why is it not \(\tfrac83 d\)?

Meta's reference code: \(h = 4d = 16384\); \(h \leftarrow \lfloor 2h/3 \rfloor = 10922\); multiply by the config's ffn_dim_multiplier 1.3 and truncate, \(\lfloor 14198.6 \rfloor = 14198\); round up to a multiple of multiple_of = 1024, which is \(14 \times 1024 = 14336\). The 70B (multiplier 1.3, multiple of 4096) gives 28,672 and the 405B (1.2, 4096) gives 53,248.

\(\tfrac83 d \approx 10{,}923\) would have been rounded up to 11,008 with Llama 1's multiple of 256. Llama 3's \(\dff/d = 3.5\) is 30% wider. The paper does not explain it, but the accounting shows the trade: GQA-8 freed 805M attention parameters, and 1.31B were spent on the wider FFN (with 0.79B on the larger vocabulary, that is exactly the 6.74B to 8.03B step from Llama 2 7B). The round multiples align with tensor-core tiles and the 8-way TP split (14,336/8 = 1,792 columns per GPU).

Core Why did Llama 3 use DPO rather than PPO in post-training?

What the paper says: it "explored on-policy algorithms such as PPO, but found that DPO required less compute for large-scale models and performed better, especially on instruction following benchmarks like IFEval", and it wanted "a relatively simple post-training procedure" instead of RL that "tends to be less stable and harder to scale". No numbers are given.

The mechanism behind it: PPO needs sampling inside the training loop and four models (actor, critic, reference, reward). Llama 2 measured 330 seconds per 70B PPO iteration and a roughly 20 times generation slowdown under FSDP, and at 405B on a synchronous 16K-GPU job that is a large source of complexity. DPO is a supervised loss on stored pairs with a frozen reference. Llama 3 keeps it nearly on-policy by training on the latest batches of preference data collected with the previous round's best models, six rounds in all, while rejection sampling (best-of-\(K\), \(K\) = 10 to 30, then SFT) does the reward-maximizing part offline. They also patch DPO's known problems: header and termination tokens are masked out of the loss and an NLL term (coefficient 0.2) on the chosen answer stops its likelihood falling. Caveats: external comparisons are mixed (Tulu 3: PPO comparable, slower; Xu et al.: PPO ahead on code), and Llama 4 later reversed course, arguing that SFT and DPO "can over-constrain the model, restricting exploration during the online RL stage".

Core What does the Llama 3 paper teach about infrastructure reliability at 16K GPUs?

In a 54-day window there were 466 interruptions: 47 planned and 419 unexpected. About 78% of the unexpected ones were hardware (confirmed or suspected), GPU issues alone were 58.7%, and only three needed manual intervention. That is 7.8 unexpected interruptions a day, one every 3.1 hours. Per GPU that is \(419/(54 \cdot 24 \cdot 16384) = 1.97\times10^{-5}\) per hour, an MTBF of about 50,700 GPU-hours (5.8 years), so each GPU is reliable and the job is not: in synchronous training the failure rate scales with the number of GPUs, and at the same rate 100K GPUs would see one every 30 minutes (extrapolation).

Effective training time stayed above 90%, which bounds the average cost per interruption (detection, restart, rollback) to about \(0.1 \cdot 77{,}760/466 \approx 17\) minutes. So the levers are: fast checkpointing and restart, automated diagnosis (NCCL flight recorder, straggler detection), scheduler automation, and a design that avoids extra risk, which is why the paper chose a dense model and simple post-training ("managing complexity"). Also relevant: a 1 to 2% diurnal throughput swing from temperature, power swings of tens of megawatts, and that Table 5's "Faulty GPU" row (148, 30.1%) is internally inconsistent (148/419 is 35.3%), so cross-check numbers before quoting them.

Core Compute Llama 3 405B's parameter count from its config and check \(6ND\) against the paper's 3.8e25 FLOPs.

Config: \(d = 16384\), \(L = 126\), \(n_h = 128\), \(n_{kv} = 8\), \(d_h = 128\), \(\dff = 53248\), \(V = 128256\), untied, no biases. Per layer: attention \(2 \cdot 16384^2 + 2 \cdot 16384 \cdot 1024 = 570{,}425{,}344\); FFN \(3 \cdot 16384 \cdot 53248 = 2{,}617{,}245{,}696\); norms \(32{,}768\); total 3,187,703,808. Times 126 is 401,650,679,808. Add embedding and head \(2 \cdot 128256 \cdot 16384 = 4{,}202{,}692{,}608\) and the final norm 16,384: 405,853,388,800. The FFN holds 81% of the parameters.

Compute: \(6 \cdot 4.0585\times10^{11} \cdot 1.56\times10^{13} = 3.80\times10^{25}\), matching the paper's 3.8e25, so it uses the \(6ND\) convention. Cross-check with hardware: the model card's 30.84M H100-hours give \(3.8\times10^{25}/(30.84\times10^6 \cdot 3600) = 342\) TFLOP/s, 34.6% of the 989 TFLOP/s bf16 peak, against a 38 to 43% MFU during training and above 90% effective time (\(0.41 \times 0.9 = 37\%\)): plausible, since GPU-hours include restarts and \(6ND\) ignores attention. The paper's scaling-law fit suggested 402B on 16.55T tokens, whose \(6ND\) is 3.99e25 (5% higher), and it chose 405B because the IsoFLOP curve is flat near the optimum.

Core Why did Llama 3 move to a 128K vocabulary, and what does it cost?

Tokenizer efficiency. The new vocabulary is 100K tiktoken BPE tokens plus 28K for non-English languages, and it raises English compression from 3.17 to 3.94 characters per token. The same text becomes \(3.17/3.94 = 80.5\%\) as many tokens: 24% more text per training FLOP, per KV-cache byte and per context window, and cheaper generation per character. The added non-English tokens improved compression and downstream performance with no impact on English tokenization.

Costs: embedding and head are \(2 \cdot 128256 \cdot 4096 = 1.05\text{B}\) parameters in the 8B (13.1% of the model, against 3.9% for Llama 2 7B, an extra 0.79B parameters); the logits tensor and softmax are 4 times wider; rare tokens get fewer gradient updates. The paper reports no isolated quality ablation.

Core Design question: you need a model of a given quality to serve about a trillion tokens. How do you decide between a compute-optimal large model and an over-trained small one?

Minimize total FLOPs: training \(6ND\) plus serving \(2N\) per generated token. For two models of equal quality, \((N_1, D_1)\) compute-optimal and \((N_2, D_2)\) smaller and over-trained, the small one wins once the served tokens \(T\) exceed \(T^\star = 3\,(N_2 D_2 - N_1 D_1)/(N_1 - N_2)\). Illustration with Llama-like numbers: \(N_1 = 70\text{B}\), \(D_1 = 1.4\text{T}\), \(N_2 = 8\text{B}\), \(D_2 = 15\text{T}\) gives \(T^\star = 3 \cdot (1.2\times10^{23} - 9.8\times10^{22})/(62\times10^{9}) \approx 1.06\times10^{12}\) tokens. Above about a trillion served tokens the 8B is cheaper in total FLOPs, and below it the larger model is.

Caveats that a strong answer raises: the equal-quality assumption is the weak link (it must come from a scaling fit, and the two models above are not equal); FLOPs are not dollars (decode is memory-bound, so batch size and hardware matter, page 17); data supply caps \(D\) (repeated epochs give less); latency and on-device constraints can force the small model regardless; and a large teacher can improve the small model in post-training, as Llama 3's flagship did.

Core How did Llama 3.1 reach a 128K context, and why is it staged at the end of pre-training?

Pretraining is at 8K. In the final stage the context grows in six increments from 8K to 128K over about 800B tokens (5% of the total), each run until short-context evaluations have fully recovered and needle-in-a-haystack is solved perfectly at that length. It is late because self-attention cost grows quadratically with length, so long sequences early would waste FLOPs on a model that has not yet learned the basics. Enablers: RoPE base 500,000 from the start (Xiong et al. showed it effective to 32K), a document mask so packed unrelated documents do not attend to each other, GQA so context-parallel all-gathers of K and V are cheap (the 128K stage uses TP8, CP16, PP16, DP8 at 38% MFU), and the 3.1 llama3 frequency rescaling (factor 8 over the original 8,192 positions, page 10).

Caveat: a 128K window is not 128K of usable context. On NoLiMa the 405B and 70B have effective length 2K and the 8B 1K.

Core Describe Llama 2's RLHF recipe and justify two reward models, rejection sampling before PPO, and Ghost Attention.

Pipeline: SFT on 27,540 high-quality annotations; human preference collection in weekly batches (over 1.4M Meta comparisons, four-level ratings); two reward models, helpfulness and safety, initialized from chat checkpoints, with a ranking loss plus a preference-strength margin; then iterative RLHF versions V1 to V5, using rejection sampling only up to V4 and rejection sampling followed by PPO after that.

Why: helpfulness and safety trade off, so a single RM is confused and "optimizing two separate models eases the reward modeling task" (safety RM output takes priority below a 0.15 threshold). Rejection sampling (best of \(K\), fine-tune on it, done with the 70B and distilled down) gives breadth, exploring \(K\) samples per prompt, cheaply, and PPO gives depth, training on its own latest samples with a KL penalty (0.01 or 0.005) against reward hacking. Ghost Attention fixes multi-turn forgetting of a system instruction: prepend the instruction to every user turn when sampling, then train with it only in the first turn and loss zeroed on earlier turns. Cost: PPO iterations of about 330 s at 70B and a large FSDP generation slowdown, the seeds of Llama 3's decision to drop PPO.

Deep-dive What is iRoPE and what problem is it meant to solve?

iRoPE is Llama 4's layer pattern for long context: "interleaved" attention layers, most with RoPE and some with no positional embedding, plus inference-time temperature scaling of attention. In the released config, three of every four layers use RoPE (\(\theta\) = 500,000) with chunked local attention over 8,192-token chunks, and every fourth layer (12 of 48) is a NoPE layer with full attention. On those layers the query is scaled by \(s(p) = 1 + 0.1\ln(\lfloor (p+1)/8192 \rfloor + 1)\): 1 up to 8,190, 1.28 at 131K, 1.72 at 10.5M, sharpening the softmax as the number of keys grows.

Problems addressed: (1) RoPE extrapolation, since the local layers never see a relative distance beyond 8,191 and the global layers have no rotation phases to go out of range, so length generalization no longer depends on interpolating frequencies; (2) attention dilution over millions of keys, countered by the logarithmic temperature (in the spirit of Scalable-Softmax); (3) cache growth, since only 12 layers keep \(T\) tokens. Scout was trained at 256K and claimed to 10M, with evidence from needle retrieval and NLL over code, no ablation, and independent NoLiMa results (effective length 1K, 21.6 at 32K against a base of 81.7). It is not a new frequency-scaling rule.

Deep-dive Why might Meta have moved Llama 4 to MoE? What are the trade-offs?

Meta's stated reason is that MoE is "more compute efficient for training and inference" and, at fixed training FLOPs, gives higher quality than a dense model. The numbers support the efficiency part: Maverick activates 17.2B parameters against 405.9B, and excluding embeddings that is \(2 \times 16.15\text{B} = 32\) GFLOPs per token against \(2 \times 403.75\text{B} = 808\) GFLOPs, about 25 times fewer. The same lever Llama 1 pulled (cost at serving time) applies, with serving targets of one H100 for Scout in Int4 and one 8-GPU host for Maverick in FP8. Llama 3 had argued against MoE for training stability at 405B, but by early 2025 large sparse runs were established (DeepSeek-V3: 671B total, 37B active), and the comparison that mattered was active parameters (Meta compares Maverick with DeepSeek-V3 "at less than half the active parameters").

Trade-offs: memory is set by total parameters (401 GB in FP8, 803 GB in bf16, against 406 GB and 812 GB for a 405B dense), so MoE serving trades FLOPs for memory and interconnect; expert parallelism and load balancing add engineering; batches touch many experts so decode bandwidth is closer to the total than the active size; and it is harder to fine-tune. Our reading of why it happened, as opposed to what Meta said, is the falling risk (external proof of stability) plus the competitive pressure on active-parameter benchmarks.

Deep-dive Design question: you must serve Llama 4 Scout with 10M-token prompts. Size the problem, and say how you would validate the claim.

Memory. Per token each layer stores \(2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 4096\) B in bf16. Only 12 global layers grow with \(T\); the 36 chunked layers hold at most 8,192 tokens. At \(T = 10{,}485{,}760\): \(12 \cdot 4096 \cdot T = 480\) GiB plus \(36 \cdot 4096 \cdot 8192 = 1.1\) GiB, so 481 GiB per sequence (1,920 GiB if all layers were global). Weights are 54 GB in Int4 or 109 GB in FP8. "Fits on one H100" is the weights only: one sequence needs more than six H100s of memory for KV alone in bf16, about half of that with an FP8 cache, and batching multiplies it.

Compute. Prefill on the 12 global layers costs about \(2 T^2 d_{\text{attn}}\) FLOPs each with \(d_{\text{attn}} = 5120\): \(1.13\times10^{18}\) per layer, \(1.35\times10^{19}\) in total, against \(3.4\times10^{17}\) for all the linear layers (\(2 \cdot 16.15\text{B} \cdot T\)). Attention is 97% of prefill, so on 8 H100s at 40% MFU a single 10M-token prefill takes about 73 minutes (our estimate). Decode reads the whole cache each step: 481 GiB across 8 GPUs at 3.35 TB/s is about 19 ms per token from KV alone. Mitigations: KV quantization, prefix caching, chunked prefill, sequence or context parallelism, sparse or retrieval-based attention (pages 09, 17).

Validation. Do not rely on literal needle-in-a-haystack. Cheat sheet

  • Timeline: Llama 1 Feb 2023 (7B to 65B, 1.0T to 1.4T tokens, 2K), Llama 2 Jul 2023 (2T, 4K, GQA on 34B and 70B), Llama 3 Apr 2024 and 3.1 Jul 2024 (~15T, 8K then 128K), Llama 4 Apr 2025 (MoE). Last Llama LLMs: Llama 4 (Meta's new family is Muse, from Apr 2026).
  • Block: pre-RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, no biases, untied embeddings, unchanged for three generations. Llama 3 adds GQA-8 at all sizes, 128K vocabulary (3.17 to 3.94 chars/token), RoPE base 500,000, document mask.
  • FFN width: \(h = \lfloor 2 \cdot 4d/3 \rfloor\), then \(\lfloor \text{mult} \cdot h \rfloor\), then round up to multiple_of. 8B: 14,336 (1.3, 1024); 70B: 28,672 (1.3, 4096); 405B: 53,248 (1.2, 4096).
  • Parameters: Llama-3-8B 8,030,261,248; 70B 70,553,706,496; 405B 405,853,388,800 (\(d\) 16,384, 126 layers, 128 heads, 8 KV, FFN 53,248). Llama 2 7B 6,738,415,616; 70B 68,976,648,192.
  • Tokens per parameter: L1 7B 148, L2 70B 29, L3 8B 1,868, L3 405B 38 (Chinchilla 20). \(6ND\) for the 405B: 3.8e25 FLOPs (paper), from 15.6T tokens.
  • KV per token: Llama 2 7B (MHA) 512 KiB; Llama 2/3 70B 320 KiB; Llama 3 8B 128 KiB; 405B 504 KiB. Scout at 10M tokens: 481 GiB with iRoPE (12 global layers), 1,920 GiB all-global.
  • Llama 3 405B: 16,384 H100s, TP8 CP1 PP16 DP128 (or CP16, DP8 at 128K), 38 to 43% MFU (400 TFLOP/s per GPU), 54 days: 466 interruptions (419 unexpected, 78% hardware, 58.7% GPU), one every 3.1 h, above 90% effective time.
  • Llama 3 recipe: peak LR \(8\times10^{-5}\), 8,000 warmup, cosine to \(8\times10^{-7}\), batch 4M to 8M to 16M tokens; six-stage 8K to 128K extension on ~800B tokens; anneal last 40M tokens; mix 50% general, 25% math and reasoning, 17% code, 8% multilingual.
  • Post-training: Llama 2 = SFT (27,540), 2 RMs, RS then PPO (V1 to V5), Ghost Attention. Llama 3 = SFT, RM, RS (\(K\) = 10 to 30), DPO (\(\beta\) 0.1, NLL 0.2), six rounds, no PPO. Llama 4 = lightweight SFT, online RL, lightweight DPO.
  • Llama 4: Scout 17B active / 109B total, 16 experts, 10M context claimed; Maverick 17B / 400B, 128 experts + 1 shared, MoE every other layer, 1M; Behemoth 288B active, ~2T, teacher. 48 layers, \(d\) 5120, expert width 8,192, dense 16,384, vocabulary 202,048.
  • iRoPE: 3 of 4 layers RoPE + 8,192-token chunked attention, 1 of 4 NoPE + global with query scale \(1 + 0.1\ln(\lfloor (p+1)/8192 \rfloor + 1)\). NoLiMa effective length: Scout 1K, Maverick 2K, Llama 3.1 405B 2K.
  • Reception: "experimental chat version" (ELO 1417) on LMArena, whose policy statement said Meta should have made clearer it was optimized for human preference; report the released artifact.
