Qwen 1 to 3 and Qwen3-Next

P1 ~45 min Prereqs: 07 GQA, 08 MoE, 09 Hybrid attention, 24 RLVR

Qwen is the open model family most research groups fine-tune and run RL on, and its architecture moved further in the last year than in the three before: from a conservative Llama-style decoder (2023), through Qwen3's fine-grained MoE, to Qwen3-Next's 3:1 Gated DeltaNet hybrid with 512 experts, now the default layout of Qwen3.5 to Qwen3.8. For every step this page asks what problem it solved, what evidence supports it, and what it cost.

Three levers

For any Qwen release ask which lever moved: the data and recipe (18T tokens, four-stage post-training), the block (bias, norms, GQA, experts) or the token mixer (softmax attention versus Gated DeltaNet). Until Qwen3 it was mostly the first. Qwen3-Next is the first release where the third moved, and the family followed within five months.

The tokenizer

Qwen starts from tiktoken's cl100k_base BPE, adds common Chinese characters, words and tokens for other languages, and splits numbers into single digits, for about 152K entries [Qwen, §2.2]. The reason is economics: tokens are training and serving cost, and a Chinese-heavy corpus on an English-centric vocabulary spends many more tokens per sentence (page 02). From Qwen2 it is a byte-level BPE 分词器 with 151,643 regular tokens and 3 control tokens (22 in Qwen2.5); Qwen3 lists 151,669. The config's 151,936 embedding rows (\(1{,}187 \times 128\)) are padding. Qwen3.5 moves to a new vocabulary of about 248K.

QKV bias in, QK-norm out

Qwen dropped biases everywhere except the Q, K and V projections, which got them "to enhance the extrapolation ability of the model", citing a blog post by Jianlin Su [Qwen, §2.3]. Qwen3 removes them and adds QK-Norm "to ensure stable training" [Qwen3, §2]. Neither report has an ablation, so read it as a change of priorities: extrapolation was the open problem at 2K training context, while at 36T tokens the problem is stability, which QK-norm attacks by bounding the attention logits (page 11). That a key bias becomes less useful once logits are bounded is our reasoning, not a tested claim.

Qwen1.5 used grouped-query attention 分组查询注意力 (GQA, page 07) only in the 32B and 110B. Qwen2 applies it to every size "so that they can enjoy the benefits of faster speed and less memory usage in model inference" [Qwen2 blog]. Long context: from free tricks to a staged recipe

Qwen (2023) trained at 2,048 tokens and extended with three training-free tools: dynamic NTK interpolation, LogN-scaling and layer-wise windows. Qwen-7B's perplexity at 16,384 tokens fell from 2,645 to 5.71 (NTK), 4.62 (plus LogN) and 4.32 (plus windows) [Qwen, Tab. 3]. Qwen2 made length a stage: 4K to 32K at the end of pretraining with the RoPE base raised from 10,000 to 1,000,000 (ABF), then YaRN and Dual Chunk Attention (DCA) at inference for 131,072 tokens [Qwen2, §3.2]. Qwen2.5-1M used five stages (4K to 256K, bases 10K to 10M) plus DCA and YaRN to reach 1M, with sparse prefill 3× to 7× faster [Qwen2.5-1M]; mechanics and RULER by stage are on page 10. The recipe's shape is raise the base, train a little at the new length, extrapolate the last factor of four for free, and Qwen3 reuses it unchanged.

Qwen2.5: data is the lever

Qwen2.5 kept the architecture and moved data from 7T to 18T tokens [Qwen2.5, §3.1]. Qwen2 had found that relaxing the quality threshold to reach 12T tokens brought no significant gain [Qwen2, §3.1]; Qwen2.5 pairs its larger corpus with better filtering (Qwen2-Instruct quality scores, reward-model-filtered synthetic data, down-sampled e-commerce and social media, up-sampled science). The specialists are data engines: Qwen2.5-Math continues from Qwen2.5 on a 1T-plus-token math corpus, and its 7B base scores 55.4 on MATH against 51.1 for Qwen2-72B [Qwen2.5-Math]; Qwen2.5-Coder trains on 5.5T tokens, and a 70% code, 20% text, 10% math mix beat code-only on the overall average in the authors' 7B ablation (code-only scored slightly higher on the two code columns) [Qwen2.5-Coder, §3.1.2, Tab. 3]. Qwen3 then uses them to synthesize trillions of tokens (page 12).

Why Qwen2.5 became the RL default, and the risk in that

By 2025 much of the open RLVR literature (page 24) ran on Qwen bases: DAPO trains Qwen2.5-32B, Oat-Zero uses Qwen2.5-Math-7B, VAPO uses Qwen2.5-32B, and the DeepSeek-R1 report runs its RL-versus-distillation control on Qwen2.5-32B. Shao et al. call Qwen "the de facto choice for RLVR research in the open-source community" [Spurious Rewards, §1]. How large a share that is has not been measured.

Why

  1. Availability. Open bases from 0.5B to 72B, most under Apache 2.0 [Qwen2.5, Tab. 1]: debug at 1.5B, report at 32B, one tokenizer.
  2. Strong math and code in the base, from Qwen2.5-Math and -Coder data (above).
  3. Reasoning behaviors already present. Under identical PPO on Countdown, Qwen2.5-3B reached about 60% and Llama-3.2-3B about 30%, because Qwen "naturally exhibits" verification, backtracking, subgoal setting and backward chaining. Priming Llama with traces containing those behaviors, even incorrect ones, made it match Qwen [Gandhi et al. 2025]. RL amplifies what appears in successful samples.
  4. Convention (our inference): once published results used Qwen, comparisons needed it too.

The risk: results that may be Qwen-specific

Shao et al. ran about 300 steps of GRPO on Qwen2.5-Math-7B with uninformative rewards. MATH-500 rose +29.1 points with ground truth, +24.1 with rewards for incorrect majority-vote labels and +21.4 with random rewards; Llama3 and OLMo2 gained little, and on post-cutoff AIME 2025 the spurious gains largely vanish [Shao et al., Abstract, §2-3, App. D]. Their mechanism is a GRPO clipping bias: with random rewards the expected gradient is zero without clipping, but clipping raises the probability of tokens the model already favors, amplifying high-prior behavior. In Qwen2.5-Math that is "code reasoning" (Python-style reasoning without execution), rising from 65% to about 90% of answers. The clipping-bias derivation and the surrounding debate are on page 24.

Wu et al. offer a compatible second account, memorization. Given the first 60% of a MATH-500 problem, Qwen2.5-Math-7B regenerates the rest with 54.6% exact match, against 3.8% for Llama3.1-8B, and on post-cutoff LiveMathBench the rate is 0.0%. On freshly generated arithmetic only correct rewards helped, and the chat template alone lowers a Qwen base's starting accuracy [Wu et al. 2025]. That is a preprint marked work in progress; the two accounts are not exclusive, since a memorized answer is a high-prior behavior.

A protocol before you trust an RLVR result

(1) Repeat on a second model family. (2) Add random-reward and incorrect-label baselines. (3) Evaluate on post-cutoff or generated problems. (4) Match the prompt template to the base's pretraining. (5) Report pass@\(k\) at large \(k\) (page 24). Qwen's own pipelines are Qwen on Qwen: Qwen3's cold-start queries are filtered by Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, its responses come from QwQ-32B, and its reference judge is Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct [Qwen3, §4.1, §4.4].

From your jet tagger

A method validated on one Monte Carlo generator's tune may exploit that tune's quirks. You would check a second generator, data the tune never saw, and a null control such as shuffled labels. Base-model families are the generators, post-cutoff problems the unseen data, random rewards the shuffled labels.

Global-batch load balancing

Qwen3 adopts the loss of Qiu et al. [Qiu et al. 2025]. The load-balancing loss 负载均衡损失 \(E \sum_i f_i P_i\) (\(f_i\): assignment fraction, \(P_i\): mean router probability) is normally computed per micro-batch, a few sequences often from one domain, so the constraint is nearly sequence-level: a code-only micro-batch must still spread over all experts. The fix all-reduces only the \(E\)-sized count vector and keeps probabilities local:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{micro}} = \frac{1}{N_P}\sum_{j=1}^{N_P} E \sum_i f^{\,j}_i P^{\,j}_i, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{global}} = \frac{1}{N_P}\sum_{j=1}^{N_P} E \sum_i \bar f_i \, P^{\,j}_i, \quad \bar f_i = \frac{\text{assignments to } i \text{ over all groups}}{\text{tokens} \cdot k} \]
\(\bar f\) is a constant; gradients still flow through the local \(P^j\)

Two experts: micro-batch 0 is all code and goes to expert 0, micro-batch 1 is all prose and goes to expert 1. Inside each, \(f = P = (1,0)\) or \((0,1)\), so \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{micro}} = 2\), the worst value; globally \(\bar f = (\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{global}} = 1\), the minimum. The global loss accepts domain-specialized experts as long as the corpus is balanced (first drill).

Problem

Idea
Synchronize expert counts across groups (or accumulate them over gradient-accumulation steps) before forming \(f\).

The constraint becomes "the corpus is balanced". A shuffled control (a micro-batch-sized random draw from the global batch) recovers nearly all the gain, so token diversity in the balancing set matters, not token count.

Pretraining: 36T tokens in three stages

Data doubles to about 36T tokens in 119 languages, adding PDF text read by Qwen2.5-VL and trillions of synthetic tokens from Qwen2.5, -Math and -Coder; over 30T tokens carry quality, domain and safety labels, and the mixture is optimized per instance with proxy models [Qwen3, §3.1]. Stages: over 30T tokens at 4,096 context; about 5T of STEM, code, reasoning and synthetic data, also at 4,096, with accelerated learning-rate decay; then hundreds of billions at 32,768 with ABF, YaRN and DCA [§3.2]. Headline claims: MoE bases match dense ones "with only 1/5 activated parameters", and Qwen3-235B-A22B-Base beats DeepSeek-V3-Base on 14 of 15 benchmarks with about 1/3 of its total and 2/3 of its active parameters (MMLU 87.81 against 87.19; MATH 71.84 against 62.62) [§3.3, Tab. 3]; data mechanics are on page 12.

Post-training: four stages, one shortcut

  1. Long-CoT cold start. Verifiable queries filtered with Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, \(N\) responses each from QwQ-32B, six response filters (wrong answer, repetition, guesswork, thinking/summary mismatch, language mixing, validation overlap). Kept small: "it is preferable to minimize both the number of training samples and the training steps."
  2. Reasoning RL. 3,995 new, learnable, hard, diverse query-verifier pairs; GRPO with a large batch, many rollouts and off-policy updates, entropy controlled. Qwen3-235B-A22B: AIME'24 70.1 to 85.1 in 170 steps.
  3. Thinking-mode fusion. SFT on the Stage-2 model with thinking data rejection-sampled from that same model, plus curated non-thinking data, under one template with /think and /no_think flags (non-thinking replies keep an empty think block).
  4. General RL. Over 20 tasks with rule-based rewards, a reference-based judge (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) and a preference reward model, including multi-turn agent rollouts.

Thinking budget. Once a model can answer with or without thinking, it can answer from partial thinking. At a user-set threshold the serving code halts generation and appends "Considering the limited time by the user, I have to give the solution based on the thinking directly now.\n</think>.\n\n", and the model writes its answer. The report says the ability "is not explicitly trained but emerges naturally" from fusion, and accuracy rises smoothly with the budget on four benchmarks [Qwen3, §4.3, §4.7]. This thinking budget 思考预算 is enforced outside the model, not learned.

Strong-to-weak distillation 蒸馏 (page 19) replaces all four stages for the 0.6B to 14B and 30B-A3B models: off-policy SFT on teacher outputs in both modes, then on-policy training that minimizes KL to the logits of Qwen3-32B or 235B-A22B, at "only 1/10 of the GPU hours". From one Qwen3-8B checkpoint (AIME'24 55.0, pass@64 90.0), RL reaches 67.6 (pass@64 unchanged) in 17,920 GPU hours and on-policy distillation 74.4 (pass@64 93.3) in 1,800 [Tab. 21]: one checkpoint, math and code only, teacher cost excluded.

2507: undoing the fusion

On July 21, 2025 the Qwen team wrote: "After talking with the community and thinking it through, we decided to stop using hybrid thinking mode. Instead, we'll train Instruct and Thinking models separately so we can get the best quality possible" [Qwen post]. Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 and -Thinking-2507 each state that they support only one mode, with 262,144 native context [cards]. No ablation of fused versus separate was published; the in-house evidence is Table 22 above. The tension (our reading): an instruct model is rewarded for short direct answers, a thinking model for spending tokens, and one set of weights and one RL run serve both. DeepSeek went the other way a month later with V3.1's template-switched hybrid (page 28). The Qwen3.8-27B card (August 2026) describes a single checkpoint again, thinking on by default and tunable by reasoning_effort [card]. The real question is how to control reasoning length without hurting peak accuracy.

GSPO: a sequence-level ratio for MoE reinforcement learning

GRPO (page 24, which also has the gradient comparison and the drill) weights each token's advantage by \(w_{i,t} = \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,\lt t}) / \pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,\lt t})\) and clips each token. Group Sequence Policy Optimization, from the Qwen team [Zheng et al. 2025], changes the unit to the whole response:

\[ s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|} = \exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t} \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,\lt t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,\lt t})}\Big), \quad J = \E\Big[\frac1G \sum_{i=1}^{G} \min\big(s_i \hat A_i,\ \clip(s_i, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\, \hat A_i\big)\Big] \]
\(\hat A_i\) is the group-normalized reward as in GRPO; \(s_i\) is the geometric mean of the token ratios
  1. Token ratios cannot do their job. Importance sampling corrects a distribution mismatch by averaging a weight over many samples; a token ratio uses one sampled token per position, so it corrects nothing and injects noise that "accumulates over long sequences and is exacerbated by the clipping mechanism" [§3]. The unit of the objective should match the unit of the reward, which is per sequence. In the gradient, GSPO gives every token of a response the same weight \(s_i\), while GRPO scales each token by its own \(w_{i,t}\) [§4.2].
  2. MoE makes it worse. In the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, roughly 10% of the experts activated under the new policy differ from those under the old policy for the same rollout after each gradient update, so token likelihoods jump. Qwen's earlier fix was Routing Replay: cache the experts chosen under \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) and force them under \(\pi_\theta\), at a cost in memory and communication and a cap on capacity. A sequence likelihood is much steadier, since the model keeps its language-modeling ability when single routes change, so GSPO needs no replay [§5.3].
  3. The scale of \(\varepsilon\) changes. Clipping ranges are \(3 \times 10^{-4}\) (left) and \(4 \times 10^{-4}\) (right) for GSPO against 0.2 and 0.27 for the tuned GRPO baseline, and the average clipped-token fraction is 0.15 for GSPO against 0.0013 for GRPO. GSPO clips about 100 times more tokens and still learns faster per unit of compute, which the authors read as evidence that token-level gradients were noisy [§5.1-5.2].

What \(\varepsilon = 4 \times 10^{-4}\) means. \(s_i^{|y_i|}\) is the ratio of sequence probabilities. For a 1,000-token response, \((1 + 4 \times 10^{-4})^{1000} = e^{0.4} \approx 1.49\): the response is clipped once the new policy gives it about 49% more probability, a mean per-token log-ratio of only 0.0004. Per-token clipping is looser: a 0.1% change on every token compounds to \(1.001^{1000} \approx 2.7\) on the sequence, yet no token approaches the 0.8 to 1.27 band. The paper also claims sequence likelihoods tolerate the numeric gap between training and inference engines [§5.4] (page 25). The ratio and loss are in the grpo drill of page 24.

Gated attention: the mechanism Qwen3-Next ships

Softmax attention cannot "do nothing": rows sum to 1, so a head with nothing to say parks its mass on a sink token and the network builds massive activations to support it. The Qwen team compared 30 variants of gated attention 门控注意力 on 15B-A2.54B MoE and 1.7B dense models trained on up to 3.5T tokens [Qiu et al. 2025]. The winner multiplies each head's attention output, before \(W_O\), by an elementwise sigmoid gate computed from the layer's pre-normed input \(X\) (position G1; page 11 has the full study):

\[ Y' = Y \odot \sigma(X W_\theta), \qquad Y \in \R^{T \times n_h \times d_h}, \quad W_\theta \in \R^{d \times n_h d_h} \]

On the 15B MoE at 400B tokens, perplexity falls from 6.026 to 5.761 and MMLU rises from 58.79 to 60.82, for 201M extra parameters (1.3%) and under 2% wall time; a headwise gate costs 1.6M parameters and reaches 5.792. Parameter-matched controls are worse: 8 KV heads (+50M) 5.979, 48 query heads (+201M) 5.953, four more experts (+400M) 5.964. First-token attention drops from 46.7% to 4.8%, and after YaRN to 128K the RULER score rises from 31.65 to 58.82 [Tab. 1, Fig. 2, Tab. 5]. The two explanations are non-linearity (per head, \(W_V W_O\) is one low-rank linear map, and the gate puts a non-linearity between) and sparsity (small, query-dependent gates let a head silence itself).

Qwen3 itself, released in April 2025 before that paper (arXiv, May 10), uses QK-norm and no gate. Qwen3-Next is the first shipped use: its q_proj is \(8192 \times 2048\) for 16 heads of 256, that is \(2 \cdot 16 \cdot 256\), and in the Hugging Face implementation it is split per head into a query and a gate, with head outputs multiplied by \(\sigma(\text{gate})\) before o_proj (modeling_qwen3_next.py). That is the paper's computation with the gate weights fused into the query matrix. The Gated DeltaNet layers have a separate output gate, an RMSNorm gated by SiLU as in the original GDN; the Qwen3.8-Next design paper swaps it for a bounded sigmoid, \(o_t = W_o[\sigma(W_z x_t) \odot \RMSNorm(y_t)]\), reporting consistent gains [Eq. 11].

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next: a preview of the "Qwen4" architecture

The card calls it an "experimental preview of the architecture that will underpin Qwen4" (its config.json declares model_type: qwen4_exp), and the design paper documents four changes [Qwen3.8-Next design paper]. Qwen Sparse Attention: at continued pretraining (256K sequences) the softmax layers become sparse, with an indexer that average-pools keys in blocks of 4 tokens and keeps the top 512 blocks (2,048 tokens); after a 2B-token indexer warm-up and about 200B tokens of sparse training, loss is within about \(10^{-4}\) of full attention, and at 1M tokens the kernels run 7.6× faster in prefill and 4.9× in decode (page 09). Gated Residual: four residual branches, an elementwise read gate and a scalar write gate; an isolated ablation at three times the optimal learning rate cuts loss spikes from 32.0 to 3.2 per 10k steps, and at four times the AdamW baseline spikes 183 times per 10k steps against none for Muon plus Gated Residual. N-gram embeddings: hashed bigrams and trigrams (config: ngram_size: 3, 8 hash heads per order) are looked up at layer 2 in tables of about 20M rows per head (ngram_vocab_size_base) and 160 dimensions, \(16 \times 20\text{M} \times 160 = 51.2\)B, matching the reported 51B held in host memory; loss falls monotonically with table size while accuracy saturates. Optimizer: Muon for 2D linear maps, AdamW for embeddings, head and router, a refit scaling law, no batch-size warmup (18.8% more steps), and a run with no loss spike and no qk-clip.

The headline is self-reported: on 14 pretraining benchmarks the base model leads the 397B-A17B on 8 and trails by at most 2.6 points on the rest, with one third of the activated parameters, one third of the tokens and roughly one ninth of the training FLOPs [Abstract]. Note the label: the paper's Table 11 names that 397B/17B baseline Qwen3.7-Plus-Base (the closed model), while its introduction points to the Qwen3.5 flagship. Treat it as a strong claim from one lab, awaiting replication. All four changes target long-context cost or stability at scale.

Common misconceptions

  • "Qwen3 is a hybrid-attention model." Qwen3 (April 2025) is a standard GQA plus QK-norm Transformer with an MoE variant. The Gated DeltaNet hybrid arrived with Qwen3-Next in September 2025 and became the family default only from Qwen3.5.
  • "Qwen3 uses gated attention." It uses QK-norm. Gated attention (a sigmoid gate on each head's output) first ships in Qwen3-Next. It is also not the Gated DeltaNet gates, which control decay and writes of the recurrent state.
  • "Activated parameters are comparable across model cards." The 22B of Qwen3-235B-A22B counts the embedding and the LM head (22.19B), while Qwen3-Next's 3B matches the non-embedding count (3.25B; 3.87B with both). Recompute from the config.
  • "Global-batch balancing is one global auxiliary loss." Only the expert counts are synchronized (an \(E\)-sized all-reduce). The probabilities stay local and are the only path for gradients.
  • "GSPO is GRPO with a sequence-level advantage." GRPO's advantage is already per sequence. GSPO changes the importance ratio and the clipping unit to the length-normalized sequence ratio, at a different scale of \(\varepsilon\).
  • "The thinking budget is a trained length controller." It is a forced stop: the serving code inserts a fixed sentence and the closing think tag at a threshold. The ability emerges from fusing the two modes, and Qwen's 2507 models then dropped the fusion.
  • "Large RL gains on Qwen bases show that RL teaches reasoning." Random and incorrect rewards give most of the MATH-500 gain on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, and the base shows signs of memorizing the benchmark. Test on another family and on post-cutoff problems.
  • "A 3:1 hybrid cuts memory 4×." The KV cache shrinks with the share of softmax layers, and Qwen3-Next's cache is 10.7× smaller than Qwen3-32B's because it also uses fewer, wider-headed KV heads. The recurrent state is a fixed cost that equals the cache at about 3K tokens, so short sequences see no saving.

Interview questions

Warm-up What changed in the attention block between Qwen2.5 and Qwen3, and why?

GQA, RoPE, pre-norm RMSNorm and SwiGLU stay. Qwen3 removes the QKV bias that Qwen introduced in 2023 "to enhance the extrapolation ability of the model", and adds QK-norm (an RMSNorm on each head's query and key over \(d_h = 128\), before RoPE) "to ensure stable training". The reports give no ablation. The plausible story is a change of priorities: extrapolation mattered when training contexts were 2K to 8K, and by 2025 the risk was logit growth and loss spikes over 36T tokens, which QK-norm bounds directly (page 11). Qwen3 also fixes \(d_h = 128\) at every size, so \(n_h d_h\) can exceed \(d\) (64 × 128 = 8192 = 2\(d\) in the 235B).

Warm-up Compute the KV cache per token of Qwen3-32B, and for one 128K-token sequence. How does Qwen3-235B-A22B compare?

Qwen3-32B has 64 layers, 8 KV heads and \(d_h = 128\): \(64 \cdot 2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2\ \text{B} = 262{,}144\) B = 256 KiB per token, so \(256\text{ KiB} \times 131{,}072 = 32\) GiB. Qwen3-235B-A22B has 94 layers but 4 KV heads: \(94 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 192{,}512\) B = 188 KiB, or 23.5 GiB. The bigger model has the smaller cache, because its KV heads are half as many and that outweighs 30 more layers.

Warm-up What is Qwen3's thinking budget, and how is it enforced?

One checkpoint answers in thinking mode (<think>...</think> then the reply) or non-thinking mode (an empty think block), switched by /think and /no_think flags or a template option. For a budget, the serving code stops generation when the thinking reaches the threshold, appends "Considering the limited time by the user, I have to give the solution based on the thinking directly now." plus the closing tag, and lets the model write its answer from the partial reasoning. Qwen says this ability "is not explicitly trained but emerges naturally" from thinking-mode fusion, and accuracy rises smoothly with the budget on four benchmarks. It is external control, not a learned length policy.

Core What architectural choices distinguish Qwen3's MoE from DeepSeek-V3's?

Qwen3-235B-A22B: 128 routed experts of width 1536, top-8, no shared expert, MoE in every layer, softmax routing with top-8 weights renormalized, a global-batch auxiliary loss, and GQA (4 KV heads) with QK-norm. DeepSeek-V3: 256 routed experts of width 2048 plus 1 shared expert, top-8, first 3 layers dense, sigmoid scores with a routed scaling factor of 2.5, bias-based auxiliary-loss-free balancing (plus a small sequence-wise loss), and MLA. Beyond the MoE: V3 has an MTP module, Qwen3 does not.

Why they differ: Qwen favors standard components that run on stock kernels and puts its novelty into data (36T tokens) and RL. DeepSeek co-designs with hardware. What they share: fine-grained top-8 experts and balancing over the whole batch, not per sequence. Evidence is thin on shared experts: DeepSeekMoE's 2B model lost a lot (Pile loss 1.808 to 2.414) when its trained shared expert was switched off at test time, but that is a probe, not a from-scratch comparison; Qwen3 dropped the shared expert with no ablation, and Qwen3-Next brought one back. Cache: 188 KiB per token for Qwen3-235B against 68.6 KiB for V3's MLA.

Core Why did Qwen introduce GSPO?

GRPO's per-token ratio \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\) is computed from one sampled token per position, so it cannot do the distribution correction that importance sampling needs. It adds variance that grows with length and is amplified by clipping, and it weights the tokens of one response unequally. In MoE models it is worse: after each gradient update roughly 10% of the activated experts change for the same rollout (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base), so token likelihoods jump. GRPO then needed Routing Replay, which costs memory and communication and constrains capacity.

GSPO uses \(s_i = (\pi_\theta(y_i)/\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i))^{1/|y_i|}\), clips whole responses at about \(3\text{-}4 \times 10^{-4}\) and weights every token equally in the gradient. Sequence likelihood is stable under route flips, so no replay is needed. Reported: about 15% of tokens clipped for GSPO against 0.13% for GRPO, yet better reward per unit of compute. Caveats: evidence from Qwen's own runs, coarser credit assignment (GSPO-token restores per-token advantages), and a new \(\varepsilon\) scale to tune.

Core What is gated attention and what does it fix?

A sigmoid gate multiplies each head's attention output before \(W_O\): \(Y' = Y \odot \sigma(X W_\theta)\) with \(X\) the layer's pre-normed input (elementwise, head-specific in the best variant). Softmax cannot output "nothing", so heads dump mass on a sink token and the network builds massive activations to support it. The gate lets a head emit near zero for an irrelevant query. On a 15B-A2.54B MoE at 400B tokens: perplexity 6.026 to 5.761, MMLU +2.0, with 201M added parameters (1.3%) and under 2% latency; first-token attention 46.7% to 4.8%; stable at larger learning rates; RULER at 128K after YaRN 31.65 to 58.82. Parameter-matched controls (more KV heads, more query heads, more experts) did worse. Two explanations: non-linearity between the low-rank \(W_V W_O\), and query-dependent sparsity. It is used in Qwen3-Next's softmax layers. Caveat: one team's study, not compared with learned sink logits.

Core Describe Qwen3-Next's hybrid design and its trade-offs.

48 layers in 12 blocks of three Gated DeltaNet layers and one gated softmax layer, each followed by an MoE block of 512 experts (10 routed plus 1 shared, width 512). Softmax layers have 16 Q heads, 2 KV heads, \(d_h = 256\), partial RoPE on 25% of dimensions. Delta-rule layers keep a fixed state (32 heads of 128 × 128 per layer) that erases what it already predicts before writing, so it acts as an overwriting associative memory.

Numbers: KV cache \(12 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 256 \cdot 2 = 24{,}576\) B = 24 KiB per token, so 3 GiB at 128K against 32 GiB for Qwen3-32B (10.7×), plus a 72 MiB fp32 state that equals the cache at 3,072 tokens. Trade-offs: a finite state cannot recall arbitrary tokens, so exact retrieval depends on the 12 softmax layers; new kernels, state precision and prefix-caching needs; the saving is a long-context effect; and with only 2 KV heads, tensor parallelism above 2 replicates the cache (page 07). Evidence: a 25B-A3B ablation averaging 53.81 for the GDN hybrid against 49.87 (full) and 51.15 (SWA hybrid) but worse on EvalPlus; throughput multiples (7× prefill at 4K, over 10× beyond 32K) are vendor claims.

Core Why are Qwen bases so common in RL research, and what is the risk in that?

Reasons: open weights across 0.5B to 72B (mostly Apache 2.0) with one tokenizer; strong math and code from pretraining data built with Qwen2.5-Math and -Coder; and pre-existing reasoning behaviors. Under identical PPO on Countdown, Qwen2.5-3B reached about 60% and Llama-3.2-3B about 30%, because Qwen already shows verification and backtracking and RL amplifies behaviors that appear in successful samples. Convention then locks it in.

Risk: findings may be Qwen-specific. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, GRPO with random rewards raised MATH-500 by 21.4 points against 29.1 for ground truth, and with incorrect labels by 24.1; Llama3 and OLMo2 did not gain. One account is clipping bias amplifying high-prior behavior (code reasoning 65% to 90%); another is memorization (54.6% exact-match completion of MATH-500 from a 60% prefix). Response: replicate on a second family, include random-reward baselines, use post-cutoff or generated problems, match templates, report pass@\(k\).

Core Explain the global-batch load-balancing loss. What does it change and what does it cost?

Standard loss: \(E \sum_i f_i P_i\), with \(f_i\) the assignment fraction and \(P_i\) the mean router probability. Frameworks compute it per micro-batch and average, which acts almost at the sequence level and forces even a code-only micro-batch to use all experts. Qiu et al. all-reduce the \(E\)-sized count vector so \(\bar f\) reflects the global batch, keep \(P^j\) local, and average \(E\sum_i \bar f_i P^j_i\) over groups (gradient only through \(P^j\); a buffer over gradient-accumulation steps approximates the global batch). Example: two domain-specific micro-batches, each collapsed onto one of two experts, give a micro loss of 2 and a global loss of 1.

Evidence: perplexity 7.347 to 7.198 (3.4B-A0.6B, 400B tokens) and 5.778 to 5.603 (15B-A2.54B); a shuffled control shows token diversity, not token count, matters. Cost: stragglers, 5.8% slower steps in one setting, reduced to 2.6% by a small micro-batch term.

Core Calculate the total and activated parameters of Qwen3-235B-A22B from its config.

Config: \(L = 94\), \(d = 4096\), 64 Q and 4 KV heads of 128, expert width 1536, 128 experts, top-8, \(V = 151{,}936\), untied. Per layer: attention \(4096 \cdot 8192 + 2 \cdot 4096 \cdot 512 + 8192 \cdot 4096 = 71{,}303{,}168\); router \(4096 \cdot 128 = 524{,}288\); experts \(128 \cdot 3 \cdot 4096 \cdot 1536 = 2{,}415{,}919{,}104\); plus small norms. Times 94, plus \(2 \cdot 151{,}936 \cdot 4096 = 1{,}244{,}659{,}712\) for embedding and head, gives 235,093,634,560 (equal to the checkpoint). Active: replace 128 experts by 8, giving 22.19B with embedding and head, 21.57B with only the head, 20.95B without either. The name's "22B" matches the first. Attention is 2.85% of the total but 32% of the non-embedding active weights.

Deep-dive Design question: you want one model that can both answer instantly and think for a long time. What did Qwen3 do, what went wrong, and what would you do?

Qwen3 trained a long-CoT cold start, then reasoning RL (3,995 pairs, GRPO), then fused non-thinking data into the RL model by SFT (thinking data rejection-sampled from the same model, one template with /think and /no_think), then general RL over 20+ tasks. Mode switching improved (ThinkFollow 88.7 to 98.9) and general scores rose (Arena-Hard 86.8 to 93.8), but Qwen3-32B's thinking-mode AIME'24 fell from 83.8 to 81.4 and LiveCodeBench from 68.4 to 65.7, and Qwen accepted the trade. In July 2025 it stopped fusing and shipped Instruct and Thinking checkpoints, then in 2026 cards returned to one checkpoint with reasoning_effort.

My plan: treat it as a routing or control problem, not a weights problem. Options: separate checkpoints and a router (best peak, double serving cost); fusion with effort control (one model, some regression); or specialists plus on-policy distillation (DeepSeek-V4 style, page 19). Measure the regression on your hardest reasoning benchmarks, the length distribution and instruction following, and keep an in-mode compliance metric like ThinkFollow.

Deep-dive Random rewards raise Qwen2.5-Math-7B's MATH-500 accuracy by about 21 points. What do you conclude, and which experiments would you run?

Conclude nothing about the reward. Separate four hypotheses. (1) Format or template adaptation: compare with the base's best greedy score without the chat template, since the template lowers Qwen base accuracy and the "gain" may only return to it. (2) Clipping bias amplifying priors: repeat with clipping disabled (one gradient step per rollout); the expected update is then zero, so gains should vanish. Track the frequency of the high-prior behavior (code reasoning). (3) Memorization: partial-prompt completion on MATH-500 and on a post-cutoff set, and training on freshly generated problems, where only correct rewards should help. (4) Model specificity: repeat on Llama and OLMo. Add pass@\(k\) at large \(k\) to see whether the model sharpens or expands. Report all with error bars over seeds, since 300-step GRPO runs are noisy. Only if random rewards fail on clean data and on other families can you say the ground-truth reward is what teaches the skill.

Deep-dive Trade-off: you must run stable RL on a 30B-A3B MoE. Compare token-level GRPO with Routing Replay, engine-routing replay, and GSPO.

Problem size: ~10% of activated experts change after one update in Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, and between an inference engine and a trainer about 10% of router decisions and 94% of tokens (at least one layer) differ (page 25). Routing Replay (train-side) fixes update-induced flips, costs memory and communication for cached routes, caps capacity and does not fix engine mismatch. Engine-routing replay also fixes mismatch, at a small logging cost, but biases the target policy and did not help on-policy in the study summarized there. GSPO needs no replay because sequence likelihood is stable, tolerates numeric mismatch, and uses engine log-probs directly, but gives one ratio per response, requires re-tuning \(\varepsilon\) near \(10^{-4}\) and has evidence mostly from Qwen. I would start with GSPO (or its token variant if multi-turn credit matters), monitor the clipped fraction (about 15% for GSPO in the paper, against 0.13% for GRPO, so a high value is not a warning by itself), the train-inference KL and the expert-flip rate, and add a replay method only if the flip rate still correlates with instability.

Deep-dive Qwen3-Next activates 10 of 512 experts and caches 24 KiB per token. What limits going sparser, and how do the two choices interact at serving time?

Limits on sparsity: all 80B parameters stay resident (about 160 GB in bf16); each 512-wide expert is a small GEMM; a token fans out to 11 experts, so all-to-all traffic and balance dominate; and the sources give only a training-loss claim for expert count, not a benchmark ablation. At decode, a batch touches most experts, so weight reads approach the full model.

Interaction: the small cache lets more sequences share a node, and a bigger batch amortizes expert reads, which is exactly what sparse MoE needs. Idealized 8 × 80 GB and 128K contexts, ignoring activations: Qwen3-Next has about 481 GB free after ~159 GB of weights, and at 3.07 GiB per sequence that is about 145 sequences; Qwen3-32B has about 574 GB free after 65.5 GB of weights, and at 32 GiB per sequence about 16. Caveat: with 2 KV heads, 8-way tensor parallelism replicates each KV head 4×, so real deployments use small attention TP or data-parallel attention (page 07), which shrinks the advantage.
