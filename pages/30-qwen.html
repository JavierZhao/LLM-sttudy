Qwen 1 to 3 and Qwen3-Next

P1 ~45 min Prereqs: 07 GQA, 08 MoE, 09 Hybrid attention, 24 RLVR

Qwen is the open model family that most research groups fine-tune and run RL on, and its architecture moved further in the last year than in the three before: from a conservative Llama-style decoder (2023), through Qwen3's fine-grained MoE, to Qwen3-Next's 3:1 Gated DeltaNet hybrid with 512 experts, which became the default layout of Qwen3.5 and Qwen3.8. This page follows that lineage and asks of every step what problem it solved, what evidence supports it, and what it cost.

What interviewers probe
  • Config fluency: from Qwen3-235B-A22B's config.json, derive total and activated parameters and the KV cache per token; then do the same for Qwen3-Next, where only one layer in four keeps a cache.
  • Qwen3 versus DeepSeek-V3: shared experts, gating and normalization, the scope of the balance loss, GQA with QK-norm versus MLA, GSPO versus GRPO. Every difference is a bet, and you should be able to name the evidence for each side.
  • GSPO: why a token-level importance ratio is a bad estimator, why MoE routing makes it worse, and what the sequence-level ratio changes in the gradient.
  • Gated attention and the hybrid: what the sigmoid gate fixes, why 3:1, and what a Gated DeltaNet layer gives up compared with a softmax layer.
  • Reading RL papers critically: why so many RLVR results come from Qwen bases, what random-reward and contamination studies found, and how to design a check.

Three levers

For any Qwen release ask which lever moved: the data and recipe (Qwen2.5's 18T tokens, Qwen3's four-stage post-training), the block (bias, norms, GQA, expert design), or the token mixer (softmax attention versus Gated DeltaNet). Until Qwen3 the answer was almost always the first. Qwen3-Next is the first release where the third lever moved, and the rest of the family followed within five months.

Qwen to Qwen2.5: a conservative backbone and where it bent

The tokenizer

Qwen starts from tiktoken's cl100k_base BPE (the GPT-4 vocabulary), adds common Chinese characters and words plus tokens for other languages, and splits numbers into single digits. The result is about 152K entries [Qwen, §2.2]. The reason is economics: a token is a unit of both training and serving cost, and a Chinese-heavy corpus on an English-centric vocabulary spends far more tokens per sentence (page 02). From Qwen2 on it is a byte-level BPE 分词器 with 151,643 regular tokens and 3 control tokens. Qwen2.5 raises the control tokens to 22, two of them for tools [Qwen2.5, §2], and Qwen3's report lists 151,669 [Qwen3, §2]. The embedding matrix in config.json has 151,936 rows (\(1{,}187 \times 128\)), padded for tensor-parallel divisibility, and the extra rows are never targets. Qwen3.5 moves to a new vocabulary of about 248K (248,320 padded rows) and advertises 201 languages, though its card does not say the two are linked (see the page 02 table).

QKV bias in, QK-norm out

Qwen followed the Llama recipe of dropping biases everywhere except one place: it added biases to the Q, K and V projections "to enhance the extrapolation ability of the model", citing a blog post by Jianlin Su [Qwen, §2.3]. Qwen2 and Qwen2.5 keep it. Qwen3 removes it and adds QK-Norm "to ensure stable training" [Qwen3, §2]. Neither report contains an ablation, so the honest summary is a change of priorities. The bias belongs to the era of 2K to 8K training contexts, when extrapolation was the open problem (Qwen was trained at 2,048 tokens). By 2025 the open problem was stability over 36T tokens, and QK-norm attacks it directly by bounding the attention logits (page 11). Once the logits are bounded, a learned key bias is plausibly less useful, but that is our reasoning, not a tested claim.

GQA arrives

Qwen1.5 used GQA (page 07) only in the 32B and 110B models. The Qwen2 announcement says so explicitly and applies it to every size "so that they can enjoy the benefits of faster speed and less memory usage in model inference" [Qwen2 blog], and the config files agree (Qwen1.5-7B and 72B have as many KV heads as query heads). Long context: from free tricks to a staged recipe

Qwen (2023) was trained at 2,048 tokens and extended with three training-free tools: dynamic NTK-aware interpolation, LogN-scaling of the attention logits, and layer-wise window attention with shorter windows in lower layers. On arXiv text, Qwen-7B's perplexity at 16,384 tokens fell from 2,645 (no tricks) to 5.71 (dynamic NTK), 4.62 (plus LogN) and 4.32 (plus windows) [Qwen, Tab. 3]. Qwen2 made length a training stage: 4,096 to 32,768 tokens at the end of pretraining with the RoPE base raised from 10,000 to 1,000,000 (ABF), then YaRN and Dual Chunk Attention (DCA) at inference for 131,072 tokens [Qwen2, §3.2]. Qwen2.5-1M pushed the same idea to five stages (4K, 32K, 64K, 128K, 256K; bases 10K, 1M, 1M, 5M, 10M) and reaches 1M tokens by extrapolating with DCA plus YaRN, with a 3× to 7× faster prefill from sparse attention [Qwen2.5-1M, §3, §5]. The mechanics, the RULER-by-stage table and the sparse-prefill trick are on page 10. What matters here is the recipe's shape: raise the base, train a little at the new length, then extrapolate the last factor of four for free. Qwen3 reuses it unchanged (over 30T plus about 5T tokens at 4K, hundreds of billions at 32K, then YaRN plus DCA) [Qwen3, §3.2].

Upcycled fine-grained MoE: Qwen1.5-MoE and Qwen2-57B-A14B

Qwen's first MoE models were built from dense ones. Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B started from Qwen-1.8B, used 64 fine-grained experts (4 shared and always on, 60 routed of which 4 fire) and, according to the blog, matched 7B dense models with 2.0B non-embedding parameters against Qwen1.5-7B's 6.5B, a 75% cut in training cost and 1.74× faster inference [Qwen1.5-MoE blog]. Qwen2-57B-A14B was upscaled from Qwen2-7B: 64 routed experts of width 2,560 with 8 active, plus 8 shared experts (fused into one FFN of width \(8 \times 2560 = 20{,}480\) in the released config), and 4.5T further tokens [Qwen2, Tab. 1].

Upcycling with fine-grained experts
Problem
Training an MoE from scratch is expensive and, in the Qwen team's early experiments, "inefficient and challenging" to bring to peak quality. A dense model already encodes a lot of compute.
Idea
Initialize the MoE from a dense checkpoint, in the sense of sparse upcycling (page 08). Qwen2 replicates the dense FFN \(\lceil n \, h_E / h_{FFN} \rceil\) times, shuffles parameters along the intermediate dimension so copies differ, cuts fine-grained experts out of the copies, and re-initializes 50% of every expert's parameters [Qwen2, §2.2.2].
Why it works
Experts start with useful features instead of noise, and the shuffle plus re-initialization breaks the symmetry that would otherwise keep experts identical. The blog reports that randomness at initialization "significantly expedites convergence" but gives no mechanism.
Evidence
A2.7B: MMLU 62.5 against 61.0 for Qwen1.5-7B [blog]. 57B-A14B: MMLU 76.5 against 74.3 for Qwen1.5-32B and 77.1 for Yi-1.5-34B [Qwen2, Tab. 3]. There is no from-scratch control in either source.
Cost
You need the dense model first, and the MoE inherits its width and tokenizer. Qwen2 itself blames the 57B-A14B's weaker knowledge scores on too few pretraining tokens [Qwen2, §5.2.2].
Used by
Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B, Qwen2-57B-A14B. The Qwen3 report does not say how its MoE models were initialized.

Qwen2.5: data is the lever

Qwen2.5 kept the Qwen2 architecture and moved the data from 7T to 18T tokens [Qwen2.5, §3.1]. Qwen2 had already tried a looser quality threshold that produced 12T tokens and found no significant gain over its 7T set [Qwen2, §3.1], so the extra tokens in Qwen2.5 came from better filtering, not a lower bar. Qwen2-Instruct models score samples across domains, math and code data come from Qwen2.5-Math and Qwen2.5-Coder, synthetic data is filtered by reward models, and over-represented domains (e-commerce, social media, entertainment) are down-sampled while technology and science are up-sampled. The specialists are themselves data engines. Qwen2.5-Math continues pretraining from Qwen2.5 on a 1T-plus-token math corpus, and its 7B base scores 55.4 on MATH, above Qwen2-72B's 51.1 [Qwen2.5-Math, §2, §5.1]. Qwen2.5-Coder continues from Qwen2.5 on 5.5T tokens, and a final mix of 70% code, 20% text and 10% math beat both a code-only mix and a more code-heavy one in the authors' 7B ablation [Qwen2.5-Coder, §3.1.2]. Qwen3 then uses these models to synthesize trillions of pretraining tokens (page 12).

Why Qwen2.5 became the RL default, and the risk in that

By 2025 a large part of the open RLVR literature (page 24) ran on Qwen bases: DAPO trains Qwen2.5-32B, Oat-Zero and SimpleRL-Zero start from Qwen2.5-Math-7B and other Qwen2.5 models, VAPO uses Qwen2.5-32B, and the DeepSeek-R1 report runs its "RL versus distillation" control experiment on Qwen2.5-32B. Shao et al. call Qwen models "the de facto choice for RLVR research in the open-source community" [Spurious Rewards, §1]. That is the sense in which it is "most" of the field, and it is worth knowing why.

Four reasons

  1. Availability across scale. A ladder of open bases from 0.5B to 72B, most under Apache 2.0 [Qwen2.5, Tab. 1], lets a lab debug a recipe at 1.5B and report the headline at 32B with one tokenizer and one chat template.
  2. Strong math and code in the base. Qwen2.5 pretraining includes Qwen2.5-Math and Qwen2.5-Coder data (above), so a base already solves problems that RL only has to sharpen.
  3. Useful reasoning behaviors already present. Under identical PPO on the Countdown game, Qwen2.5-3B reached about 60% while Llama-3.2-3B reached about 30%, because Qwen "naturally exhibits" verification, backtracking, subgoal setting and backward chaining and Llama does not. Priming Llama with traces that contain those behaviors, even incorrect ones, made it match Qwen [Gandhi et al. 2025, §3]. RL amplifies behaviors that occur in successful samples, so a base that already emits them is a better substrate.
  4. Convention. Once DAPO, Dr. GRPO and others published Qwen numbers, comparisons required the same base. This one is our inference, not a documented cause.

The risk: results that may be Qwen-specific

Shao et al. ran GRPO for about 300 steps on Qwen2.5-Math-7B with rewards that carry little or no information. On MATH-500 the gain was +29.1 points with ground-truth rewards, +24.1 with rewards for matching incorrect majority-vote labels and +21.4 with rewards assigned at random. Llama3 and OLMo2 gained little or nothing, and on AIME 2025, whose problems postdate the models, the spurious gains largely vanish [Shao et al. 2025, Abstract, §2-3, App. D]. Their mechanism is a clipping bias in GRPO: without clipping, random rewards give a zero expected gradient, but with it the update raises the probability of tokens the model already favors, amplifying whatever high-prior behavior exists. In Qwen2.5-Math that behavior is "code reasoning" (writing Python-style reasoning without executing it), whose frequency rises from 65% to about 90% under any reward, and answers that use it are correct 60.9% of the time [§4-5].

Wu et al. offer a second, compatible explanation: memorization. Given the first 60% of a MATH-500 problem, Qwen2.5-Math-7B regenerates the remaining 40% with 54.6% exact match and answers 53.6% of the truncated problems correctly, against 3.8% and 2.4% for Llama3.1-8B. On LiveMathBench (a post-cutoff set) its completion rate is 0.0%. On a freshly generated arithmetic benchmark, only correct rewards produced reliable gains and random ones did not [Wu et al. 2025, §1, §4]. They also show that the official chat template lowers a Qwen base's starting accuracy, so part of the "RL gain" is adaptation to the format. That paper is a preprint marked work in progress, and it reports similar memorization for Qwen3 in an appendix. The two accounts are not exclusive (a memorized answer is a high-prior behavior), and neither study proves the other wrong.

A protocol before you trust an RLVR result

(1) Repeat on a second model family. (2) Add random-reward and incorrect-label baselines: if they recover most of the gain, the reward is not the cause. (3) Evaluate on problems the base could not have seen (post-cutoff or generated). (4) Match the prompt template to how the base was pretrained. (5) Report pass@\(k\) at large \(k\) as well as pass@1 (page 24). Qwen's own pipelines are Qwen on Qwen: Qwen3's cold-start queries are filtered by Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, its cold-start responses come from QwQ-32B, and its reference-based judge in general RL is Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct [Qwen3, §4.1, §4.4], so the family's biases can propagate.

From your jet tagger

A tagger method validated on a single Monte Carlo generator's tune can be exploiting that generator's quirks. You would check it on a second generator and on data the tune never saw, and you would run a null control such as shuffled labels. Base-model families play the role of generators, post-cutoff problems the unseen data, and random rewards the shuffled-label control.

Global-batch load balancing

Qwen3 adopts the loss of Qiu et al. (the Qwen team, January 2025) [Qiu et al. 2025]. The standard load-balancing loss for \(E\) experts is \(E \sum_i f_i P_i\), where \(f_i\) is the fraction of assignments to expert \(i\) and \(P_i\) the mean router probability. The trouble is where the mean is taken. Frameworks compute \(f_i\) inside each micro-batch (a few sequences, often from one domain) and average across parallel groups, so the constraint is nearly sequence-level: a code-only micro-batch must still be spread over all experts. The fix is to all-reduce only the \(E\)-sized count vector, and keep the probabilities local:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{micro}} = \frac{1}{N_P}\sum_{j=1}^{N_P} E \sum_i f^{\,j}_i P^{\,j}_i, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{global}} = \frac{1}{N_P}\sum_{j=1}^{N_P} E \sum_i \bar f_i \, P^{\,j}_i, \quad \bar f_i = \frac{\text{assignments to } i \text{ over all groups}}{\text{tokens} \cdot k} \]
\(\bar f\) is a constant (no gradient); gradients still flow through the local \(P^j\)

A two-expert example shows the difference. Micro-batch 0 is all code and every token goes to expert 0; micro-batch 1 is all prose and goes to expert 1. Inside each micro-batch \(f = (1,0)\) and \(P = (1,0)\), so \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{micro}} = 2\), the worst possible value. Globally \(\bar f = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{global}} = \tfrac12(2 \cdot \tfrac12 + 2 \cdot \tfrac12) = 1\), the minimum. The global loss is content with domain-specialized experts as long as the corpus is balanced. This is the first drill (Hands-on).

Global-batch load balancing
Problem
Micro-batch balancing is a near sequence-level constraint that pushes every sequence, whatever its domain, to spread over all experts, which suppresses specialization.
Idea
Synchronize expert selection counts across data-parallel groups (or accumulate them over gradient-accumulation steps with a buffer) before forming \(f\).
Why it works
The constraint moves from "each sequence is balanced" to "the corpus is balanced". A shuffled control that uses a micro-batch-sized random draw of tokens from the global batch recovers almost all of the gain, so what matters is token diversity in the balancing set, not token count.
Evidence
3.4B-A0.6B on 400B tokens: perplexity 7.347 with balance batch size 4, 7.214 at 128 and 7.198 at 512; shuffled control 7.214 against 7.383 for micro-batch. 15B-A2.54B: 5.778 to 5.603. Experts in the math domain reach selection frequencies above 0.2, against below 0.15 with micro-batch balancing [Qiu et al. 2025, Tab. 1-2, §4-5].
Cost
A lopsided micro-batch means a straggler device: 5.8% slower steps on the 43B-A6.6B model, of which about 1% is communication. A micro-batch term at 1% of the global weight cut that to 2.6% at a small quality cost.
Used by
Qwen3 MoE [Qwen3, §2], Qwen3-Next (the blog names "global load balancing"). DeepSeek-V3's batch-wise balancing is the same principle (page 08).

Pretraining: 36T tokens in three stages

Qwen3 doubles Qwen2.5's data to about 36T tokens in 119 languages and dialects (Qwen2.5 covered 29). The additions are PDF-like documents read by Qwen2.5-VL and cleaned by Qwen2.5, and trillions of synthetic tokens (textbooks, Q&A, instructions, code) from Qwen2.5, Qwen2.5-Math and Qwen2.5-Coder. Over 30T tokens are annotated for educational value, domain and safety, and the mixture is optimized per instance with small proxy models rather than per source [Qwen3, §3.1]. Training has three stages: a general stage of over 30T tokens at 4,096 context; a reasoning stage of about 5T tokens with more STEM, code, reasoning and synthetic data and an accelerated learning-rate decay; and a long-context stage of hundreds of billions of tokens at 32,768 (75% of sequences between 16K and 32K), with ABF, YaRN and DCA as above [§3.2]. As in Qwen2.5, scaling laws predict the learning-rate schedule and batch size per model and stage. The data mechanics are on page 12. The headline base-model claim is that Qwen3 MoE bases match Qwen3 dense bases "with only 1/5 activated parameters", and that Qwen3-235B-A22B-Base beats DeepSeek-V3-Base on 14 of 15 benchmarks with about 1/3 of its total and 2/3 of its activated parameters (MMLU 87.81 against 87.19, MATH 71.84 against 62.62) [Qwen3, §3.3, Tab. 3].

Post-training: four stages, one shortcut

The flagship models follow four stages [Qwen3, §4]:

  1. Long-CoT cold start. Queries are filtered with Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct (drop non-verifiable ones and any it answers correctly without chain of thought), \(N\) responses per query come from QwQ-32B, and six response filters apply (wrong answer, repetition, guesswork, thinking/summary mismatch, language mixing, overlap with validation items). The set is kept deliberately small: "it is preferable to minimize both the number of training samples and the training steps."
  2. Reasoning RL. 3,995 query-verifier pairs that are new, learnable, hard and diverse; GRPO with a large batch, many rollouts per query and off-policy updates, controlling entropy to rise steadily or hold. Qwen3-235B-A22B goes from 70.1 to 85.1 on AIME'24 in 170 steps.
  3. Thinking-mode fusion. Continual SFT on the Stage-2 model. Thinking data is rejection-sampled from that same model so its reasoning does not regress, non-thinking data is curated separately, and one chat template carries a /think or /no_think flag (a non-thinking reply keeps an empty think block).
  4. General RL. Over 20 tasks scored by rule-based rewards, a reference-based judge (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct) and a preference reward model, including multi-turn agent rollouts with real tool feedback.

Thinking budget. Once a model can answer with or without thinking it can also answer from partial thinking. To cap the thinking length, the serving code halts generation at a user-set threshold and appends the instruction "Considering the limited time by the user, I have to give the solution based on the thinking directly now.\n</think>.\n\n", after which the model writes its answer. The report stresses that this ability "is not explicitly trained but emerges naturally" from fusion, and shows smooth accuracy gains as the budget grows on four benchmarks [Qwen3, §4.3, §4.7]. It is a 思考预算 (thinking budget) enforced outside the model, not a learned length controller.

On July 21, 2025 the Qwen team wrote: "After talking with the community and thinking it through, we decided to stop using hybrid thinking mode. GSPO: a sequence-level ratio for MoE reinforcement learning

GRPO (page 24) multiplies each token's advantage by a per-token ratio \(w_{i,t} = \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,\lt t}) / \pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,\lt t})\) and clips each token. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), from the Qwen team [Zheng et al. 2025], changes the unit to the whole response:

\[ s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|} = \exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,\lt t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,\lt t})}\Big), \qquad J = \E\Big[\frac1G \sum_{i=1}^{G} \min\big(s_i \hat A_i,\ \clip(s_i, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\, \hat A_i\big)\Big] \]
\(\hat A_i\) is the group-normalized reward, as in GRPO; \(s_i\) is the geometric mean of the token ratios

The argument has four steps.

  1. Token ratios cannot do their job. Importance sampling corrects a distribution mismatch by averaging a weight over many samples. A token-level ratio uses one sampled token from each next-token distribution, so it corrects nothing and instead injects high-variance noise that "accumulates over long sequences and is exacerbated by the clipping mechanism" [GSPO, §3]. Their principle: the unit of the objective should match the unit of the reward, and the reward is per sequence.
  2. The gradient changes. GSPO's gradient is \(\frac1G \sum_i s_i \hat A_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \nabla \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid \cdot)\): every token of a response gets the same weight. In GRPO each token is weighted by its own \(w_{i,t}\), which lies in \((0, 1+\varepsilon]\) for positive advantages and \([1-\varepsilon, \infty)\) for negative ones, and those unequal weights compound over training [§4.2].
  3. MoE makes it worse. In the 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, "roughly 10% of the experts activated under the new policy are different from those under the old policy" for the same rollout after every gradient update. Routing flips make token likelihoods jump, so token ratios fluctuate wildly. Qwen's earlier fix was Routing Replay: cache the experts chosen under \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) and force them under \(\pi_\theta\), at a cost in memory and communication and a cap on model capacity [§5.3]. A sequence likelihood is much steadier than any single token's, because the model keeps its language-modeling ability even when individual routes change, so GSPO trains MoE models without replay.
  4. The scale of \(\varepsilon\) changes. The paper uses clipping ranges of \(3 \times 10^{-4}\) (left) and \(4 \times 10^{-4}\) (right) for GSPO against 0.2 and 0.27 for its tuned GRPO baseline, and the average fraction of clipped tokens is 0.0013 for GSPO against 0.15 for GRPO, two orders of magnitude apart. GSPO clips far more tokens (a whole response at a time) and still learns faster per unit of compute, which they read as evidence that token-level gradients were noisy [§5.1-5.2].

What \(\varepsilon = 4 \times 10^{-4}\) means. \(s_i^{|y_i|}\) is the ratio of full-sequence probabilities. For a 1,000-token response, \(s_i = 1 + 4 \times 10^{-4}\) corresponds to \((1 + 4 \times 10^{-4})^{1000} = e^{0.4} \approx 1.49\): the response is clipped once the new policy assigns it about 49% more probability than the old one, which is a mean per-token log-ratio of only 0.0004. Per-token GRPO clipping is loose by comparison: a 0.1% change on every token of the same response compounds to \(1.001^{1000} \approx 2.7\) on the sequence, yet no token comes near the 0.8 to 1.27 clip band.

The same paper argues an infrastructure benefit: sequence likelihoods tolerate the numerical gap between training and inference engines, so the engine's log-probabilities could be used without recomputation, which matters for partial rollouts and multi-turn RL [§5.4] (page 25). A token-level variant, GSPO-token, keeps the same numerical ratio but lets each token have its own advantage for multi-turn credit assignment. The clipping ranges, the ratio computation and the loss are implemented in the grpo drill of page 24, so this page does not repeat them.

Idea

Gated attention: the mechanism Qwen3-Next ships

Softmax attention cannot "do nothing": every row sums to 1, so a head with nothing useful to say parks its mass on a sink token, and the network learns huge activations to make that stable. The Qwen team asked whether a gate fixes this and compared 30 variants on 15B-A2.54B MoE and 1.7B dense models trained on up to 3.5T tokens [Qiu et al. 2025]. The winner multiplies the attention output of each head, before the output projection \(W_O\), by an elementwise sigmoid gate computed from the layer's own pre-normed hidden state \(X\) (position G1, page 11 has the full study):

\[ Y' = Y \odot \sigma(X W_\theta), \qquad Y \in \R^{T \times n_h \times d_h}, \quad W_\theta \in \R^{d \times n_h d_h} \]

On the 15B MoE at 400B tokens, perplexity falls from 6.026 to 5.761 and MMLU rises from 58.79 to 60.82, for 201M extra parameters (1.3%) and under 2% wall time; a headwise gate (one scalar per head) costs 1.6M parameters and reaches 5.792. Parameter-matched controls do worse: 8 KV heads (+50M) gives 5.979, 48 query heads (+201M) 5.953 and four more experts (+400M) 5.964. The mean share of attention that the first token receives drops from 46.7% to 4.8%, and after YaRN extension to 128K the RULER score rises from 31.65 to 58.82 [Tab. 1, Fig. 2, Tab. 5]. The authors' two explanations are non-linearity (per head, \(W_V W_O\) is one low-rank linear map, and the gate puts a non-linearity in between) and sparsity (the gates are small and query-dependent, so a head can silence itself).

Qwen3 itself, released two weeks before that paper appeared, uses QK-norm and no gate. Qwen3-Next is the first shipped use. Its checkpoint has a query projection of \(8192 \times 2048\) for 16 heads of 256, that is \(2 \cdot 16 \cdot 256\), and in the Hugging Face implementation that projection is split per head into a query and a gate, and the head outputs are multiplied by \(\sigma(\text{gate})\) before o_proj (modeling_qwen3_next.py). The gate's weights are stored fused with the query projection (one matrix of twice the width), which is the same computation as the paper's separate \(W_\theta\) applied to the same pre-normed input. The Gated DeltaNet layers have their own output gate, an RMSNorm gated by SiLU as in the original Gated DeltaNet. The Qwen3.8-Next design paper replaces it with a bounded sigmoid gate, \(o_t = W_o[\sigma(W_z x_t) \odot \RMSNorm(y_t)]\), and reports consistent improvements [Qwen3.8-Next design paper, Eq. 11]. The Qwen3-Next blog says the output gate removes attention sinks and massive activations; the measurements behind that statement are those of the 2025 paper, not repeated for Qwen3-Next itself.

Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B is billed as the first installment of a series that tests architecture for training and inference efficiency at long context. It combines four ideas: a 3:1 hybrid of Gated DeltaNet and gated softmax attention (page 09 derives the delta rule and the chunkwise algorithm, which this page does not repeat), a much sparser MoE, stability changes to the normalization and router, and multi-token prediction (MTP, page 11) [model card]. It was pretrained on 15T tokens, a subset of Qwen3's 36T corpus according to the announcement [Qwen3-Next blog].

Why each choice

3:1 Gated DeltaNet to gated attention
Problem
Softmax attention costs \(O(T^2)\) FLOPs and a KV cache that grows with \(T\); agentic and long-document workloads made both the binding constraint. Pure linear layers keep a fixed state but cannot retrieve exact tokens.
Idea
Keep three linear-time Gated DeltaNet layers per softmax layer (75% to 25%), so the model has a compact running memory and periodic exact lookup. The 25% layer gets a wider head (128 to 256), partial RoPE and the output gate.
Why it works
The delta rule writes a key-value pair by first erasing what the state already predicts for that key, so a fixed matrix state behaves like an associative memory with overwrite, not a running sum (page 09). The remaining softmax layers supply what a finite state cannot: exact recall of any earlier token. The blog reports that Gated DeltaNet beats sliding-window attention and Mamba2 in in-context learning.
Evidence
A later Qwen study trains three 28-layer 25B-A3B MoE models (400B tokens at 4K, then 80B at 32K) and reports averages over nine benchmarks of 49.87 for full attention, 51.15 for a window-128 SWA hybrid and 53.81 for the GDN hybrid. The GDN hybrid beats full attention on 8 of 9 benchmarks and SWA on 7 of 9, and it loses on EvalPlus (49.71 against 51.01 and 52.12) [Qwen3.8-Next design paper, Tab. 1]. The same paper finds that removing RoPE from the full-attention layers (NoPE) looks identical in pretraining but produces a "substantially higher rate of endless generation after post-training".
Cost
A fixed state cannot store more than its capacity, so retrieval-heavy tasks must route through the 12 softmax layers. New kernels (chunkwise recurrence), state precision (fp32) and prefix-caching design are needed, and the benefit appears only at long \(T\) (see below). One lab's ablation at 25B-A3B and 480B tokens is not evidence at 1M tokens (page 09 lists conflicting results from MiniMax and GLM).
Used by
Qwen3-Next, Qwen3-Coder-Next, Qwen3.5, Qwen3.6, Qwen3.8 (all 3:1). Kimi Linear uses the same ratio with a different linear layer.
Ultra-sparse MoE, stable normalization, MTP
Problem
At long context the cost of an MoE model is attention plus the cost of moving weights; the FFN's FLOPs per token can be cut further by activating a smaller fraction of a bigger expert pool.
Idea
512 routed experts of width 512 with 10 active and 1 shared, that is 2% of routed experts against 6.25% in Qwen3-235B-A22B (8 of 128) and 3.1% in DeepSeek-V3 (8 of 256). The number of possible expert sets grows from \(\binom{128}{8} \approx 10^{12}\) to \(\binom{512}{10} \approx 10^{20.5}\). Unlike Qwen3, it brings back a shared expert.
Why it works
Same argument as fine-grained experts (page 08): finer experts give more specialization per activated FLOP, and global-batch balancing keeps them used. Stability changes follow: RMSNorm gains are applied as \(1 + w\) with \(w\) initialized to zero ("zero-centered", checked in the Hugging Face implementation) and weight-decayed, "to constrain the growth of RMSNorm weights" [Qwen3.8-Next design paper, §2.1.1], router weights are normalized at initialization so early expert selection is unbiased, and the output gate removes sinks and massive activations (Qwen blog). MTP is trained in several steps so training matches inference, giving a high acceptance rate for speculative decoding.
Evidence
The sources we read give no ablation of the expert count, the zero-centered norm or the router initialization, only the end result: the base model "outperforms Qwen3-32B-Base on downstream tasks with 10% of the total training cost", and the instruct model is "on par with Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 on certain benchmarks" (for example AIME25 69.5 against 70.3, LiveCodeBench v6 56.6 against 51.8, MMLU-Pro 80.6 against 83.0) [model card].

All 80B parameters stay resident (about 160 GB in bf16) although about 3B are used per token; each 512-wide expert is a small GEMM; every token fans out to 11 experts, which stresses all-to-all communication and balancing.
Used by
Qwen3-Next, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (512 experts, 10 + 1), Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B (512, 10 + 1). The smaller Qwen3.5 and 3.6 models use 256 experts with 8 + 1.

Trade-offs to state in an interview

  • Recall versus memory. The 36 linear layers hold \(32 \cdot 128 \cdot 128\) numbers each regardless of length; exact multi-needle retrieval must come from the 12 softmax layers. The design bets that a 25% softmax budget is enough, and the evidence at 1M tokens is mixed (page 09).
  • Sparsity versus systems. 3B active parameters do not mean 3B of memory traffic: a batch of decode tokens touches most experts, so the weights are read from HBM almost in full. Expert parallelism and balance matter more, not less.
  • Infrastructure risk. Recurrent state complicates prefix caching, speculative decoding and precision. Qwen3-Next ships MTP partly to offset decode cost.
  • Position encoding. RoPE is kept on 25% of head dimensions in the softmax layers, and the Qwen ablation warns against removing it.

Where it went. Qwen3-Coder-Next (80B-A3B, released as weights on January 30, 2026 and described in a February 28 report) starts from the Qwen3-Next base, continues pretraining on code and agent data with the context extended from 32,768 to 262,144 tokens, then trains specialist experts (web development, UX, single-turn RL, software engineering) and distills them into one model [Qwen3-Coder-Next, §3-4]. The rest of the family followed the same layout, as the next section shows.

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next: the announced preview of "Qwen4"

The Flash-Next card calls the model an "experimental preview of the architecture that will underpin Qwen4", and the design paper (August 2026) documents its four changes [Qwen3.8-Next design paper]:

  • Qwen Sparse Attention (QSA). The softmax layers become sparse at continued pretraining (256K sequences): a compressed indexer average-pools keys in blocks of \(r = 4\) tokens and each query keeps its top 512 blocks (2,048 tokens). Training has a dense-distillation warm-up (about 2B tokens) and about 200B tokens of sparse training, with loss within about \(10^{-4}\) of full attention. At 1M tokens the kernels are 7.6× faster in prefill and 4.9× in decode (page 09).
  • Gated Residual. The residual stream is widened to four branches, read through an elementwise gate and written through a per-branch scalar gate. An isolated ablation identifies the gate as a key contributor to stability under four times the optimal learning rate.
  • N-gram embeddings. A single table of 20M bigram and trigram entries, injected at layer 2, adds 51B parameters held in host memory and prefetched, so parameter count grows with negligible FLOPs. The paper finds that loss falls monotonically with table size while downstream accuracy saturates.
  • Optimizer recipe. Muon (8 Newton-Schulz steps) for 2D linear maps and AdamW for embeddings, output head, router and the low-rank gate projections; a refit hyperparameter scaling law that predicts larger batches and learning rates; no batch-size warmup (it would cost 18.8% more optimizer steps); and a full-scale run reported to have had no loss spike and no need for qk-clip or SwiGLU-clip.

The headline is self-reported: on 14 pretraining benchmarks the base model leads the 397B-A17B model on 8 and trails it by at most 2.6 points on the rest, with one third of the activated parameters, one third of the tokens and roughly one ninth of the training FLOPs [Abstract]. Treat it as a strong claim from one lab, awaiting independent replication, and note the pattern across the whole page: Qwen's architecture changes since Qwen3-Next were introduced mainly to make long-context and agentic workloads cheaper, and each was justified by the lab's own ablations.

Read the rows as bets. The evidence on the two most debated rows is thin on both sides:

  • Shared experts. DeepSeekMoE's 2B-parameter ablation raised loss from 1.808 to 2.414 without the shared expert, while Qwen3 dropped it with no ablation in its report, and Qwen3-Next brought one back, scaled by a learned scalar gate (page 08). The convergence is the more useful fact: both labs ended up with fine-grained sparse FFNs of about 8 to 10 active experts, batch-level balancing, and long-context attention that reads or stores less than full softmax attention does. The two paths differ in what they keep exact: DeepSeek keeps every token but selects or compresses, while Qwen replaces most layers with a lossy fixed-size memory and keeps a few exact layers.

Common misconceptions

  • "Qwen3 is a hybrid-attention model." Qwen3 (April 2025) is a standard GQA plus QK-norm Transformer with an MoE variant. The Gated DeltaNet hybrid arrived with Qwen3-Next in September 2025 and became the family default only from Qwen3.5.
  • "Qwen3 uses gated attention." It uses QK-norm. Gated attention (a sigmoid gate on each head's output) first ships in Qwen3-Next. It is also not the Gated DeltaNet gates, which control decay and writes of the recurrent state.
  • "Activated parameters are comparable across model cards." The 22B of Qwen3-235B-A22B counts the embedding and the LM head (22.19B), while Qwen3-Next's 3B matches the non-embedding count (3.25B; 3.87B with both). Recompute from the config.
  • "Global-batch balancing is one global auxiliary loss." Only the expert counts are synchronized (an \(E\)-sized all-reduce). The probabilities stay local and are the only path for gradients.
  • "GSPO is GRPO with a sequence-level advantage." GRPO's advantage is already per sequence. GSPO changes the importance ratio and the clipping unit to the length-normalized sequence ratio, at a different scale of \(\varepsilon\).
  • "The thinking budget is a trained length controller." It is a forced stop: the serving code inserts a fixed sentence and the closing think tag at a threshold. The ability emerges from fusing the two modes, and Qwen's 2507 models then dropped the fusion.
  • "Large RL gains on Qwen bases show that RL teaches reasoning." Random and incorrect rewards give most of the MATH-500 gain on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, and the base shows signs of memorizing the benchmark. Test on another family and on post-cutoff problems.
  • "A 3:1 hybrid cuts memory 4×." The KV cache shrinks with the share of softmax layers, and Qwen3-Next's cache is 10.7× smaller than Qwen3-32B's because it also uses fewer, wider-headed KV heads. The recurrent state is a fixed cost that equals the cache at about 3K tokens, so short sequences see no saving.

Interview questions

Warm-up What changed in the attention block between Qwen2.5 and Qwen3, and why?

GQA, RoPE, pre-norm RMSNorm and SwiGLU stay. Qwen3 removes the QKV bias that Qwen introduced in 2023 "to enhance extrapolation", and adds QK-norm (an RMSNorm on each head's query and key over \(d_h = 128\), before RoPE) "to ensure stable training". The reports give no ablation. The plausible story is a change of priorities: extrapolation mattered when training contexts were 2K to 8K, and by 2025 the risk was logit growth and loss spikes over 36T tokens, which QK-norm bounds directly (page 11). Qwen3 also fixes \(d_h = 128\) at every size, so \(n_h d_h\) can exceed \(d\) (64 × 128 = 8192 = 2\(d\) in the 235B).

Warm-up Compute the KV cache per token of Qwen3-32B, and for one 128K-token sequence. How does Qwen3-235B-A22B compare?

Qwen3-32B has 64 layers, 8 KV heads and \(d_h = 128\): \(64 \cdot 2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2\ \text{B} = 262{,}144\) B = 256 KiB per token, so \(256\text{ KiB} \times 131{,}072 = 32\) GiB. Qwen3-235B-A22B has 94 layers but 4 KV heads: \(94 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 192{,}512\) B = 188 KiB, or 23.5 GiB. The bigger model has the smaller cache, because its KV heads are half as many and that outweighs 30 more layers.

Warm-up What is Qwen3's thinking budget, and how is it enforced?

One checkpoint answers in thinking mode (<think>...</think> then the reply) or non-thinking mode (an empty think block), switched by /think and /no_think flags or a template option. For a budget, the serving code stops generation when the thinking reaches the threshold, appends "Considering the limited time by the user, I have to give the solution based on the thinking directly now." plus the closing tag, and lets the model write its answer from the partial reasoning. Qwen says this ability "is not explicitly trained but emerges naturally" from thinking-mode fusion, and accuracy rises smoothly with the budget on four benchmarks. It is external control, not a learned length policy.

Core What architectural choices distinguish Qwen3's MoE from DeepSeek-V3's?

Qwen3-235B-A22B: 128 routed experts of width 1536, top-8, no shared expert, MoE in every layer, softmax routing with top-8 weights renormalized, a global-batch auxiliary loss, and GQA (4 KV heads) with QK-norm. DeepSeek-V3: 256 routed experts of width 2048 plus 1 shared expert, top-8, first 3 layers dense, sigmoid scores with a routed scaling factor of 2.5, bias-based auxiliary-loss-free balancing (plus a small sequence-wise loss), and MLA. Beyond the MoE: V3 has an MTP module, Qwen3 does not.

Why they differ: Qwen favors standard components that run on stock kernels and puts its novelty into data (36T tokens) and RL. DeepSeek co-designs with hardware. What they share: fine-grained top-8 experts and balancing over the whole batch, not per sequence. Evidence is thin on shared experts: DeepSeekMoE's 2B ablation raised loss from 1.808 to 2.414 without one, Qwen3 dropped it with no ablation, and Qwen3-Next brought one back. Cache: 188 KiB per token for Qwen3-235B against 68.6 KiB for V3's MLA.

Core Why did Qwen introduce GSPO?

GRPO's per-token ratio \(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}}\) is computed from one sampled token per position, so it cannot do the distribution correction that importance sampling needs. It adds variance that grows with length and is amplified by clipping, and it weights the tokens of one response unequally. In MoE models it is worse: after each gradient update roughly 10% of the activated experts change for the same rollout (Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base), so token likelihoods jump. GRPO then needed Routing Replay, which costs memory and communication and constrains capacity.

GSPO uses \(s_i = (\pi_\theta(y_i)/\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i))^{1/|y_i|}\), clips whole responses at about \(3\text{-}4 \times 10^{-4}\) and weights every token equally in the gradient. Sequence likelihood is stable under route flips, so no replay is needed. Reported: about 0.13% of tokens clipped against 15% for GRPO, yet better reward per unit of compute. Caveats: evidence from Qwen's own runs, coarser credit assignment (GSPO-token restores per-token advantages), and a new \(\varepsilon\) scale to tune.

Core What is gated attention and what does it fix?

A sigmoid gate multiplies each head's attention output before \(W_O\): \(Y' = Y \odot \sigma(X W_\theta)\) with \(X\) the layer's pre-normed input (elementwise, head-specific in the best variant). Softmax cannot output "nothing", so heads dump mass on a sink token and the network builds massive activations to support it. The gate lets a head emit near zero for an irrelevant query. On a 15B-A2.54B MoE at 400B tokens: perplexity 6.026 to 5.761, MMLU +2.0, with 201M added parameters (1.3%) and under 2% latency; first-token attention 46.7% to 4.8%; stable at larger learning rates; RULER at 128K after YaRN 31.65 to 58.82. Parameter-matched controls (more KV heads, more query heads, more experts) did worse. Two explanations: non-linearity between the low-rank \(W_V W_O\), and query-dependent sparsity. It is used in Qwen3-Next's softmax layers. Caveat: one team's study, not compared with learned sink logits.

Core Describe Qwen3-Next's hybrid design and its trade-offs.

48 layers in 12 blocks of three Gated DeltaNet layers and one gated softmax layer, each followed by an MoE block of 512 experts (10 routed plus 1 shared, width 512). Softmax layers have 16 Q heads, 2 KV heads, \(d_h = 256\), partial RoPE on 25% of dimensions. Delta-rule layers keep a fixed state (32 heads of 128 × 128 per layer) that erases what it already predicts before writing, so it acts as an overwriting associative memory.

Numbers: KV cache \(12 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 256 \cdot 2 = 24{,}576\) B = 24 KiB per token, so 3 GiB at 128K against 32 GiB for Qwen3-32B (10.7×), plus a 72 MiB fp32 state that equals the cache at 3,072 tokens. Trade-offs: a finite state cannot recall arbitrary tokens, so exact retrieval depends on the 12 softmax layers; new kernels, state precision and prefix-caching needs; the saving is a long-context effect; and with only 2 KV heads, tensor parallelism above 2 replicates the cache (page 07). Evidence: a 25B-A3B ablation averaging 53.81 for the GDN hybrid against 49.87 (full) and 51.15 (SWA hybrid) but worse on EvalPlus; throughput multiples (7× prefill at 4K, over 10× beyond 32K) are vendor claims.

Core Why are Qwen bases so common in RL research, and what is the risk in that?

Reasons: open weights across 0.5B to 72B (mostly Apache 2.0) with one tokenizer; strong math and code from pretraining data built with Qwen2.5-Math and -Coder; and pre-existing reasoning behaviors. Under identical PPO on Countdown, Qwen2.5-3B reached about 60% and Llama-3.2-3B about 30%, because Qwen already shows verification and backtracking and RL amplifies behaviors that appear in successful samples. Convention then locks it in.

Risk: findings may be Qwen-specific. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, GRPO with random rewards raised MATH-500 by 21.4 points against 29.1 for ground truth, and with incorrect labels by 24.1; Llama3 and OLMo2 did not gain. One account is clipping bias amplifying high-prior behavior (code reasoning 65% to 90%); another is memorization (54.6% exact-match completion of MATH-500 from a 60% prefix). Response: replicate on a second family, include random-reward baselines, use post-cutoff or generated problems, match templates, report pass@\(k\).

Core Explain the global-batch load-balancing loss. What does it change and what does it cost?

Standard loss: \(E \sum_i f_i P_i\), with \(f_i\) the assignment fraction and \(P_i\) the mean router probability. Frameworks compute it per micro-batch and average, which acts almost at the sequence level and forces even a code-only micro-batch to use all experts. Qiu et al. all-reduce the \(E\)-sized count vector so \(\bar f\) reflects the global batch, keep \(P^j\) local, and average \(E\sum_i \bar f_i P^j_i\) over groups (gradient only through \(P^j\); a buffer over gradient-accumulation steps approximates the global batch). Example: two domain-specific micro-batches, each collapsed onto one of two experts, give a micro loss of 2 and a global loss of 1.

Evidence: perplexity 7.347 to 7.198 (3.4B-A0.6B, 400B tokens) and 5.778 to 5.603 (15B-A2.54B); a shuffled control shows token diversity, not token count, matters. Cost: stragglers, 5.8% slower steps in one setting, reduced to 2.6% by a small micro-batch term.

Core Calculate the total and activated parameters of Qwen3-235B-A22B from its config.

Config: \(L = 94\), \(d = 4096\), 64 Q and 4 KV heads of 128, expert width 1536, 128 experts, top-8, \(V = 151{,}936\), untied. Per layer: attention \(4096 \cdot 8192 + 2 \cdot 4096 \cdot 512 + 8192 \cdot 4096 = 71{,}303{,}168\); router \(4096 \cdot 128 = 524{,}288\); experts \(128 \cdot 3 \cdot 4096 \cdot 1536 = 2{,}415{,}919{,}104\); plus small norms. Times 94, plus \(2 \cdot 151{,}936 \cdot 4096 = 1{,}244{,}659{,}712\) for embedding and head, gives 235,093,634,560 (equal to the checkpoint). Active: replace 128 experts by 8, giving 22.19B with embedding and head, 21.57B with only the head, 20.95B without either. The name's "22B" matches the first. Attention is 2.85% of the total but 32% of the non-embedding active weights.

Deep-dive Design question: you want one model that can both answer instantly and think for a long time. What did Qwen3 do, what went wrong, and what would you do?

Qwen3 trained a long-CoT cold start, then reasoning RL (3,995 pairs, GRPO), then fused non-thinking data into the RL model by SFT (thinking data rejection-sampled from the same model, one template with /think and /no_think), then general RL over 20+ tasks. Mode switching improved (ThinkFollow 88.7 to 98.9) and general scores rose (Arena-Hard 86.8 to 93.8), but Qwen3-32B's thinking-mode AIME'24 fell from 83.8 to 81.4 and LiveCodeBench from 68.4 to 65.7, and Qwen accepted the trade. In July 2025 it stopped fusing and shipped Instruct and Thinking checkpoints, then in 2026 cards returned to one checkpoint with reasoning_effort.

My plan: treat it as a routing or control problem, not a weights problem. Options: separate checkpoints and a router (best peak, double serving cost); fusion with effort control (one model, some regression); or specialists plus on-policy distillation (DeepSeek-V4 style, page 19). Measure the regression on your hardest reasoning benchmarks, the length distribution and instruction following, and keep an in-mode compliance metric like ThinkFollow.

Deep-dive Random rewards raise Qwen2.5-Math-7B's MATH-500 accuracy by about 21 points. What do you conclude, and which experiments would you run?

Conclude nothing about the reward. Separate four hypotheses. (1) Format or template adaptation: compare with the base's best greedy score without the chat template, since the template lowers Qwen base accuracy and the "gain" may only return to it. (2) Clipping bias amplifying priors: repeat with clipping disabled (one gradient step per rollout); the expected update is then zero, so gains should vanish. Track the frequency of the high-prior behavior (code reasoning). (3) Memorization: partial-prompt completion on MATH-500 and on a post-cutoff set, and training on freshly generated problems, where only correct rewards should help. (4) Model specificity: repeat on Llama and OLMo. Add pass@\(k\) at large \(k\) to see whether the model sharpens or expands. Report all with error bars over seeds, since 300-step GRPO runs are noisy. Only if random rewards fail on clean data and on other families can you say the ground-truth reward is what teaches the skill.

Deep-dive Trade-off: you must run stable RL on a 30B-A3B MoE. Compare token-level GRPO with Routing Replay, engine-routing replay, and GSPO.

Problem size: ~10% of activated experts change after one update in Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, and between an inference engine and a trainer about 10% of router decisions and 94% of tokens (at least one layer) differ (page 25). Routing Replay (train-side) fixes update-induced flips, costs memory and communication for cached routes, caps capacity and does not fix engine mismatch. Engine-routing replay also fixes mismatch, at a small logging cost, but biases the target policy and did not help on-policy in that study. GSPO needs no replay because sequence likelihood is stable, tolerates numeric mismatch, and uses engine log-probs directly, but gives one ratio per response, requires re-tuning \(\varepsilon\) near \(10^{-4}\) and has evidence mostly from Qwen. I would start with GSPO (or its token variant if multi-turn credit matters), monitor the clipped fraction (order 0.1% in the paper), the train-inference KL and the expert-flip rate, and add a replay method only if the flip rate still correlates with instability.

Deep-dive Qwen3-Next activates 10 of 512 experts and caches 24 KiB per token. What limits going sparser, and how do the two choices interact at serving time?

Limits on sparsity: all 80B parameters stay resident (about 160 GB in bf16); each 512-wide expert is a small GEMM; a token fans out to 11 experts, so all-to-all traffic and balance dominate; and the sources give no ablation of expert count. At decode, a batch touches most experts, so weight reads approach the full model.

Interaction: the small cache lets more sequences share a node, and a bigger batch amortizes expert reads, which is exactly what sparse MoE needs. Idealized 8 × 80 GB and 128K contexts, ignoring activations: Qwen3-Next has about 481 GB free after ~159 GB of weights, and at 3.07 GiB per sequence that is about 145 sequences; Qwen3-32B has about 574 GB free after 65.5 GB of weights, and at 32 GiB per sequence about 16. Caveat: with 2 KV heads, 8-way tensor parallelism replicates each KV head 4×, so real deployments use small attention TP or data-parallel attention (page 07), which shrinks the advantage.
