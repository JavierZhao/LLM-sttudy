Mistral, Gemma, gpt-oss

P1 ~45 min Prereqs: 07 GQA/MQA, 08 MoE, 09 Windows and sinks, 11 Stability tweaks

Three families made three different bets against the same budget: KV-cache bytes per token, FLOPs per token, and stability at scale. Mistral showed how far a sliding window and an open sparse MoE go, then used latent attention in its newer MoEs. Gemma turned local/global layering, QK-norm and distillation into a small-model recipe. OpenAI's gpt-oss packaged banded attention with learned sinks, a 128-expert MoE and 4-bit expert weights so a 117B-parameter model fits on one 80 GB GPU. This page tells each lineage with its reasons and links back to the mechanism pages instead of re-deriving them.

Mistral: cheap inference first, then open MoE, then latent attention in its MoEs

Mistral 7B: a Llama-shaped model with two inference-side changes

Mistral 7B is a Llama-style decoder (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE) with \(d = 4096\), 32 layers, 32 query and 8 KV heads of dimension 128, FFN width 14,336 and a 32,000-token vocabulary [Mistral 7B, Tab. 1], 7.24B parameters by my count. The paper's contribution is two changes that lower the cost of serving. GQA with 8 KV heads cuts the cache 4× against MHA (page 07). Sliding window attention 滑动窗口注意力 bounds what each layer reads and caches [Mistral 7B, §1-2].

Receptive field. A state at layer \(k\) attends to the previous layer's states at positions \(i-W\) to \(i\), and those states already summarize their own windows. Information therefore moves up to \(W\) positions per layer and, after \(k\) layers, up to \(k \cdot W\) tokens. With \(W = 4096\) and 32 layers the paper quotes "a theoretical attention span of approximately 131K tokens" (\(32 \times 4096 = 131{,}072\)). A rolling buffer of \(W\) entries includes the current token, so the reach is \(W-1\) per layer (131,040 after 32 layers), an immaterial difference from the paper's \(k \cdot W\). This receptive field 感受野 is a bound, not a retrieval range: Table 1 lists an 8,192-token context, the paper reports no long-context retrieval test for the 7B, and information passed through many layers is diluted (page 09).

Rolling buffer. No layer reads further back than \(W\), so the cache has a fixed size of \(W\) entries per layer, with position \(i\) written to slot \(i \bmod W\) over the entry that just left the window. This rolling buffer cache 滚动缓冲区缓存 cuts memory on a 32K sequence 8× "without impacting the model quality" [Mistral 7B, §2] (\(32768/4096 = 8\)). RoPE makes it easy: keys are rotated by their absolute position when written and scores depend only on differences, so the buffer stays in slot order. Long prompts are pre-filled in chunks of size \(W\): each chunk attends to itself causally and to the cache through the window (Fig. 3 of the paper), the end-aligned banded mask of the drill below and the ancestor of chunked prefill in serving (page 17).

class RollingKV:
    """Per-layer cache of the last W tokens (keys are stored already RoPE-rotated)."""
    def __init__(self, W, n_kv, d_h):
        self.W, self.n = W, 0
        self.k = torch.zeros(W, n_kv, d_h)
        self.v = torch.zeros(W, n_kv, d_h)

    def append(self, k_t, v_t):                  # (n_kv, d_h) each, for absolute position self.n
        slot = self.n % self.W                   # overwrite the entry that just left the window
        self.k[slot], self.v[slot] = k_t, v_t
        self.n += 1

    def visible(self):                           # softmax is order-invariant, so slot order is fine
        m = min(self.n, self.W)
        return self.k[:m], self.v[:m]
Sliding window with a rolling buffer (Mistral 7B)
Problem
Full attention makes prefill cost grow as \(T^2\) and the cache as \(T\). At 128 KiB per token (GQA-8, bf16) long sequences limit batch size.
Idea
Each layer attends to the last \(W = 4096\) tokens, the cache is a ring buffer of \(W\) entries, and prompts are pre-filled in chunks of \(W\).
Why it works
Most of what a language model needs is local, and stacked layers pass information forward \(W - 1\) positions per layer, so depth restores some long-range reach. Decode cost per token is constant in \(T\).
Evidence
2× speed at 16K, cache 8× smaller at 32K, "without impacting the model quality" [Mistral 7B, §2]. The paper has no window ablation and no long-context retrieval test.
Cost
The 131K span is a bound, and exact retrieval beyond \(W\) depends on depth. A ring buffer discards the early sequence, which conflicts with sharing a cached prefix across requests (page 17).
Used by
Mistral 7B v0.1. Hybrids that keep a few global layers: Gemma 2, 3, 4 and gpt-oss (below).

Worked number. One token costs \(32 \cdot 2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 131{,}072\) B = 128 KiB (page 07). The rolling buffer holds \(4096 \times 128\) KiB = 512 MiB per sequence, flat for any \(T \ge 4096\), against 4 GiB for a full cache at 32K (8×) and 16 GiB at 128K (32×).

Mixtral: the same backbone with a sparse FFN

Mixtral 8x7B keeps Mistral 7B's attention (GQA, no window) and replaces every FFN with eight SwiGLU experts 混合专家 of width 14,336. A linear router picks two per token, mixed by a softmax over the two selected logits [Mixtral, §2.1]:

\[ y = \sum_{i=0}^{7} \operatorname{Softmax}\!\big(\operatorname{Top2}(x W_g)\big)_i \cdot \operatorname{SwiGLU}_i(x) \]

It has 47B parameters, 13B active (46.70B and 12.88B by my count, page 08), and a dense 32K context. Against Llama 2 70B, with 5× more active parameters, the authors' own re-runs give MMLU 70.6 vs 69.9, HumanEval 40.2 vs 29.3, MATH 28.4 vs 13.8 and GSM8K 74.4 vs 69.6 [Mixtral, Tab. 2]. Mixtral 8x22B scales the recipe: 56 layers, \(d = 6144\), 48 query and 8 KV heads, expert width 16,384, 64K context, 141B total and 39B active [Mistral, 17 Apr 2024]. The config gives 140.62B and 39.15B by my count.

The routing analysis (§5) corrects a common belief. On subsets of The Pile, the distribution of selected experts at layers 0, 15 and 31 is "very similar" for ArXiv, PubMed abstracts and PhilPapers: no obvious topic specialization (only DM Mathematics differs a little). What the router does track is syntax: 'self' in Python, 'Question' in English and indentation tokens go to consistent experts. Consecutive tokens also repeat their first-choice expert far above the 12.5% chance level: 13.6 to 14.9% at layer 0, 23.6 to 28.4% at layer 15 and 19.7 to 26.3% at layer 31 across domains [Mixtral, §5, Tab. 5]. The authors' consequences: temporal locality over-subscribes some experts under expert parallelism, and it can be exploited for caching or offloading.

Coarse open top-2 MoE (Mixtral)
Problem
Dense 70B quality costs 70B parameters of compute per token. The target was serving cost.
Idea
Eight full-size FFN experts per layer, two active per token, everything else as in Mistral 7B.
Why it works
Capacity (47B) grows with the number of experts, per-token FLOPs only with \(k = 2\) (13B active). The router needs no topic structure; it learns token-level and syntactic patterns.
Evidence
Matches or beats Llama 2 70B and GPT-3.5 on most benchmarks with 5× fewer active parameters [Mixtral, Tab. 2-3].
Cost
All 47B parameters must be resident (93.4 GB in bf16, more than one 80 GB GPU), and at large batch nearly every expert is read (page 08). Eight coarse experts give fewer routing combinations than DeepSeekMoE (page 26). The paper gives no token count and does not say whether experts were initialized from the dense model.
Used by
Mixtral 8x7B and 8x22B. Mistral's later MoEs use 128 experts (below).

Magistral: a reasoning model from a home-built RL stack

The one recent Mistral paper with method detail is Magistral (June 2025). Magistral Medium is trained on Mistral Medium 3 "with RL alone", with no distillation from a reasoning model, and lifts AIME-24 pass@1 from 26.8 to 73.6. Magistral Small (24B, Apache 2.0) starts from SFT on Medium's traces and then runs RL: 70.7, against 65.4 for SFT alone and 65.8 for RL alone [Magistral, Tab. 2-3]. Gemma: Gemini's recipe, distilled into small open models

Gemma is Google DeepMind's line of open models "built from the research and technology used to create Gemini" [Gemma, abstract]. Habits that recur: a large Gemini vocabulary (256K, 262K from Gemma 3), a head dimension of 256 or 128 (512 in Gemma 4's global layers) decoupled from \(d/n_h\), and tied embeddings (stated for Gemma 1 and 2, configured for 4) with the input scaled by \(\sqrt{d}\). The scaling is in the Hugging Face code of Gemma 1, 2 and 3 (embed_scale = hidden_size ** 0.5): the "keep the shortcut large" trick of page 11, since a tied table is trained at the scale the output logits need. What changed between generations is mostly attention.

Knowledge distillation as a way to buy tokens

The report's diagnosis: small models are still under-trained, but training longer "only scales logarithmically with dataset size", and the latest small models need up to 15T tokens for gains of under 1 to 2% [Gemma 2, §1]. So change the target instead. With a larger teacher, the student minimizes \(\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{x} P_T(x \mid x_c)\log P_S(x \mid x_c)\) at every position. The gradient on the student's logits is \(P_S - P_T\) instead of \(P_S - \mathbb{1}_{\text{next}}\): all 256K vocabulary entries get a graded signal, including plausible alternatives the corpus did not contain. Knowledge distillation 知识蒸馏 lets them "simulate training beyond the number of available tokens": the 2B and 9B saw "more than 50× the compute-optimal quantity" of tokens. At 20 tokens per parameter I get 50× on the nominal 2B (2T tokens) and 44× on the nominal 9B (8T), or 38× and 43× counting embeddings (2.6B and 9.2B parameters); the report does not show its calculation, so read "more than 50×" as an order of magnitude: 40 to 50× beyond Chinchilla.

Distillation during pretraining (Gemma 2, 3)
Problem
A small model trained far past compute-optimal still improves, but only logarithmically, and unique tokens run out.
Idea
Train the student on the teacher's next-token distribution over ordinary text, for many tokens, instead of on one-hot labels.
Why it works
Each position supplies a full distribution (richer target, lower-variance gradient) rather than one sampled token, so each token teaches more.
Evidence
A 2B student on 500B tokens averages 67.7 over three benchmarks distilled from a 7B teacher against 60.3 from scratch; perplexity improves 23 to 21, 19 to 17, 17 to 15 for 200M, 400M and 1B students [Gemma 2, Tab. 6-7]. The Gemma 2 2B (2T tokens) also beats the Gemma 1 2B (3T), though architecture and data changed too.
Cost
A teacher forward per training token: at \(6N_S + 2N_T\) FLOPs, a 9B student with a 27B teacher costs \(54 + 54 = 108\) GFLOPs per token, 2× plain training (illustrative: the report names 27B to 9B only as the ablation's "target distillation", not the production teacher).
Used by
Gemma 2 (2B, 9B), all Gemma 3 models, and Gemma 2's SFT stage ("distillation from the teacher on the student's distribution"). See page 19.

Pretraining distillation is not training on the teacher's generated text: the corpus is fixed and only the target changes. Teacher-generated responses appear in Gemma 2's SFT stage [Gemma 2, §4].

QK-norm in place of soft-capping (Gemma 3)
Problem
Attention logits are bilinear in the weights and can grow without limit (page 11). Gemma 2 capped them with \(50 \tanh(z/50)\).
Idea
RMS-normalize each query and key per head before RoPE, with a learned gain, and drop the cap: "we replace the soft-capping of Gemma 2 with QK-norm", citing ViT-22B, Wortsman et al. and Chameleon [Gemma 3, §2].
Why it works
With unit-RMS \(q, k\) and the standard \(1/\sqrt{d_h}\) scale, \(|z| \le g^2 \sqrt{d_h}\) for any weights, so sharpness grows only through \(d_h\) gains (Gemma 3 27B scales by \(1/\sqrt{168}\) via query_pre_attn_scalar and Gemma 4's code by 1, which changes the constant, not the weight-independence). A cap bounds range only (one key at 50 among 8,191 zeros is still one-hot) and its \(\tanh\) gradient vanishes past the cap (page 11), and it puts a \(\tanh\) inside every score, which fused kernels had to support (FlashAttention added a softcap argument in v2.6, README changelog).
Evidence
The report gives no Gemma-specific ablation, only the citations: ViT-22B diverging at 8B parameters without it, and Wortsman et al.'s small-proxy studies.
Cost
Two per-head norms per attention layer, and materialized keys are required, so it does not fit absorbed MLA (page 11). Gemma 4's released config keeps final_logit_softcapping = 30 (official 31B config) while Gemma 3's has it unset: the final cap returned, the attention cap did not.
Used by
Gemma 3 and 4, OLMo 2, Qwen3, DeepSeek-V4 (page 11).

Gemma 4 (April 2026): shared K and V, fewer global heads, and a MoE

Google released Gemma 4 on 2 April 2026 (E2B, E4B, 26B-A4B and 31B; the 12B followed on 3 June) under Apache 2.0 [Google Open Source Blog], where Gemma 1 to 3n used the custom Gemma Terms of Use. The report (dated 19 June 2026, on arXiv 2 July, revised 24 July) lists dense models with 2.3B (E2B) and 4.5B (E4B) effective parameters, a 12B and a 31B, and a 26B MoE with 3.8B activated (26B-A4B) [Gemma 4, §1-2]. E2B and E4B use per-layer embeddings (5B and 8B total); the 12B is "encoder-free", projecting raw audio chunks and 48×48 image patches into the embedding space. The model card gives E2B 35 layers, E4B 42, 12B 48, 31B 60 and 26B-A4B 30, with contexts of 128K (E2B, E4B) or 256K [Gemma 4 model card].

Attention. The interleave stays 5:1 (4:1 for E2B) with a local window of 1,024 tokens (512 in E2B and E4B), and per the model card the last layer is always global. Global layers use keys as values ("values = keys", except in E2B and E4B) with p-RoPE at \(p = 0.25\) (a quarter of the dimensions rotated), bases 1M global and 10K local; E2B and E4B also share KV across layers. The report says these "reduce the global KV cache footprint by up to 37.5%" [Gemma 4, §1-2] without showing how it arrives at that figure (cf. DeepSeek-V4, page 07). The released Hugging Face code answers how K=V treats rotation: a global layer has a k_proj and no v_proj, K goes through k_norm and p-RoPE, and V is a scale-free RMSNorm (v_norm) of the same raw projection with no rotation, so K and V differ after their norms. Attention logits use scale 1.0 rather than \(1/\sqrt{d_h}\). The stock cache stores both tensors, so the memory saving needs a cache that keeps one projection and applies the two norms on read. The official 31B config.json gives the shapes: local layers 16 KV heads of dimension 256; global layers only 4 KV heads of dimension 512, attention_k_eq_v true.

Check against the report's own table. Per token per layer a local layer caches \(2 \cdot 16 \cdot 256 = 8192\) numbers and a global layer \(4 \cdot 512 = 2048\). With global layers at 5, 11, ..., 59 (ten), at \(T = 32{,}768\) and an int8 cache: \(50 \cdot 8192 \cdot 1024 + 10 \cdot 2048 \cdot 32768 = 419.4\text{M} + 671.1\text{M} = 1.0905\) GB, against +1.10 GB in the report's Table 3. The rest. Pretraining is described only as "similar" to Gemma 3's, with no token count and no restated distillation detail. Attention: banded and dense layers, GQA, YaRN

Learned attention sinks

The card: "Each attention head has a learned bias in the denominator of the softmax, similar to off-by-one attention and attention sinks, which enables the attention mechanism to pay no attention to any tokens" [gpt-oss model card, §2.2]. Each head in each layer owns one scalar \(z\), an attention sink 注意力汇聚 in learned-logit form, and

\[ p_{t,j} = \frac{\exp(s_{t,j})}{\sum_{k \le t} \exp(s_{t,k}) + \exp(z)}, \qquad o_t = \sum_{j \le t} p_{t,j}\, v_j \]

The sink is only in the denominator: a fictitious key with a zero value vector. The real weights sum to \(1 - p_{\text{sink}} < 1\), and a head with nothing to read can send its mass there and output almost nothing. In the released Hugging Face code the sink is an nn.Parameter of shape (num_attention_heads,) per attention module, so 64 scalars per layer, 2,304 in the 120b, and the eager path is:

sinks = module.sinks.reshape(1, -1, 1, 1).expand(B, n_h, T, 1)      # one logit per head
logits = torch.cat([attn_weights, sinks], dim=-1)                   # (B, n_h, T, S + 1)
probs = torch.softmax(logits - logits.max(-1, keepdim=True).values, dim=-1)
scores = probs[..., :-1]                                            # drop the sink column: rows now sum to < 1
out = scores @ v

Why a logit and not a token. Softmax weights sum to one, so a head with no relevant key must put its mass somewhere, and trained models dump it on the first tokens, which every later query sees [StreamingLLM, §3.1]. Evicting them in a sliding window sends Llama-2-13B from perplexity 5.40 (4 sink tokens + 1,020 recent) to 5,158 (plain 1,024 window), and newlines in their place still give 5.60 (Tab. 1; page 09). StreamingLLM's fix for new models is a dedicated learnable sink token, with a catch: in their 160M pretraining runs it scored 18.01 with the token in the cache and 1,235 without (Tab. 3), so it must be pinned in the KV cache forever. Softmax-off-by-one is a fixed zero sink ("equivalent to prepending a token with an all-zero Key and Value") and did not by itself remove the reliance on other initial tokens (29,214 without them). A learned per-head logit has no cache entry to lose, behaves the same in dense and banded layers, and costs 2,304 parameters. After 128 positions the first token is outside every banded window, so a token-based sink would need special cache handling in exactly the layers with the smallest cache.

Two identities. With \(Z_t = \sum_k e^{s_{t,k}}\) and \(\mathrm{LSE}_t = \log Z_t\), the sink is a sigmoid gate on the head output, since \(Z/(Z + e^z) = \sigma(\mathrm{LSE} - z)\):

\[ o^{\text{sink}}_t = o^{\text{plain}}_t \cdot \sigma\!\big(\mathrm{LSE}_t - z\big), \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z} = -\sum_t p_{\text{sink},t}\, \langle dO_t,\, O_t\rangle \]

The first means a FlashAttention-style kernel keeps its tiled loop: it already yields the row-wise log-sum-exp, and the sink is an epilogue rescale (page 16). The second, which I checked against autograd, reuses the \(D_t = \langle dO_t, O_t\rangle\) term of the flash backward pass. The drill checks the forward identity, and the gate view links sinks to gated attention (page 11).

Learned per-head sink logits (gpt-oss)
Problem
Softmax forces each head to distribute unit mass even when no key is relevant. Models park it on early tokens, and windowed inference that evicts them breaks the model.
Idea
Add one learned scalar per head to the softmax denominator (a zero-valued key), trained from the start.
Why it works
The head gets an explicit place for surplus mass, so it need not repurpose real tokens as dumps, and it can output nearly zero. It is available at every position and needs no cache entry.
Evidence
The card states the design and gives no ablation. Independent evidence: in MiMo-V2-Flash's 32B ablation a 128-token window without a sink loses 2.4 MMLU points against all-global (54.9 vs 57.3) and with one reaches 58.3 (page 09); StreamingLLM's Tab. 3 shows the token variant works but must stay in cache.
Cost
2,304 parameters and one row-wise rescale, but kernels must support it. \(z\) is per head and layer, not per query.
Used by
gpt-oss, MiMo-V2-Flash and DeepSeek-V4 (page 09). The Gemma reports do not mention sinks.

The MoE block

All 36 layers are MoE, with no dense prefix and no shared expert. A linear router scores the 128 experts, keeps the top 4 and mixes them with a softmax over only those four logits, as in Mixtral [gpt-oss model card, §2.2] (page 08). The card calls the gated SwiGLU "unconventional, including clamping and a residual connection": in the released code the gate is clamped at 7.0, the linear branch to \([-7, 7]\), and the output is \((\text{up} + 1)\cdot\text{gate}\cdot\sigma(1.702\cdot\text{gate})\), with biases (the card's 114.71B "MLP" figure is expert weights 114.66B plus biases 0.04B plus routers 0.01B). The card does not say why; bounded activations suit 4-bit weights. No balancing method or expert-width ablation is disclosed.

MXFP4 experts: how 117B parameters fit on one GPU

"We post-trained the models with quantization of the MoE weights to MXFP4 format, where weights are quantized to 4.25 bits per parameter. The MoE weights are responsible for 90+% of the total parameter count, and quantizing these to MXFP4 enables the larger model to fit on a single 80GB GPU" [gpt-oss model card, §2.1]. MXFP4 is the 4-bit member of the OCP microscaling family 微缩放: blocks of 32 values share one power-of-two scale (E8M0, 8 bits) and each value is an E2M1 float [Rouhani et al. 2023, Tab. 1]. Conversion (their Algorithm 1) sets \(X = 2^{\lfloor \log_2 \max_i |v_i| \rfloor - 2}\), where 2 is the exponent of E2M1's largest value 6, then rounds each \(v_i/X\) to the nearest of 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6 with overflow clamped to 6 (pages 15 and 17 cover MX and NVFP4). Cost per weight: \((32 \cdot 4 + 8)/32 = 4.25\) bits.

Worked numbers. Experts: \(114.71\text{B} \times 4.25/8 = 60.94\) GB = 56.75 GiB. The BF16 remainder (attention 0.96B plus embeddings 1.16B) is \(2.12\text{B} \times 2 = 3.95\) GiB. Total 60.70 GiB against the card's 60.8 (routers, expert biases and norms are the rest: the Hugging Face metadata lists 2.167B BF16 parameters in all, which gives 60.77 GiB): 4.46 bits per parameter on average (4.47 with the exact figure), 3.58× below the 217.6 GiB of BF16. The config's modules_to_not_convert lists attention, router, embedding and lm_head. On an 80 GB GPU (74.5 GiB) that leaves 13.8 GiB for KV cache and activations, and a 128K sequence needs 4.5 GiB (below); BF16 weights would need three GPUs. The 20b gives 12.81 GiB (card: 12.8).

def weight_bytes_mxfp4(n_mx, n_bf16, block=32, scale_bits=8, elem_bits=4):
    return n_mx * (elem_bits + scale_bits / block) / 8 + n_bf16 * 2

weight_bytes_mxfp4(114.71e9, 0.96e9 + 1.16e9) / 2**30     # 60.70 GiB (card: 60.8)

The accuracy risk. A 4-bit float grid is coarse: relative rounding error reaches about 20% in most bins (33% between 0.5 and 1), values below a quarter of the scale flush to zero, and because the scale is a power of two a block maximum in \([6, 8)\cdot X\) is clamped to 6 (up to 25% error). One large weight sets the scale and can erase its 31 neighbors. The microscaling paper's direct-cast results on LLaMA-7B show the size: wikitext perplexity 9.49 in FP32, 11.48 with MXFP4 weights and MXFP8 activations, 11.15 with MXFP6 activations, and 27.20 with MXFP4 on both [Rouhani et al., Tab. 6]. Three things reduce gpt-oss's risk: only weights are 4-bit (activations stay BF16), the model was post-trained with the quantization in the loop, and attention and router stay BF16. The card publishes no BF16-versus-MXFP4 comparison, so the residual loss is unquantified. And fewer weight bytes speed bandwidth-bound decoding, but matmul FLOPs only shrink on hardware with FP4 tensor cores (page 17).

Tokenizer, the harmony format and reasoning effort

The tokenizer, o200k_harmony, is a byte-level BPE with 201,088 tokens that extends GPT-4o's o200k vocabulary with tokens for the harmony chat format [gpt-oss model card, §2.3]. A message is <|start|>{header}<|message|>{content}<|end|>; roles are system, developer, user, assistant and tool, with the hierarchy System > Developer > User > Assistant > Tool. The new idea is channels marking visibility: analysis for chain of thought, commentary for function calls and preambles, final for the answer shown to the user [harmony spec]:

<|start|>user<|message|>What is 2 + 2?<|end|>
<|start|>assistant
<|channel|>analysis<|message|>User asks: "What is 2 + 2?" Simple arithmetic. Provide answer.<|end|>
<|start|>assistant<|channel|>final<|message|>2 + 2 = 4.<|return|>

Generation stops at <|return|> (token 200002, the config's eos_token_id) or <|call|> (200012). The card says the format "is critical to deploy our gpt-oss models properly" and that past turns' reasoning "should be removed": the template-mismatch failure of page 19 with higher stakes. What converged, and what did not

  • KV bytes per token is the shared target, attacked on different axes. GQA-8 is the baseline.   • Short-window hybrids became a common open choice, after Mistral's all-window design was dropped, though not a universal one (Mistral Medium 3.5 is full attention with GQA-8). The two shipped hybrids differ sharply: Gemma 3 and 4 keep one global layer in six with a 1,024 window, gpt-oss one in two with a 128 window. Per-layer cache size matters as much as the ratio: gpt-oss caches 1,024 numbers per token per layer, Gemma 3 27B 4,096.
  • Stability tools solve different problems: QK-norm (Gemma 3, 4) bounds logit growth, sinks (gpt-oss) give surplus softmax mass a home, soft-capping bounded range only. The Gemma reports do not mention sinks, and the gpt-oss card does not mention QK-norm.

Local:global ratio and window
Problem
Pick how many layers get full context and how wide the local window is, under a KV budget at the target context.
Idea
Solve \(f(r)\) for the budget, then take the smallest global fraction and window that keep long-context evaluations intact after context extension, with sinks if the window is small.
Why it works
Local layers do local mixing at constant cost, and a minority of global layers do exact retrieval. Depth stacks the two.
Evidence
Gemma 3: perplexity is nearly flat from 1:1 to 7:1 and for windows 4,096 down to 512, and at 32K a 2B model's KV overhead falls from 60% (global only) to under 15% (the paper's "1:3" configuration with a 1,024 window) [Gemma 3, §5.2]. But perplexity mostly measures local prediction: MiMo found a 512 window collapsing on long-context tests after extension while 128 did not, and MiniMax saw SWA hybrids degrade beyond 32K (page 09).
Cost
Fewer global layers means less retrieval capacity, with more reliance on each. Windows interact with sinks, per-layer RoPE bases, prefix caching and paged-KV block sizes.
Used by
Gemma 2 (1:1, 4,096), Gemma 3 and 4 (5:1, 1,024; Gemma 4 ends on a global layer), gpt-oss (1:1, 128).
From your jet tagger

Your tagger let every particle attend to every other: \(T\) was tens or a few hundred, and a local window would have discarded exactly the pairwise structure you wanted. A language model is the opposite regime: \(T\) is \(10^5\), most useful dependencies are short-range, and a few are very long-range, so local/global layering pays for full attention only where language needs it. Sinks have a closer analogy: a class token every particle can read, used here as a place to put attention you do not want to spend.

Common misconceptions

  • "With a sliding window the model only uses the last \(W\) tokens." Information moves \(W-1\) positions per layer, so the top layer can depend on tokens about \(\ell (W-1)\) back (131K for Mistral 7B). But that is a bound, diluted through many layers, and exact retrieval beyond \(W\) is what global layers are for.
  • "5:1 local:global means 6× less KV cache." It is 5.96× for Gemma 3 27B at 128K only. At \(T \le W\) the saving is zero, it depends on 10 of 62 layers being global, and the server must actually allocate window-sized caches for local layers.
  • "Attention sinks are special tokens you keep in the cache." That is StreamingLLM's fix for models trained without them. gpt-oss's sink is a learned scalar per head in the softmax denominator: no token, no cache entry, 2,304 parameters in total.
  • "The sink logit is a bias added to every score." It enters the denominator only, as one extra term \(e^z\). The real weights sum to less than 1, and the head output is scaled by \(\sigma(\mathrm{LSE} - z)\).
  • "MXFP4 makes gpt-oss 4-bit and 4× faster." Only the MoE weights (98% of parameters) are MXFP4, at 4.25 bits including the scale. Attention, router and embeddings stay BF16, so the checkpoint averages 4.46 bits per parameter (3.58× smaller than BF16), and activations stay BF16. Bytes drop; matmul FLOPs only drop on hardware with FP4 tensor cores.
  • "Gemma's 'feedforward hidden dims' is \(d_{\text{ff}}\)." For GeGLU the paper's number is twice the width of each branch (Gemma 2B: 32,768 listed, 16,384 per branch). Also the head dimension is decoupled from \(d\): Gemma 7B has \(16 \times 256 = 4096 > d = 3072\), and gpt-oss has \(64 \times 64 = 4096 > 2880\).
  • "Gemma distillation means training on text generated by the teacher." In pretraining the corpus is fixed, and the student matches the teacher's next-token distribution on it (Gemma 3: 256 sampled logits per token). Teacher-generated text is used in the SFT stage.
  • "Soft-capping and QK-norm are two ways of doing the same thing." A cap bounds the range of a logit but not the sharpness of a row, and saturates the gradient. QK-norm bounds \(|z|\) by \(g^2\sqrt{d_h}\) for any weights. Gemma 4's config still has a final-logit cap, so they can coexist.

Interview questions

Warm-up Why does sliding-window attention still allow long-range information flow?

In a layer with window \(W\), the state at position \(i\) attends to the previous layer's states at positions \(i-W+1\) to \(i\). Those states have themselves attended to their windows, so the dependency set grows by \(W-1\) positions per layer: after \(\ell\) layers it reaches about \(\ell(W-1)\) tokens. Mistral 7B: \(32 \times 4096 = 131{,}072\), the paper's "theoretical attention span of approximately 131K tokens".

Three caveats an interviewer expects. It is a bound: the path crosses \(\ell\) attention and MLP layers, each mixing with softmax and residual streams, so signal from far tokens is diluted and exact retrieval is not guaranteed (the paper shows no long-range retrieval test, and the model's listed context is 8,192). It is one-directional and per layer, so the model can only route information, not look it up. And the fix used in practice is hybrid: keep a minority of global layers for exact lookup (Gemma 3's 1 in 6, gpt-oss's every other layer) and let the local layers stay constant-cost.

Warm-up How big is Mistral 7B's KV cache with the rolling buffer, and why does RoPE not break it?

Per token: \(32 \cdot 2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2\) B = 128 KiB. The buffer holds \(W = 4096\) entries per layer, key and value of position \(i\) in slot \(i \bmod W\), so it is \(4096 \times 128\) KiB = 512 MiB per sequence for any length beyond 4,096. At 32K a full cache is 4 GiB, so the saving is the paper's 8×.

RoPE is fine because keys are rotated by their absolute position when they are written, and a score depends only on the position difference, so entries can stay in slot order (softmax does not care about key order). Contrast StreamingLLM, which evicts middle tokens and therefore assigns positions inside the cache and caches keys before rotation (page 09).

Warm-up Gemma 1 2B's Table 1 lists "feedforward hidden dims 32768" and Table 2 lists 1,981,884,416 non-embedding parameters. Reconcile them.

GeGLU has two input projections (gate and up) plus a down projection, so the per-branch width is half of 32,768: \(d_{\text{ff}} = 16{,}384\). Per layer (\(d = 2048\), 8 query heads and 1 KV head of dimension 256): attention \(2048 \cdot 2048\) (Q) \(+ 2 \cdot 2048 \cdot 256\) (K, V) \(+ 2048 \cdot 2048\) (O) = 9,437,184; FFN \(3 \cdot 2048 \cdot 16384 = 100{,}663{,}296\); two RMSNorm vectors 4,096; total 110,104,576. Times 18 layers is 1,981,882,368, plus the final norm 2,048 gives 1,981,884,416, exactly the table. The embedding \(256{,}128 \times 2048 = 524{,}550{,}144\) is shared with the output and counted once.

The lesson is to check the gating convention before trusting any "hidden dim". The same method reproduces Gemma 2's three sizes exactly once you add four norms per layer.

Core What are attention sinks, and how does gpt-oss implement them?

Softmax weights sum to one, so a head with no relevant key must still put its mass somewhere. Trained models dump it on the first tokens, visible to every later query. StreamingLLM showed that evicting them breaks a windowed model (Llama-2-13B: perplexity 5,158 with a plain 1,024 window, 5.40 with 4 initial tokens kept), and that it is position, not content, that matters (newlines work: 5.60).

gpt-oss gives each head in each layer one learned scalar \(z\) added to the softmax denominator: \(p_j = e^{s_j}/(\sum_k e^{s_k} + e^z)\). It is a key with a zero value vector, so the weights over real keys sum to \(1 - p_{\text{sink}}\) and a head can output nearly zero. Code: append a column of \(z\) to the scores, softmax, drop the column. It costs 64 scalars per layer (2,304 in the 120b). A logit beats StreamingLLM's sink token because a token must stay pinned in the cache (their 160M model: 18.01 with it, 1,235 without) while a logit has no cache entry, and it works identically in the banded layers where the first token is outside the 128-token window.

Core Why did Gemma 3 switch from soft-capping to QK-norm?

What the report says: "we replace the soft-capping of Gemma 2 with QK-norm", inspired by ViT-22B, Wortsman et al. and Chameleon. It gives no Gemma-specific ablation, so the rest is the technical case (page 11).

Soft-capping \(c\tanh(z/c)\) with \(c = 50\) bounds a logit's range but not the sharpness of a row: one key at 50 among 8,191 zeros is still one-hot to 18 digits, and beyond the cap the \(\tanh\) gradient vanishes (0.42 at \(z = c\), 0.07 at \(2c\)). It also puts a \(\tanh\) inside every attention score, which fused kernels had to add support for (FlashAttention added a softcap argument in v2.6). QK-norm normalizes \(q\) and \(k\) per head before RoPE, giving \(|z| \le g^2\sqrt{d_h}\) for any weights (with the standard \(1/\sqrt{d_h}\) scale), so logit growth cannot come from the two weight matrices and is limited to the \(d_h\) gains. Costs: two norms per attention layer, and it needs materialized keys (so it cannot be used with absorbed MLA). One nuance: Gemma 4's released config has final_logit_softcapping = 30, so the final cap returned, while the attention cap did not.

Core How does MXFP4 let gpt-oss-120b fit on one GPU? What is the accuracy risk?

MoE experts are 114.71B of the 116.83B parameters (98.2%). MXFP4 stores blocks of 32 E2M1 values with one shared 8-bit power-of-two scale: \((32 \cdot 4 + 8)/32 = 4.25\) bits. Experts: \(114.71\text{B} \times 4.25/8 = 60.94\) GB = 56.75 GiB. Attention, embeddings and unembedding (2.12B) stay in BF16: 3.95 GiB. Total 60.7 GiB (the card says 60.8), against 217.6 GiB in BF16, so it fits an 80 GB GPU (74.5 GiB) with about 13.8 GiB left for KV cache (4.5 GiB per 128K sequence) and activations.

Risks: 15 distinct element values (relative rounding error up to about 20%, values below a quarter of the scale flush to zero); a power-of-two scale means a block maximum in \([6,8)X\) clamps to 6X (up to 25% error); one outlier weight degrades its 31 neighbors. Direct-cast MXFP4 hurts (LLaMA-7B wikitext perplexity 9.49 to 11.48 with MXFP8 activations, 27.20 with MXFP4 activations). Mitigations: weights only, BF16 activations, BF16 attention and router, and post-training with the quantization in the loop. The card publishes no BF16-versus-MXFP4 comparison, so the residual loss is unquantified. The speed benefit is bandwidth (3.58× fewer weight bytes), not FLOPs.

Core Compute the KV cache at 128K tokens for Gemma 3 27B (5:1 local:global, window 1,024) and compare it with all-global.

Config: 62 layers, 16 KV heads, head dimension 128, so \(2 \cdot 16 \cdot 128 = 4096\) numbers per token per layer, 8 KiB in bf16. Global layers are at indices 5, 11, ..., 59: 10 global, 52 local. At \(T = 131{,}072 = 2^{17}\): a global layer holds \(8\text{ KiB} \times 2^{17} = 1\) GiB, a local layer \(8\text{ KiB} \times 1024 = 8\) MiB.

All-global: 62 GiB. Hybrid: \(10 + 52 \times 8/1024 = 10.41\) GiB, a 5.96× reduction (not exactly 6: 10 of 62 layers are global, and local layers still hold 1,024 tokens). With bf16 weights of 50.3 GiB, all-global needs 112 GiB and does not fit on one 80 GB GPU; the hybrid needs 60.7 GiB. Caveats: at \(T \le 1024\) there is no saving, and the saving exists only if the server allocates window-sized caches for local layers. Gemma 4 31B, which shares K=V in 4 wide global heads, is 5.78 GiB by the same arithmetic.

Core What did the Mixtral routing analysis find, and what does it imply?

Expert assignments at layers 0, 15 and 31 look very similar across ArXiv, PubMed and PhilPapers text: there is no domain specialization (only DM Mathematics differs a little). The router does track syntax: 'self' in Python, 'Question' and indentation tokens go to consistent experts. And consecutive tokens repeat their first-choice expert well above the 12.5% chance level: 13.6 to 14.9% at layer 0 (close to random), 23.6 to 28.4% at layer 15, 19.7 to 26.3% at layer 31 (Tab. 5).

Implications: do not describe experts as "the math expert" (specialization by domain appears only under particular balancing regimes, page 08). Temporal locality means that within one sequence some experts are over-subscribed under expert parallelism, and it makes expert caching or offloading plausible, because the set of hot experts changes slowly. It also means the "all experts are read at large batch" argument is softened for small batches of correlated tokens.

Core Explain Gemma 2's distillation: the loss, why it helps a small model, what it costs, and what the evidence is.

Loss: \(-\sum_x P_T(x|x_c)\log P_S(x|x_c)\) at every position, so the student's logit gradient is \(P_S - P_T\) instead of \(P_S - \text{one-hot}\). Each position teaches the whole distribution over 256K tokens, a richer, lower-variance signal. The report's argument: small models are under-trained and simply training longer scales only logarithmically, so distill for a very large number of tokens ("more than 50× the compute-optimal quantity") to "simulate training beyond the number of available tokens". With 20 tokens per parameter I get 50× and 44× on the nominal sizes (38× and 43× counting embeddings); the report does not show its calculation.

Evidence: 2B student, 500B tokens: 67.7 average over three benchmarks distilled from a 7B teacher against 60.3 from scratch; perplexity 23 to 21, 19 to 17, 17 to 15 for 200M, 400M, 1B students. Cost: a teacher forward per token; with \(6N_S + 2N_T\) FLOPs, a 9B student and a 27B teacher is \(54 + 54 = 108\) GFLOPs per token, 2× plain (the production teacher is not named, only "27B to 9B" as the ablation's target; whether logits are precomputed is not stated). The 27B was trained from scratch. Gemma 3 uses 256 sampled logits per token, renormalized.

Deep-dive Local:global ratio: how would you choose it?

Start from the budget. Relative to all-global the KV cache (and decode read, and roughly prefill attention FLOPs) is \(f = \frac{1}{r+1} + \frac{r}{r+1}\min(1, W/T)\) for \(r\) local per global layer. At 128K and \(W = 1024\): 1:1 is 0.50, 3:1 0.26, 5:1 0.17, 7:1 0.13, 11:1 0.09. Diminishing returns: 5:1 to 11:1 saves 8 points of cache and halves the layers able to do exact lookup. Per-layer cache size matters too: gpt-oss at 1:1 with a 128 window caches 2 KiB per token-layer, Gemma 3 27B 8 KiB, so with also fewer layers (36 against 62) gpt-oss ends at 4.5 GiB and Gemma 3 at 10.4 GiB despite the worse ratio.

Then quality. Perplexity is nearly flat from 1:1 to 7:1 and windows to 512 (Gemma 3 §5.2), but it mostly measures local prediction. I would pick the smallest global fraction and window that keep RULER, NoLiMa and multi-hop tasks intact after context extension, at 32K and 128K, as per-length curves. MiMo's ablation is the warning: 512 collapsed after extension while 128 did not; MiniMax saw hybrids degrade past 32K. Also ablate sinks (a small window needs them), place a global layer last (Gemma 4), give global layers a higher RoPE base (Gemma 3), and check that the serving stack supports window-sized paged-KV blocks and prefix caching. Default: 5:1 to 7:1, a 512 to 1,024 window, sinks, then revise from the curves.

Deep-dive Show that a sink is a sigmoid gate on the head output, and say what it means for a FlashAttention-style forward and backward kernel.

Let \(Z = \sum_j e^{s_j}\), \(\mathrm{LSE} = \log Z\), and \(o = \sum_j (e^{s_j}/Z) v_j\) the plain output. With a sink, \(o' = \sum_j e^{s_j} v_j / (Z + e^z) = o \cdot Z/(Z + e^z) = o \cdot \sigma(\mathrm{LSE} - z)\), since \(Z/(Z+e^z) = 1/(1 + e^{z - \mathrm{LSE}})\).

Forward: the tiled loop is unchanged. It already keeps the running max and sum and ends with \(\mathrm{LSE}\), so the kernel applies the gate as an epilogue, or equivalently stores \(\mathrm{LSE}' = \operatorname{logaddexp}(\mathrm{LSE}, z)\) and scales \(o\) by \(e^{\mathrm{LSE} - \mathrm{LSE}'}\). Backward: use \(P = e^{S - \mathrm{LSE}'}\). The sink column has a zero value, so its \(dP\) is 0 and \(D_t = \sum_j P_{tj}\, dP_{tj} = \langle dO_t, O'_t\rangle\) is the usual flash term; \(dS = P \circ (dP - D)\) is unchanged. For the sink itself, \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial z = -\sum_t p_{\text{sink},t}\,\langle dO_t, O'_t\rangle\), one more reduction over the \(D_t\) values (I checked this against autograd). The drill test verifies the forward identity, and the sink-off case (\(z \to -\infty\)) recovers plain attention.

Deep-dive Reconcile gpt-oss-120b's 116.83B total and 5.13B active parameters from its config.

Config: 36 layers, \(d = 2880\), 64 query heads and 8 KV heads of dimension 64, 128 experts of width 2,880 with top-4, vocabulary 201,088, untied embeddings. Experts: \(36 \cdot 128 \cdot (3 \cdot 2880^2 + 8640) = 114.70\)B with biases (114.66B without); adding the routers, \(36 \cdot (2880 \cdot 128 + 128) = 0.013\)B, gives the card's 114.71B "MLP" figure. Attention per layer: \(2880 \cdot 4096 + 2 \cdot 2880 \cdot 512 + 4096 \cdot 2880\) plus 8,000 biases (4,096 + 512 + 512 + 2,880) = 26,550,080; \(\times 36 = 0.956\)B. Embedding plus unembedding: \(2 \cdot 201{,}088 \cdot 2880 = 1.158\)B. Total 116.83B.

Active: 4 of 128 experts, \(36 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2880^2 = 3.583\)B, plus attention 0.956B, plus the unembedding 0.579B (the card counts the unembedding but not the embedding), plus routers \(36 \cdot 2880 \cdot 128 = 0.013\)B: 5.13B. Experts are 98.2% of the parameters, which is why quantizing only them gives 3.58×. Conventions differ across labs: Gemma 4's 3.8B active appears to include its tied embedding table, DeepSeek counts differently again (page 26), so always ask what is counted.

Deep-dive Design question: a new 36-layer, ~100B-total MoE with GQA-8 heads of dimension 128 must serve 128K contexts. Compare attention options by KV memory, and say what you would evaluate.

Baseline all-global GQA-8: \(2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2\) B = 4 KiB per token per layer, so 144 KiB per token and 18 GiB at 128K. Options: (a) Gemma-style 5:1 with a 1,024 window: 6 global layers, \(6 \times 4\text{ KiB} \times 2^{17} = 3\) GiB plus \(30 \times 4\text{ KiB} \times 1024 = 120\) MiB: 3.12 GiB (5.8×). (b) gpt-oss-style 1:1 with a 128 window: 9 GiB (2×) at this head size, and the small window needs sinks. (c) MLA as in Mistral Small 4: \(36 \times 320 \times 2\) B = 22.5 KiB per token, 2.81 GiB (6.4×), uniform and no window, but with heavier kernels, decode FLOPs that grow with the head count, and no head-sharded tensor parallelism (page 07). (d) Stack them: MLA in the few global layers of a hybrid.

What each saves: windows cut capacity and bandwidth for local layers but not the global ones; MLA cuts both per layer; neither reduces the FLOPs of global layers at 128K. Risks: retrieval in hybrids, and kernel or TP complexity for MLA. Evaluation: perplexity as a regression check only, RULER, NoLiMa and multi-hop at 32K and 128K after context extension, ablations of window, ratio and sinks, measured decode tokens/s and batch capacity on the target hardware. I would pick (a) or (d) as the main bet, with the full-attention twin as the control.
