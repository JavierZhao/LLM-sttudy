Kimi: Muon at a trillion parameters

Moonshot's line is one story. k1.5 (January 2025) was a long-context RL recipe with no value network [k1.5]. Moonlight (February 2025) showed that Muon, with weight decay and an update scale of \(0.2\sqrt{\max(n,m)}\) that matches AdamW's update RMS (so its learning rate carries over), trains a 3B-active, 16B-total MoE with about 52% of AdamW's FLOPs [Moonlight]. K2 (July 2025) took Muon to 1.04T parameters, hit a new failure, and fixed it with MuonClip [K2]. Later releases extend K2 with linear attention (Kimi Linear, K3), INT4 QAT, native vision and agent swarms. The report is titled "Open Agentic Intelligence", and the agent it means is a 智能体: a system that perceives, plans and acts through tools.

MuonClip and the worked QK-Clip numbers

Pages 14 and 11 explain the failure: with vanilla Muon in a 9B-active, 53B-total run the max attention logit passed 1,000 within about 15,000 steps (read off K2 Fig. 2, left), and QK-Norm cannot be used because absorbed MLA never materializes keys (page 07) [K2, §2.1]. K2 instead acts on the weights after each update. For head \(h\), with \(S^h_{\max}\) the largest pre-softmax logit over the batch \(B\) and all causal pairs:

\[ S^h_{\max} = \frac{1}{\sqrt d}\max_{X\in B}\,\max_{i,j}\; q^h_i\, {k^h_j}^{\top}, \qquad \gamma_h = \min\!\Big(1, \frac{\tau}{S^h_{\max}}\Big), \qquad W^h_q \leftarrow \gamma_h^{\alpha} W^h_q, \quad W^h_k \leftarrow \gamma_h^{1-\alpha} W^h_k \]
QK-Clip, applied after the Muon step; α = 0.5 ("typically"), τ = 100 in K2 [K2, §2.1, Alg. 1]

The logit is bilinear in the two weights, so factors \(\gamma^{\alpha}\) and \(\gamma^{1-\alpha}\) scale every logit of the head by \(\gamma\). The report first gives a layer-wide version with \(S_{\max}=\max_h S^h_{\max}\), then moves to the per-head factor because "only a small subset of heads exhibit exploding logits". \(S^h_{\max}\) is read from the forward pass that already ran, so the current step's forward and backward are unchanged. The report's Appendix E analysis defines the max logit with an absolute value, \(\max|q_i\,k_j^{\top}|\); §2.1 and Algorithm 1 use the signed value, as here and in the drill.

Worked example, \(\tau = 100\). Four heads in one layer with batch max logits 250, 80, 60, 30:

Only H1 changes. A layer-wide rule would use 250 for everyone and shrink H2 to H4 to 32, 24 and 12, flattening their softmaxes for no reason (our reasoning). A different split moves weights but not the logit: \(\alpha = 0.25\) gives \(0.4^{0.25} = 0.795\) on \(W_q\) and \(0.4^{0.75} = 0.503\) on \(W_k\), and \(0.795 \times 0.503 = 0.4\).

MLA needs three factors. An MLA logit is \((q^C\!\cdot k^C + q^R\!\cdot k^R)/\sqrt{d_{nope}+d_{rope}}\): a content term from per-head queries and keys, and a rotary term from a per-head \(q^R\) and one rotary key \(k^R\) shared by all heads (page 07). K2 scales \(q^C\) and \(k^C\) by \(\sqrt{\gamma_h}\), \(q^R\) by \(\gamma_h\), and leaves \(k^R\) alone, so both terms shrink by exactly \(\gamma_h\) and no other head is disturbed. Scaling the shared \(k^R\) would change every head's rotary term at once.

import torch

def qk_clip(Wq, Wk, S, tau, alpha=0.5):
    """Wq, Wk: (H, D, d) per-head weights; S: (H,) max logits from this step's forward pass."""
    gamma = torch.where(S > tau, tau / S.clamp_min(1e-12), torch.ones_like(S))    # (H,)
    return Wq * gamma.pow(alpha).view(-1, 1, 1), Wk * gamma.pow(1 - alpha).view(-1, 1, 1), gamma

# In the training loop, after optimizer.step():  W_q, W_k, gamma = qk_clip(W_q, W_k, S_max_from_forward, tau=100.0)
# For MLA: W_qc, W_kc *= gamma.sqrt();  W_qr *= gamma;  the shared W_kr is left untouched.

In the K2 run logits rose to the cap of 100 and decayed on their own after about 30% of training: 12.7% of heads triggered the clip at least once in the first 70,000 steps, after which every head had at some point brought its max logit below 100 and the clip was inactive (15.5T tokens at 67M per batch is about 231K steps, so 70K steps is 30%), a transient safety net, and the report states 15.5T tokens with zero loss spikes [K2, §2.1, App. D]. Because \(S^h_{\max}\) is measured before the update, a head's logit on new data is only approximately \(\tau\) afterward; the drill tests exactness on the same inputs (our reading).

QK-Clip (the "Clip" in MuonClip)
Problem
Muon-trained attention logits exploded (above 1,000 at mid scale), a regime tied to loss spikes, and QK-Norm cannot be used with absorbed MLA.
Idea
After each step, for every head with \(S^h_{\max} > \tau\), multiply \(W_q\) and \(W_k\) by \(\gamma^{1/2}\) each (MLA: \(\sqrt\gamma, \sqrt\gamma, \gamma\), shared \(k^R\) untouched).
Why it works
Bilinearity: factors multiplying to \(\gamma\) scale the head's logits by \(\gamma\); no activations are touched and heads under \(\tau\) are unchanged.
Evidence
K2 Fig. 2 and App. D: capped at 100, decayed after about 30% of steps, no spikes in 15.5T tokens.
Cost
A per-head max exported by the attention kernel each step (our reasoning: FlashAttention-style kernels already track a running max, page 16); \(\tau\) to tune; it treats the symptom, not the optimizer behind it.
Used by
K2, Kimi Linear, K2.5, and K3 alongside per-head Muon [K3, §2.5].

The sparsity law

Sparsity 48: 384 experts, 8 active
Problem
At a fixed activated-parameter budget, how many total experts? K2 defines sparsity 稀疏度 as total over activated experts; V3 had 32.
Idea
Hold activated parameters fixed (8 routed plus 1 shared expert, so constant FLOPs), vary only the expert count, fit a scaling law under Muon, and stop where extra savings no longer pay for extra system complexity.
Why it works
Total parameters add capacity that costs memory but no per-token FLOPs, so equal loss needs fewer FLOPs.
Evidence
Loss fell consistently with sparsity; at validation loss 1.5, sparsity 48 needs 1.69, 1.39 and 1.15 times fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8, 16 and 32, so returns diminish [K2, §2.3, Fig. 5]. Page 08 compares other MoE laws.
Cost
"Increased infrastructure complexity". The law counts FLOPs, not the memory and all-to-all traffic serving pays (our observation).
Used by
K2 family at 384 experts; K3 at 896 with 16 active (56). Others here sit at 20 to 36 (GLM-4.5 20, Nemotron 3 Ultra 23, V3, GLM-5 and M2 32, Step 3.5 Flash 36).

64 heads instead of 128

V3 set its head count to about twice its layer count "to better utilize memory bandwidth". K2's counter: "as the context length increases, doubling the number of attention heads leads to significant inference overhead". At 128K, going from 64 to 128 heads with 384 experts costs 83% more inference FLOPs, while doubling heads (heads equal to layers versus twice the layers, in small runs) lowers validation loss only 0.5% to 1.2% [K2, §2.3, Fig. 6]. The report gives no accounting for the 83%, so we rebuild it from page 07: absorbed MLA scores each head against the 576-dim cached entry and sums over its 512-dim latent, so a cached key costs \(2n_h(576+512)\) FLOPs per layer and 1,152 bytes in bf16.

Training K2: 16-way PP, 16-way EP, no DualPipe

K2 trained on H800 nodes with a layout that runs on any multiple of 32 nodes: 16-way pipeline parallelism 流水线并行 with virtual stages, 16-way expert parallelism 专家并行, and ZeRO-1 data parallelism, a 256-GPU model-parallel group. BF16 parameters plus a FP32 gradient buffer take about 6 TB (\(1.04\text{T}\times6\) B), so about 24 GB per GPU before optimizer state; the report says about 30 GB per GPU for all states [K2, §2.4.2].

Why no DualPipe. V3's DualPipe hides the expert all-to-all by feeding micro-batches from both pipeline ends, at the price of two copies of parameters and gradients (page 27). It "doubles the memory required for parameters and gradients", forcing more PP (bubbles) or EP (overhead), and "the additional costs are prohibitively high" above 1T parameters. K2 uses interleaved 1F1B with extra warm-up micro-batches to overlap EP all-to-all with compute, and decouples weight-gradient computation from each backward pass to overlap pipeline sends, at the cost of more PP traffic and a warm-up bubble (page 15). The report gives design reasoning, not a DualPipe ablation [K2, §2.4.2].

Why EP = 16? With 64 heads K2's attention is shorter than V3's, leaving less compute to hide all-to-all behind, so EP operations must be short; and a smaller EP group "relaxes expert-balance constraints". Our inference: V3 limits tokens to 4 nodes through expert grouping at 64-way EP over 8 nodes, while 16-way EP spans only 2 nodes if its ranks are packed together (the report does not say), consistent with K2's "Expert Grouping: No". Activations are recomputed, stored in FP8-E4M3 (storage only: "we do not apply FP8 in computation") or offloaded. The recipe: 15.5T tokens at 4,096 context with WSD (500-step warmup, \(2\times10^{-4}\) for 10T, cosine to \(2\times10^{-5}\) over 5.5T, weight decay 0.1, 67M-token batch), an anneal to \(7\times10^{-6}\) on 400B tokens at 4K plus 60B at 32K, then YaRN to 128K [K2, §2.4.3, §2.5].

Problem
One pass over knowledge-dense text is not enough, and multi-epoch repetition "yields diminishing returns and increases the risk of overfitting".
Idea
An LLM rewrites each document in varied styles and perspectives (WRAP-inspired prompts), chunk by chunk so long documents avoid the rewriter's output limit, with a fidelity check against the source. Math becomes "learning-note" style (following SwallowMath) and is translated into English.
Why it works
Rows two and three see the facts ten times, as row one does, so extra compute does not explain the gain. Our reading: varied surface forms force the model to store the fact rather than the string.

Cost
Generation compute and risk to factual accuracy; the authors call synthetic scaling "an active area of investigation" (page 12).
Used by
K2; related to WRAP and Nemotron-CC's rephrases.

From k1.5 to K2's joint RL

k1.5 scaled RL context to 128K without tree search, value functions or process reward models, and introduced partial rollouts: each iteration has a token budget, and an unfinished trajectory goes to a replay buffer and continues next iteration, so only the current segment needs fresh on-policy compute (page 25) [k1.5]. K2 keeps k1.5's objective (a squared error regressing the policy log-ratio onto the centered group reward), adds per-task budget control (overlong responses are truncated and penalized) and an auxiliary PTX loss against forgetting, and uses Muon for SFT and RL because a Muon-pretrained checkpoint fine-tunes best with Muon [K2, §3.1, §3.2.3].

The distinctive part is the reward mix in one joint stage [K2, §3.2]. Verifiable rewards cover math, STEM, logic, instruction following and coding (GitHub pull-request environments on Kubernetes sandboxes, over 10,000 concurrent). A self-critique rubric reward covers open-ended qualities: the K2 critic ranks responses pairwise against core rubrics, "prescriptive rubrics that aim to eliminate reward hacking" and human-written rubrics, and is continually refit on rollouts from verifiable tasks, which "grounds its more subjective judgments in verifiable data". A learned judge normally drifts or is exploited as the policy improves, so K2 re-anchors it on ground truth; the report shows no ablation of whether this resists over-optimization (page 25). Before RL, SFT uses a three-stage agentic data pipeline: build tool specs (real and LLM-synthesized), generate an agent and tasks per tool set, then generate tool-use trajectories, because real environments are costly to build at scale (page 25).

After K2: Linear, Thinking, K2.5 and K3

Kimi Linear (October 2025) became K3's backbone: Kimi Delta Attention (Gated DeltaNet with a per-channel gate, page 09) interleaved 3:1 with MLA layers on a 48B/3B MoE, with no positional encoding in the MLA layers because the gates carry position. At 1.4T tokens under an identical recipe it reports MMLU-Pro 51.0 versus 47.2 for full MLA and up to 75% less KV cache; validation perplexity was 5.65 at 3:1, 5.70 at 7:1, 5.82 at 15:1 and 5.77 for pure MLA [Kimi Linear, Abstract, Tab. 1] (page 09).

K2 Thinking (same 1T/32B shape, 256K context) interleaves reasoning with up to 200 to 300 consecutive tool calls, and its systems decision is INT4 quantization-aware training 量化感知训练 ("INT4 weight-only quantization to MoE components", about twice the generation speed) [card]. Our reading: decoding a 1T MoE streams expert weights from HBM, so fewer weight bytes attack the bottleneck, and QAT lets the model adapt to the rounding it is served with (page 17). K2.5 (arXiv 2602.02276) adds native vision (early fusion at 10:90 vision to text beat late fusion at 50:50) and Agent Swarm, a trainable orchestrator over frozen sub-agents scored by "critical steps" (main-agent steps plus the slowest sub-agent per stage), for up to 4.5 times lower latency [K2.5]. K2.6 scales it to 300 sub-agents [card], and K2.7 Code (June 2026) is a coding-tuned K2.6 with about 30% fewer thinking tokens [card]. The configs of K2 Thinking, K2.5, K2.6 and K2.7 Code all keep K2's shape (61 layers, 384 experts, 8 active plus 1 shared, 64 heads, width 7,168), so K3 is the first redesign of the flagship.

GLM: deep-narrow MoE, Muon and slime

Zhipu AI (Z.ai, with Tsinghua) moved from dense GLM models to MoE with GLM-4.5 (28 July 2025; the report followed in August): 355B total and 32B active, a 106B/12B "Air" variant, 23T tokens, and hybrid reasoning 混合推理 (thinking and non-thinking in one model), aimed at ARC: agentic, reasoning and coding [GLM-4.5]. GLM-4.6 (30 September 2025, context 128K to 200K) and GLM-4.7 (22 December 2025) keep the 4.5 shape in their configs (92 layers, 160 experts, 96 heads, 8 KV heads) and target coding and agents; the 4.6 card cites the GLM-4.5 report as its technical report. GLM-4.5: the opposite bet from Kimi

  • Deeper, narrower. "Reduce the width (hidden dim and # routed experts) and increase its height (# layers)", because "deeper models exhibited better reasoning capacity": 92 layers at width 5,120 with 160 experts, against K2's 61 layers at 7,168 with 384.
  • More heads than the width suggests. 96 heads of dimension 128 at width 5,120, \(96\cdot128/5120 = 2.4\) times the width (the report says 2.5); see the heads card above.
  • Conventional attention, V3-style MoE. GQA with 8 KV heads, partial RoPE and QK-Norm "to stabilize the range of attention logits", one MTP layer, loss-free sigmoid routing, and Muon with cosine decay (WSD underfit SimpleQA and MMLU in early experiments) [GLM-4.5, §2].
Deep-narrow MoE (GLM-4.5)
Problem
With a parameter budget, spend on width (hidden size, experts) or depth?
Idea
Fewer experts and a narrower hidden size than V3 and K2, more layers, more heads.
Why it works
The report's reason is reasoning capacity, stated as an observation; we found no depth-versus-width ablation in its architecture section.

Final-model benchmarks (rank 3 overall, 2 on agentic tasks in its Fig. 1 as of July 28, 2025).
Cost

GLM-4.5, 4.6, 4.7, Air.

Post-training and slime

Separate reasoning, agent and general experts are trained first, then unified by self-distillation. Reasoning RL uses GRPO without KL. Against growing output length in stages (16K to 64K), a single stage at 64K scored higher (83.4 versus 80.6 on AIME 2024), because short early stages caused an "unlearning" later stages did not repair. Agentic RL gives zero reward for a malformed tool call and puts loss only on model-generated tokens [GLM-4.5, §3].

slime is the RL framework behind GLM-4.5 to 5.x (page 25): a Megatron trainer, SGLang rollout engines and a data buffer that run colocated and synchronous for reasoning RL, where on-policy batches matter, or disaggregated and asynchronous for slow agentic rollouts that would leave a synchronous trainer idle. Training is BF16 with FP8 rollouts quantized online after each update, a deliberate mismatch to monitor (page 25) [GLM-4.5, §3.5], [slime].

5.1 to 5.3 (7 April, 16 June and 18 August 2026 in Z.ai's release notes): GLM-5.1 targets "hundreds of rounds and thousands of tool calls" [card]. GLM-5.3 reuses the 5.2 base: "every gain comes from post-training" [card], under a custom MIT-style license with a security review for very large model-as-a-service operators.

Its config lists 45 layers in a 3:1 pattern, 34 linear-attention layers it labels KDA to 11 DSA layers, MLA without rotary dimensions (kv_lora_rank 512, qk_rope_head_dim 0), 64 heads, and 288 routed experts with 8 active plus 1 shared. Read as K3's layout at a smaller size (our reading): GLM held to full-attention MLA with DSA through GLM-5.2 and now ships a linear hybrid, the direction Kimi took and the opposite of MiniMax's M2.

Why the hybrid was abandoned

MiniMax-01's case: pure linear attention "demonstrates limited retrieval capabilities", so one softmax layer in eight restores recall. For M2 the team went back to full attention in all 62 layers for three reasons [M2 report, §2.2.2]. Evaluation: hybrids matched full attention on MMLU, BBH, MATH and LongBench at small scale, but larger scale showed "clear deficits" on multi-hop reasoning, and proxy metrics were fragile. Infrastructure: linear layers are memory-bound even in training, sensitive to low-precision state, and awkward with prefix caching and speculative decoding. Sliding-window hybrids: M2's own experiments with them degraded retrieval, multi-hop reasoning and in-context learning after hundreds of billions to trillions of tokens of continued pretraining. The report's Table 2, full attention versus a sliding-window hybrid at the M2 architecture scale, shows why suites miss it: MMLU 85.5 versus 85.6, but 128K common-words extraction 90.0 versus 72.0. It adds that "as context lengths grow and GPU compute scaling slows, sub-quadratic attention will become increasingly relevant", and M3 took the sparse route (MSA).

M2 otherwise follows the V3 lineage (256 fine-grained experts, 8 active, sigmoid gating with a learnable bias). Its ablation shows 32 experts with top-2 versus 128 with top-8 improving MATH 19.6 to 24.1 [M2 report, Tab. 1], the direction of K2's sparsity law.

CISPO and the M1 recipe

CISPO (derived on page 24) fixes a failure seen in M1's zero-RL runs [M1, §3.1]. PPO-style clipping zeroes the gradient of a token once its ratio has left \([1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]\) on the side its advantage pushes toward (above \(1+\epsilon\) when the advantage is positive), and reflection-starting tokens ("Wait", "Recheck") are rare in the base model, so one update pushes their ratio out and they are clipped from the 16 later off-policy updates. CISPO instead clips the importance weight and applies it as a stop-gradient constant on a plain policy-gradient term, so every token keeps a gradient; on Qwen2.5-32B it matched DAPO's AIME 2024 score with half the steps. M1's engineering outlives the algorithm: an FP32 language-model head lifted the train-inference probability correlation from 0.987 to 0.997 (page 25); AdamW with \(\beta_2 = 0.95\) and \(\epsilon = 10^{-15}\) because gradients spanned \(10^{-18}\) to \(10^{-5}\) (page 14); and early truncation once 3,000 consecutive tokens exceed probability 0.99. The RL run took three weeks on 512 H800 GPUs, about USD 534,700. On the M2 line, Forge is MiniMax's agent-native RL system (windowed FIFO scheduling, prefix-tree merging of shared rollout prefixes, white-box and black-box agents) [M2 series report].

OLMo: fully open, and what that buys you

Ai2's product is the recipe, not the score. OLMo 2 (7B, 13B, 32B [OLMo 2]) and Olmo 3 (7B and 32B, with a 3.1 refresh [Olmo 3]) are 全开源: weights, training data, code and recipes, logs and (OLMo 2) thousands of intermediate checkpoints. Olmo 3 calls it the model flow: "every stage, checkpoint, data point, and dependency" used to build the family.

"Fully open" versus open weights
Problem
With open weights you can run and fine-tune a model, but not tell which data produced a behavior, rerun one stage with a change, or check a claim about training dynamics.
Idea
Release the whole flow: pretraining, mid-training and long-context data (Dolma 3 Mix, Dolmino, Longmino), post-training data (Dolci), training, data-processing, decontamination and evaluation code, logs, and checkpoints at each stage.
Why it matters
Intervention at any stage becomes possible. Evidence
OLMo 2's stability study (page 11) and Olmo 3's RL-Zero are results only an open model could publish and others could check.
Cost
Release effort and a smaller frontier: Olmo 3.1 Think 32B trails Qwen 3 32B on GPQA (57.5 versus 67.3) and MMLU (86.4 versus 88.8), ties on AIME 2024 (80.6 versus 80.8) and leads on AIME 2025 (78.1 versus 70.9) and IFBench (68.1 versus 37.3) [Olmo 3, Tab. 1].
Used by
OLMo, and efforts the report compares with (Marin, Apertus). I did not verify that the Chinese labs or NVIDIA publish logs and per-stage checkpoints.

The recipe. A conventional dense decoder with the stability set of page 11 (QK-norm, reordered norm, z-loss at \(10^{-5}\) as page 11 settles, no weight decay on embeddings, AdamW \(\epsilon = 10^{-8}\), and a plain \(N(0, 0.02)\) initialization for every parameter instead of depth-scaled) [OLMo 2, §3]. OLMo 2 mid-trains on Dolmino Mix 1124 in several anneals with different data orders and averages the models: three of 50B tokens for the 7B, and three of 100B plus one of 300B for the 13B and 32B [OLMo 2, §2.3]. OLMo 2-Instruct applies the Tulu 3 recipe of SFT, DPO and RLVR (multi-stage RLVR here) [Tulu 3]. Olmo 3 adds sliding windows of 4,096 on three of four layers, with YaRN on the full layers only. Pretraining uses Dolma 3 Mix (5.9T tokens for the 7B, 5.5T for the 32B), mid-training 中期训练 uses 100B tokens (two seeds merged for the 32B, echoing OLMo 2's "souping" 权重平均 of anneals), and 65K-context extension uses 50B to 100B tokens, partly from olmOCR science PDFs. Post-training is SFT, DPO on contrastive pairs ("delta learning"), then RLVR with OlmoRL: the GRPO fixes of page 24 plus asynchronous continuous batching, a 4 times faster RL loop. Olmo 3.1 Think continued that run from 750 to 2,300 steps (21 more days on 224 GPUs) for over 4 more AIME points and 20 on IFBench, at 5 points lower AlpacaEval.

RL-Zero. Open RLVR studies mostly start from models that "do not reveal their pretraining or mid-training data", so contamination confounds them: if mid-training data holds the evaluation, spurious rewards can be "as effective as true reward" (page 24). Olmo 3 runs RLVR straight from its 7B base on decontaminated Dolci RL-Zero sets; because the base data is public, the decontamination can be checked and a gain attributed to RL. It is 7B only [Olmo 3, §2.2, §6].

Olmo Hybrid. A controlled hybrid-versus-transformer comparison, released open like the rest: Olmo 3 7B with its 75% sliding-window layers swapped for Gated DeltaNet layers (with negative eigenvalues), so 3 linear layers per full-attention layer, trained on up to 6T tokens. It matches Olmo 3 7B's MMLU with 49% fewer training tokens and gains 14.1% on RULER at 64K, and the authors' fitted scaling laws project 1.3 to 1.9 times fewer tokens for equal loss from 1B to 70B parameters [Olmo Hybrid, §1, §4.1]. Its long-context test stops at 64K, so it does not settle the 128K to 1M range.

Cost, as wall-clock. The report declines a GPU-hour headline (it cites DeepSeek-V3's USD 5.576M) and gives elapsed time: about 56 days for Olmo 3 Think 32B on 1,024 H100s (about 47 pretraining, about 9 post-training). At USD 2 per H100-hour, \(1024 \times 56 \times 24 \times 2 = 2{,}752{,}512\), the report's USD 2.75M, a best-recipe replay excluding research [Olmo 3, §2.4].

Nemotron: Mamba hybrids, FP8, then FP4

NVIDIA optimizes tokens per second per GPU, designing around what its hardware does badly (moving a KV cache) and well (low-precision matrix multiplies). The lineage: Nemotron-H (8B, 56B, pruned 47B [Nemotron-H]), then Nemotron 3 [family paper]: Nano 30B-A3B, Super 120B-A12B (report April 2026 [Super]) and Ultra 550B-A55B (June 2026 [Ultra]).

Nemotron-H: 8% attention, FP8 pretraining, MiniPuzzle

Mostly Mamba-2, a few attention layers
Problem
Attention's per-token cost and KV cache grow with context, and reasoning models write long outputs.
Idea
About 8% of layers are attention (4 of 52 in the 8B, 10 of 118 in the 56B), "evenly dispersed"; the rest alternate Mamba-2 and FFN. GQA with 8 KV heads and no position embeddings at all (the Nemotron 3 paper says Mamba provides implicit position).
Why it works
Mamba-2 layers use constant compute and memory per token, so batches can be bigger: at 65,536 input and 1,024 output tokens on H100s the 56B generates 2.4 times more tokens per second per GPU than Qwen-2.5-72B and Llama-3.1-70B (2.9 times after pruning).
Evidence
Against a same-data transformer, the 8B hybrid is higher on 7 of 15 tasks and within a point on 4 more (MMLU 72.8 versus 73.2); the 56B beats Llama-3.1-70B on 16 of 17 [Nemotron-H, §2.1, §2.5].
Cost
State-space layers recall poorly (page 09), and prefix-caching and speculative-decoding tooling is less mature.
Used by
Nemotron-H, Nano 2, Nemotron 3; the same idea at other ratios in Jamba, Kimi Linear and K3.

FP8 pretraining. The 56B was NVIDIA's first fully FP8 run (20T tokens) with the coarsest recipe: per-tensor dynamic scaling, E4M3 for weights and activations, E5M2 for gradients, BF16 for the first and last four layers. The loss gap against BF16 was typically below 0.1%. Two lessons: loss was "not a reliable predictor" (FP8 matched or beat BF16 downstream), and the recipe had to be validated at 8B or larger because "results with smaller models did not generalize" [Nemotron-H, §2.3]. MiniPuzzle prunes the 56B to 47B for a target of 1M tokens of context on a 32 GiB GPU in FP4 (the 56B's FP4 weights alone take 29.5 GiB): it scores layers by the MSE their removal causes, prunes layers and FFN neurons (pruning Mamba heads "caused severe accuracy degradation"), and distills for 63B tokens. The 47B keeps 5 of 10 attention layers and is 1.2 times faster at near-equal accuracy, with about 300 times fewer tokens than training from scratch [Nemotron-H, §3]. Pruning 剪枝 plus distillation is the standard way to derive a family from one big run (page 19).

Nemotron 3: LatentMoE and NVFP4

LatentMoE: route in a smaller width, buy more experts
Problem
Low-latency serving is bound by reading expert weights (\(d\times m\) each), throughput serving by all-to-all volume \(\propto d\times K\). Quality tracks \(K\cdot m\), and more experts and a larger \(K\) add combinations.
Idea
Project tokens from \(d\) to \(\ell\) (\(W_\downarrow\in\mathbb R^{\ell\times d}\)), route and run experts in width \(\ell\), project back; reinvest \(\alpha = d/\ell\): \(N' = \alpha N\), \(K' = \alpha K\). Router, shared expert and non-expert layers stay at \(d\).
Why it works
Payload per token \(K'\ell = Kd\) and routed weights \(N'm\ell = Nmd\) are unchanged, while subsets grow from \(\binom NK\) to \(\binom{\alpha N}{\alpha K}\ge\binom NK^{\alpha}\). The task's effective feature rank lower-bounds \(\ell\); \(\alpha\) up to 4 held quality.
Evidence
At about 8B active, 73B total and 1T tokens, standard (\(d=4096\), 128 experts, top-6) versus LatentMoE (\(\ell=1024\), 512 experts, top-22): MMLU-Pro 48.30 to 52.87, MMLU 70.10 to 72.11, at 8.09B versus 8.02B active [Nemotron 3, Tab. 1].
Cost
Two extra projections and a rank floor; the evidence is the authors' and at moderate scale.
Used by
Nemotron 3 Super and Ultra [Elango et al. 2026]; Kimi K3 (\(\ell=3584\), \(\alpha=2\)).

Super and Ultra. Super (120.6B/12.7B) has 88 layers, which its config shows as 40 Mamba-2, 40 LatentMoE and 8 attention (2 KV heads, 512 experts, top-22, \(\ell=1024\)), trained on 25T NVFP4 tokens with WSD and AdamW [Super, §2]. Ultra (550B/55B) has 108 layers (48 Mamba-2, 48 LatentMoE, 12 attention), \(\ell=2048\), 20T tokens after a cut from 25T, and reports 5.9 times GLM-5.1's and 4.8 times Kimi K2.6's output throughput at 8K input and 64K output on GB200, but trails Qwen-3.5-397B on prefill-heavy inputs, where its 3.2 times larger active count costs FLOPs [Ultra]. NVFP4 uses E2M1 elements in 16-element blocks with E4M3 scales, 2D block scaling for weights, a random Hadamard transform on wgrad inputs and stochastic rounding on gradients. The final 15% of layers (for stability at scale), latent projections, MTP, attention projections and embeddings stay BF16, and the Mamba output projection MXFP8 to avoid underflow (NVFP4 flushed up to 40% of its values to zero in Nano) [Super, Tab. 3], [Nemotron 3]. A hybrid-specific serving cost: an FP16 Mamba state cache with ordinary rounding caused a verbosity problem that stochastic rounding fixed [Super, §4]. Nemotron 3.5 Lightning (30B-A3B, August 2026) carries the recipe down: interleaved Mamba-2 and MoE layers with a few attention layers (2 KV heads), MTP and NVFP4 pretraining, and its base card reports RULER at 1M tokens of 69.6 against 51.2 for Nemotron 3 Nano [card].

Ultra's two divergences

Divergence 1 (about 8T tokens) [Ultra, §2.7]: to move data-parallel gradient reduction to BF16 for throughput, the team had dropped the output layer's gradient accumulation from FP32 to BF16, and the MTP blocks' contribution to that shared layer's weight gradient (loss factor 0.05 each) was "essentially lost" at BF16's 7 mantissa bits; the MTP-2 loss spiked first. The fix was a rollback and FP32 reduction. Divergence 2 (about 16T) had no determined cause; rolling back to 15T and annealing the learning rate at once mitigated it (so the horizon fell from 25T to 20T), and an all-BF16 rerun still diverged, so it was not an FP4 problem. Reusable diagnostics: MaxVio (max expert load over mean, ceiling \(E/k = 512/22 = 23.27\); Ultra's worst layer rose from 4.8 to about 12 by 12T, median near 1.2) and residual-stream norms (early-layer norms began spiking from about 7.5T tokens and sharply near 11T).

Three lessons. The attention fraction is not the cache: MiniMax-01 (12.5% of layers) needs 40 KiB but K3 (26%) needs 27, because MLA's 576 elements are a quarter of GQA-8's 2,048. Converged. Sigmoid routing with a bias instead of a large auxiliary loss and 8 or more active fine-grained experts (K2, GLM-4.5, M2, Nemotron 3); about 3:1 hybrids with a minority of global layers (K3, GLM-5.3-Flash, Olmo Hybrid, Olmo 3, Step 3.5 Flash); specialists consolidated by on-policy distillation (K3, Ultra, GLM-5's last stage); four-bit weights (MXFP4 QAT, NVFP4 pretraining). Open. How to buy long context (Olmo Hybrid is the cleanest equal-recipe comparison but stops at 64K; none at 1M exists, page 09), depth versus width, head count, WSD versus cosine, whether to keep clipping once the optimizer is fixed, and how much openness is worth.

Common misconceptions

  • "MuonClip is a new optimizer." It is Muon (weight decay, update RMS matched to 0.2 by \(0.2\sqrt{\max(n,m)}\)) plus QK-Clip, a post-update per-head weight rescale that fires only when a head's max logit exceeds \(\tau\). In K2 it fired for 12.7% of heads early and was inactive after 70K steps.
  • "QK-Clip rescales the layer." The report's layer-wide form was replaced by a per-head factor because only a few heads explode. With max logits 250, 80, 60, 30 and \(\tau=100\), only the first head changes.
  • "K2 halved the heads because heads do not matter." Its ablation shows 0.5% to 1.2% lower loss for doubling them, GLM-4.5 reports reasoning-benchmark gains, and V3's choice filled a roofline gap. Heads are a cost knob to price for your hardware, and the 83% comes without an accounting.
  • "More experts is free." The sparsity law counts FLOPs, not the memory and all-to-all traffic of extra experts, which is why K2 stopped at 48 and why LatentMoE exists.
  • "MiniMax showed linear attention fails." M2 reported failures of its own sliding-window hybrids and infrastructure gaps, while Kimi Linear and K3 ship 3:1 linear hybrids at scale. The lesson is about evaluating at length, after SFT, on multi-hop tasks.
  • "Open weights means fully open." A license (MIT, modified MIT, community) says nothing about data, code, logs or intermediate checkpoints; Olmo 3 defines fully open as the whole model flow.
  • "A hybrid's cache follows its attention fraction." It is layers × elements × bytes: Ultra's 11% of layers with 2 KV heads costs 12 KiB per token, MiniMax-01's 12.5% with 8 KV heads costs 40 KiB, and recurrent layers add a fixed state you must keep precise.
  • "CISPO is PPO without clipping." It clips the importance weight and detaches it, so every token keeps a (slightly biased) gradient, whereas PPO clipping zeroes the gradient of tokens whose ratio has left the range on the side their advantage pushes toward, including rare reflection tokens.

Interview questions

Warm-up A head has a max attention logit of 250 and \(\tau = 100\). What does QK-Clip do to its weights, and to which other heads?

\(\gamma = \min(1, \tau/S) = 100/250 = 0.4\). With the usual \(\alpha = 0.5\), \(W_q\) and \(W_k\) of that head are each multiplied by \(\sqrt{0.4} = 0.632\), so the logit scales by \(0.632 \times 0.632 = 0.4\) and the head's max logit on the same inputs becomes exactly \(0.632 \cdot 0.632 \cdot 250 = 100\). Heads with \(S \le 100\) have \(\gamma = 1\) and are bitwise unchanged, because the rule is per head, not per layer. Only the product of the two factors matters for the logit: \(\alpha = 0.25\) gives 0.795 and 0.503, whose product is again 0.4.

For an MLA head the factors are \(\sqrt{\gamma}\) on \(q^C\) and \(k^C\), \(\gamma\) on the head-specific rotary query \(q^R\), and 1 on the shared rotary key \(k^R\), so both terms of the logit shrink by \(\gamma\) and no other head is affected. The rescale happens after the optimizer step and uses the max logit from that step's forward pass, so the forward and backward passes of the current step are unchanged.

Warm-up What does "fully open" mean for OLMo, and why does it matter for research?

It means the whole model flow is public: weights, the pretraining, mid-training and long-context data, the post-training data, training and data-processing code, evaluation code, training logs, and checkpoints at each stage. Open weights give you only the end of the pipeline, so you can run and fine-tune the model but cannot say which data produced a behavior, rerun one stage with a change, or check a claim about training dynamics.

Concrete uses: OLMo 2's spike-score ablations (reordered norm plus QK-norm, no weight decay on embeddings) can be checked against released checkpoints and logs. Olmo 3's RL-Zero runs RLVR straight from a base model whose pretraining data is public and decontaminated, so a gain can be attributed to RL rather than to leaked evaluation data (the spurious-rewards problem). Reasoning traces can be traced back to training data. The caveats: a license is not openness (MIT, Kimi's modified MIT and a community license say nothing about data or logs), and openness does not guarantee the strongest model. Olmo 3.1 Think 32B trails Qwen 3 32B on GPQA (57.5 versus 67.3) but leads on IFBench (68.1 versus 37.3).

Warm-up Compute the KV cache per token for Kimi K2 and for Kimi K3, and the cache at 1M tokens.

K2 has 61 MLA layers, each caching a 512-dim latent plus a 64-dim rotary key, 576 elements: \(61 \times 576 \times 2\text{ B} = 70{,}272\) B \(= 68.6\) KiB per token, or 68.6 GiB at \(2^{20}\) tokens. K3 has 93 layers but only 24 are (gated) MLA; the other 69 are KDA layers with a fixed-size state. So \(24 \times 576 \times 2 = 27{,}648\) B \(= 27\) KiB per token, 27 GiB at 1M, 2.5 times smaller than K2 (\(70{,}272/27{,}648 = 2.54\)). The KDA layers add a fixed state of \(96 \times 128 \times 128 \times 4\text{ B} = 6\) MiB each (fp32 assumed), \(69 \times 6 = 414\) MiB per sequence, which does not grow with context. (K3's 576 elements per MLA layer follow page 09's accounting.)

Core What is MuonClip and why did Kimi need it?

MuonClip is Muon with weight decay and update-RMS matching (each update scaled by \(0.2\sqrt{\max(n,m)}\) to match AdamW's RMS so its learning rate and decay transfer) plus QK-Clip. Muon's token efficiency (about 2 times AdamW in Moonlight) came with a failure at scale: attention logits exploded, exceeding 1,000 within about 15,000 steps in a 9B-active run, which usually means loss spikes and occasional divergence. The usual fixes did not fit. QK-Norm needs materialized keys, which absorbed MLA does not have, and a soft-cap does not stop \(q\cdot k\) from growing before the cap.

QK-Clip rescales, after each update, the query and key weights of any head whose batch max logit \(S^h_{\max}\) exceeds \(\tau\): by \(\gamma_h = \tau/S^h_{\max}\), split as \(\gamma^{1/2}\) on each (MLA: \(q^C, k^C\) by \(\sqrt\gamma\), \(q^R\) by \(\gamma\), shared \(k^R\) untouched). It touches no activations, is per head so healthy heads are not taxed, and was only transiently active: with \(\tau=100\), 12.7% of heads triggered it in the first 70K steps and it was inactive afterward, and K2 trained 15.5T tokens with no spikes. Muon is worse than Adam, K2 hypothesizes, because its update has a flat singular spectrum and grows the spectral norm of \(W_q, W_k\) more readily. Other labs fixed the same symptom differently: GLM-5 splits Muon's orthogonalization per head (and reports stable logits without clipping), DeepSeek-V4 normalizes q and k, and K3 combines per-head Muon with K2's clipping.

Core Why did Kimi K2 use 64 attention heads instead of DeepSeek-V3's 128? Show the attention arithmetic at 128K.

Under absorbed MLA, attention FLOPs per cached token scale with the head count while bytes read do not. One cached key costs \(2n_h(576+512)\) FLOPs per layer: 139,264 for 64 heads and 278,528 for 128. At 128K over 61 layers that is \(139{,}264 \times 131{,}072 \times 61 = 1.11\) TFLOP per generated token versus 2.23 TFLOP, against only \(2N_{\text{act}} = 65.2\) GFLOP for everything else. So attention is 17 times the rest of the forward pass at 64 heads, and the two are equal at about 7,700 tokens.

K2 reports that going from 64 to 128 heads at 128K, with 384 experts, costs 83% more inference FLOPs, while doubling heads (heads equal to layers versus twice the layers, in small runs) lowers validation loss only 0.5% to 1.2%, and with sparsity 48 already strong the report judged that gain not worth the inference cost. My reconstruction of the 83%: absorbed decode gives +95% (the extra 5.24B parameters of the wider projections add 10.5 GFLOP), MHA-mode prefill with causal average context gives +76%, and 83% sits between. MHA-mode decode with the parameter count held fixed gives +83.4%, so that may be the report's accounting, but the report does not say. The counter-argument is V3's: decode is bandwidth-bound, and 128 heads sit at 242 FLOPs per byte against an H800 ridge point near 295 (64 heads: 121), so the extra FLOPs were partly free there. GLM-5 says the choice is hardware-specific and takes a third route, fewer heads (64) with a larger head dimension.

Core What is CISPO and what problem does it address?

CISPO (MiniMax-M1) addresses the loss of rare, important tokens under PPO/GRPO clipping. In M1's zero-RL runs GRPO failed to grow long chains of thought. Tokens that start reflection ("However", "Recheck", "Wait") are low-probability in the base model, so a single update raises their probability ratio above \(1+\epsilon\) and the clipping zeroes their gradient in all later off-policy updates (M1 does 16 per generation batch), which are exactly the tokens the model needs to learn.

CISPO starts from REINFORCE with a stop-gradient importance weight and clips that weight, \(\hat\rho = \text{clip}(\rho, \cdot, 1+\varepsilon^{IS}_{\text{high}})\), multiplying \(\hat A\,\log\pi_\theta\). Every token keeps a gradient, the weight only bounds variance, and the lower bound was dropped. The price is a slightly biased gradient and no trust region, so stability leans on the cap and gradient clipping. On Qwen2.5-32B zero-RL it matched DAPO's AIME 2024 score with half the steps. The derivation and comparison with DAPO's clip-higher and GSPO are on page 24; M1's other important fixes were an FP32 language-model head (train-inference probability correlation 0.987 to 0.997) and \(\epsilon = 10^{-15}\) in AdamW.

Core Work through LatentMoE: \(d = 4096\), a standard MoE with 128 experts and top-6. What changes with \(\ell = 1024\), and why does it help?

\(\alpha = d/\ell = 4\). Tokens are projected to width 1,024 for routing and expert computation, then projected back; the router, shared expert and attention stay at 4,096. The saving is reinvested: experts \(N' = 4 \times 128 = 512\), top-\(K' = 4 \times 6 = 24\) (the paper used 22). Per-token all-to-all payload is \(K'\ell = 24 \times 1024 = 24{,}576\), the same as \(6 \times 4096\) (with 22 it is 22,528, 0.92 times). Routed expert weights are \(512 \times 1024 \times m\) versus \(128 \times 4096 \times m\), identical. So communication and memory traffic are unchanged while the number of expert subsets grows from \(\binom{128}{6} = 5.4\times10^{9}\) to \(\binom{512}{24} \approx 10^{41}\) (\(10^{38.4}\) at top-22; our computation), and quality tracks the effective nonlinear budget \(K\cdot m\), which is held.

Reported effect at about 8B active and 73B total parameters on 1T tokens: MMLU-Pro 48.3 to 52.9, MMLU 70.1 to 72.1, at 8.09B versus 8.02B active. Limits: the effective feature rank of the task lower-bounds \(\ell\) (\(\alpha\) up to 4 held quality), the two projections cost a little, and the result is from the authors at moderate scale. Kimi K3 adopted it with \(\ell = 3584\), \(\alpha = 2\), so 8 active experts became 16 at the same payload (\(16 \times 3584 = 8 \times 7168\)).

Core Why did Kimi K2 not use DualPipe, what did it use instead, and why is its expert parallelism only 16-way?

DualPipe feeds micro-batches from both ends and holds two copies of the parameters and gradients per device. For a model above 1T parameters that memory forces more PP (more bubbles) or more EP (more overhead), so the report calls the cost "prohibitively high". K2 uses interleaved 1F1B with virtual stages and extra warm-up micro-batches so the EP all-to-all overlaps with compute, and it decouples weight-gradient computation from each backward pass to overlap it with pipeline communication. The layout is 16-way PP, 16-way EP and ZeRO-1, a 256-GPU (32-node) model-parallel group; parameters in BF16 plus a FP32 gradient buffer are about 6 TB (\(1.04\text{T} \times 6\) B), so about 24 GB per GPU before optimizer state.

EP is the smallest feasible because with 64 heads K2's attention is shorter than V3's, leaving less compute to hide all-to-all behind, so the EP operations must be short, and a smaller EP group relaxes expert-balance constraints. Our inference: V3 needed expert grouping to limit nodes per token at 64-way EP over 8 nodes, whereas 16-way EP spans only 2 nodes if its ranks are packed together (the report does not say), consistent with K2 dropping grouping.

Core What did K2's sparsity scaling law show, and what does it not capture?

Holding activated parameters fixed (8 routed plus 1 shared expert, so constant FLOPs) and varying only the number of experts, under Muon, loss falls consistently as sparsity (total over active experts) rises. At validation loss 1.5, sparsity 48 needs 1.69 times, 1.39 times and 1.15 times fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8, 16 and 32, so returns diminish (32 to 48 gives 1.15 times). K2 picked 48 (8 of 384 experts) "to balance model performance with cost", the cost being infrastructure complexity.

What it does not capture: the law measures loss per FLOP, but extra experts cost memory, optimizer state and all-to-all traffic, and serving pays these even when FLOPs are equal. The experiments are small-scale controlled runs. Sparsity has kept rising since: K3 uses 896 experts with 16 active (56), made affordable in communication by LatentMoE, while other labs ship 20 to 36 (GLM-4.5 20, V3 and GLM-5 32).

Deep-dive Why might a lab move from a linear-attention hybrid back to full attention? What would you test before agreeing?

MiniMax-01 shipped a 7:1 lightning hybrid, and M2 went back to full attention in all 62 layers for three stated reasons. Evaluation: at small scale hybrids matched full attention on MMLU, BBH, MATH and LongBench, but at larger scale showed clear deficits on complex multi-hop reasoning, and proxy metrics were fragile. Infrastructure: linear layers are memory-bound even in training, sensitive to low-precision state storage, and awkward with prefix caching and speculative decoding (StepFun independently gave the speculative-decoding reason for choosing windows). Sliding-window hybrids trained on hundreds of billions to trillions of tokens still lost retrieval and multi-hop accuracy. Their Table 2 (full attention versus a sliding-window hybrid) shows why a benchmark suite misses it: MMLU 85.5 versus 85.6, but RULER at 128K common-words extraction 90.0 versus 72.0.

I would not read this as a verdict on linear attention, because Kimi Linear (3:1 KDA, MMLU-Pro 51.0 versus 47.2, RULER 128K 84.3 versus 81.3 at 1.4T tokens) and K3 ship such hybrids, and MiniMax's own M2 report expects sub-quadratic attention to matter more as context grows (M3 chose sparse attention). Before agreeing I would test at 2 to 4 times the target length, after SFT and RL, on multi-hop and aggregation tasks, with the same tokens and recipe for both arms; measure real decode throughput with speculative decoding and prefix caching on; and check state precision. The conclusion depends on what you measure.

Deep-dive Your 500B hybrid MoE run diverges around 8T tokens, and the MTP loss spikes first. Where do you look?

This is Nemotron 3 Ultra's first divergence. MTP heads share the output layer with the main head, so a first MTP spike points to that shared weight's gradient path before anything architectural. Check the wgrad norm by parameter group and the dtype of local gradient accumulation and of the data-parallel reduction. Ultra's team had moved output-layer accumulation from FP32 to BF16 for throughput, and the MTP blocks' contribution (loss factor 0.05 each) was "essentially lost" at BF16's 7 mantissa bits. Our elaboration: an addend below half a unit in the last place (0.2% to 0.4% of the running sum, \(2^{-9}\) to \(2^{-8}\)) rounds away, so a small, consistently signed term can vanish while the main term keeps updating, and the heads drift out of agreement. The fix was rolling back to a good checkpoint and reducing in FP32.

General playbook: log MaxVio (max expert load over mean; ceiling \(E/k = 23.3\) for 512 experts, top-22) and per-layer residual norms as leading indicators (Ultra's early-layer residual norms began spiking from about 7.5T tokens); separate precision suspects from data and optimizer suspects by rerunning the segment with all tensors in BF16 (the second Ultra divergence at 16T survived that, so it was not an FP4 problem); and if the cause stays unknown, roll back and anneal the learning rate earlier, which is what they did by cutting the horizon from 25T to 20T tokens.

Deep-dive Design question: you must pick the attention stack for a 1M-context agent model. Compare the choices these five labs made and say what you would build.

Use bytes per token and risk as the axes (the KV table in the synthesis section). Full or sparse attention (MiniMax-M2, GLM-5.2, M3): the best-understood quality, but the cache stays at 88 to 250 KiB per token (M3: 120 KiB), so 1M tokens is 88 to 250 GiB per sequence and sparse selection only saves bandwidth and FLOPs. 3:1 windowed hybrid (Olmo 3, Step 3.5): a 4 times cache cut (64 KiB), simple, but MiniMax saw such hybrids degrade past 32K after SFT. KDA or GDN hybrid with MLA global layers (Kimi K3): 27 KiB plus a 0.4 GiB state at 1M, NoPE, positive matched-budget results from Kimi, Qwen and Ai2 (Olmo Hybrid), GLM-5.3-Flash now shipping the same layout, and the price is recurrent-state infrastructure. Mamba-2 hybrid with about 11% GQA layers and 2 KV heads (Nemotron 3 Ultra): 12 KiB plus a 0.4 GiB state, the best throughput claims, and the same state-precision and MTP-plus-SSM serving work.

I would make the K3-style hybrid the main bet with a full-attention twin at matched tokens as the control, and keep a windowed or sparse arm as the low-risk fallback. The evaluation plan is what the M2 report argues: per-length RULER and multi-hop tasks out to 2 to 4 times the target length, after SFT and RL, with speculative decoding and prefix caching enabled, since infrastructure readiness decided MiniMax's outcome. Choose head counts by the ridge point of the serving hardware (K2, GLM-5), and only then decide whether 64, 96 or 128 heads is worth its FLOPs.
