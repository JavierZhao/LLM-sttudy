Architecture atlas

P0 ~35 min Prereqs: 07 KV-efficient attention, 08 MoE, 09 Beyond full attention, 26 to 32 (the case studies)

Thirty open configurations from GPT-3 to DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash side by side, with parameters, KV bytes per token and FLOPs per token recomputed from each released config.json. Read it to know the numbers an interviewer expects you to have in your head (head dimension 128, eight KV heads, RoPE bases, expert widths), what the field converged on and why, and which choices are still open.

What interviewers probe
  • Typical numbers on demand. "What head dimension, how many KV heads, what FFN ratio, how many experts and how many active, what vocabulary and RoPE base would you pick for a new model?" A good answer gives the modal value, the spread, and the reason.
  • Config to numbers in a minute. Given a config.json: total and activated parameters, KV bytes per token, FLOPs per token. Which \(N\) you mean, and whether the headline count is exact.
  • Convergence with reasons. Why RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, GQA-8, a large vocabulary, MoE at the frontier, QK-norm; what each replaced and which paper introduced it.
  • Open questions with experiments. Attention family, MoE granularity, shared experts, balancing, optimizer, local-to-global ratio: what evidence exists and what run would settle it.
  • Exactness. "405B" is 405.85B, Kimi K2's report says 1.04T while its config gives 1.026T. Can you reconcile a headline with a config, and say which gap is a convention and which is a discrepancy?

After this page you can

  • State from memory the modal value and the spread of head dimension, KV heads, \(d_{ff}/d\), vocabulary size, RoPE base, routed experts and top-\(k\), expert width relative to \(d\), and tokens per parameter.
  • Compute total parameters, activated parameters, KV bytes per token and FLOPs per token from a config.json, and say which convention you used.
  • Explain each convergent choice in one sentence (the problem it solved) and point to the page that derives it.
  • Name six open design dimensions and, for each, the evidence so far and the experiment that would settle it.
  • Reconcile a headline parameter count with a config in three worked cases (Kimi K2, GLM-4.5, MiniMax-M2).
  • Implement load_config, params, kv_bytes_per_token and flops_per_token, and reproduce 8,030,261,248 for Llama-3-8B and 671,026,404,352 for DeepSeek-V3.

How to read the atlas

The atlas has 30 rows in four tables that share one row order: 12 dense models, then 18 sparse, hybrid or compressed-attention models, each block in release order. DeepSeek-V3 and V3.2 share a row (the backbone is the same; V3.2 adds sparse attention). Table 1 gives identity and scale, Table 2 the attention block, positions and norms, Table 3 the FFN and experts, Table 4 the derived columns. A single table with 35 columns is unreadable, so read the four as one table cut into vertical bands. The two shaded rows are the course's reference models: Llama-3-8B (pages 04 and 06) and DeepSeek-V3 (pages 07, 08, 27).

Where each number comes from. Architecture cells are read from the released config.json (for gated repositories, an ungated mirror of the same file, named in the Sources column) and checked against the technical report or model card when one exists. Advertised quantities (parameters as named, training tokens, context length) come from the report or card and are labeled as such. Where the report and the config disagree, both are shown; nothing is guessed, and "n/d" means the primary source does not state it. Every row was compared with the finished page that treats it, and the counts agree with those pages (for example 671,026,404,352 for V3 on page 08, 235,741,434,880 for V2 on page 26, 235,093,634,560 for Qwen3-235B on page 30, and for gpt-oss-120b 116.79B without biases on page 08 against the checkpoint's 116,829,156,672 on page 31).

Conventions of the derived columns. They are the drill's, so you can reproduce any cell (Hands-on).

  • Recount. Decoder only: norm gains are parameters, rotary tables and other buffers are not, and the vision tower and any multi-token-prediction (MTP) module are excluded. Total is every weight; activated is total minus the routed experts a token does not select.
  • Three activated counts. Without either vocabulary matrix (non-embedding), with the output head only, or with both matrices. Sources use different ones: Qwen3-235B's "22B" is the both-matrices count (22.19B) while MiniMax-M2's "9.8B" is the non-embedding count (9.80B). Table 1 shows what each source says, Table 4 shows the span.
  • KV bytes per token. bf16, the marginal growth per token once every window is full, so only global layers count. MLA caches \(d_c + d_h^R\) numbers per layer (page 07). The GiB at 128K includes the fixed part held by windowed layers.
  • GFLOPs per token at 8K. \(2 N_{\text{act}}\) (input table excluded, output head included) plus causal attention averaged over an 8K sequence: \(2\,n_h (d_{qk} + d_v)\) FLOPs per cached key, over \(T/2\) keys in a global layer and \(w - w^2/2T\) keys in a layer with window \(w\). A multiply-add is 2 FLOPs (pages 04, 34).
  • Tokens per activated parameter. Reported training tokens over the reported activated parameters. Chinchilla's compute-optimum is about 20 (page 13). Conventions move the ratio by a few percent: page 29 divides Maverick's 22T by the recounted 17.18B (1,281) and page 34 divides V3's 14.8T by the reported 37B (400); the table uses the reported count throughout (1,294 for Maverick).

What the numbers already say

What the field converged on, and why

A "standard" 2025 dense decoder is the LLaMA block of 2023 (Touvron et al.), with three later changes. Take GPT-3's block (pre-LayerNorm, GELU MLP with a \(4d\) hidden layer, learned absolute positions, biases, full multi-head attention) and replace each part: LayerNorm by RMSNorm 均方根归一化 (Zhang and Sennrich, 2019), the GELU MLP by a gated SwiGLU (Shazeer, 2020), learned positions by rotary embeddings 旋转位置编码 (Su et al., 2021), drop the biases, then share keys and values across query groups (GQA 分组查询注意力, Ainslie et al., 2023) and enlarge the vocabulary. The three later changes are QK-norm on queries and keys, a MoE FFN at large scale, and a cheaper attention for long context. Table 5 counts each choice in the atlas.

Three points on the table deserve care. Stability tools that spread: QK-norm before soft-capping
Problem
Attention logits grow with training, which sharpens softmax rows and causes loss spikes at large learning rates.
Idea
Normalize \(q\) and \(k\) per head (QK-norm), or cap logits with \(c\tanh(z/c)\) (Gemma 2: \(c = 50\)), or clip the weights after the optimizer step when a head's logits explode (Kimi's QK-Clip).
Why it works
With unit-RMS \(q, k\) and gains \(g\), \(|z| \le g^2\sqrt{d_h}\) for any weights, so the bound holds by construction; a cap bounds the range only and its gradient vanishes past \(c\) (page 11, page 31).
Evidence
Gemma 3 replaced Gemma 2's soft-cap by QK-norm [Gemma 3, §2]; Qwen3 added QK-norm while dropping QKV bias [Qwen3, §2]; Kimi K2 used QK-Clip under Muon [Kimi K2, §2.1]; the V4 report says its RMSNorm on q and KV entries makes QK-Clip unnecessary (page 28).
Cost
Two norms per attention layer, and keys must be materialized, so it does not fit absorbed MLA without applying the norm to latents.
Used by
OLMo 2, Gemma 3 and 4, Qwen3 and Qwen3-Next, GLM-4.5, MiniMax-M2 (all with QK-norm); Gemma 2 (soft-cap); K2 (QK-Clip); V4 (RMSNorm on q and KV).

Where designs still disagree

Below the shared skeleton, the 2025 to 2026 releases differ on the choices that set inference cost and long-context behavior. These are the dimensions an interviewer will ask you to argue about; for each, the table gives the options present in the atlas, the evidence that exists, and the experiment that would settle it.

Attention for long context: five families in production
Problem
At 128K to 1M tokens decode reads the whole KV cache at every step and serving capacity is set by cache bytes per sequence, not by FLOPs.
Idea
The cache is a product: (global layers) × (numbers cached per layer) × (bytes per number) × (tokens read). Each family attacks one factor. GQA-8: numbers per layer, 2,048 (Llama). MLA: 576 numbers (V3, K2, K3). Windows: only 1 of every 6 layers global (Gemma 3, 4), 1 in 2 (gpt-oss, Gemma 2), 1 in 4 with 8,192-token chunks (Llama 4). Linear hybrids: cache in 1 layer of 4 (Qwen3-Next, 3.5, 3.8-Flash-Next), 1 of 8 (MiniMax-M1) or 24 of 93 (K3). Sparse and compressed: read only top-2,048 (V3.2) or a 4× to 128× compressed cache (V4), reuse one layer's cache in several layers and store it in FP4 (V4.1).
Why it works
The factors multiply, so designs stack: K3 stacks a 3:1 hybrid on MLA (27,648 B), V4.1-Flash stacks compression, selection, cross-layer reuse and FP4 (890 B).
Evidence
Per-token bytes in Table 4. Against hybrids: MiniMax-M2 went back to full attention in all 62 layers after larger-scale hybrid runs showed deficits in multi-hop reasoning [MiniMax-M2 report, §2.2.2]; MiMo's sliding window of 512 collapsed after context extension while 128 did not (page 09). For hybrids: Kimi Linear's KDA-to-MLA ablation favors 3:1 over pure MLA (page 09).
Cost
Hybrids and sparse designs need new kernels, prefix-caching and speculative-decoding support, and lose exact recall; an indexer is linear in \(T\) (page 28); compression loses detail a query may need.
Used by
GQA-8: Llama, Qwen3, GLM-4.5, M2, gpt-oss. MLA: V2, V3, K2, K3 (with KDA). Windows: Gemma, gpt-oss, Mistral 7B, Llama 4. Linear hybrids: Qwen3-Next, 3.5, K3, MiniMax-M1. Sparse or compressed: V3.2, V4, V4.1, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next.
MoE granularity and the shared expert
Problem
At fixed activated compute, more stored parameters give lower loss, but each extra expert adds all-to-all traffic, memory and imbalance.
Idea
Cut experts finer and route among more of them: sparsity (routed over active) from 4 in Mixtral to 16 (Qwen3-235B), 32 (V3, gpt-oss, M2), 48 (K2), 51 (Qwen3-Next), 56 (K3), 64 (V4-Pro) and 128 (Maverick, which routes top-1 plus a shared expert). Optionally add always-on shared experts: none (Mixtral, Qwen3-235B, gpt-oss, M1, M2), one (V3, K2, GLM-4.5, Maverick, Qwen3-Next, V4), two (V2, K3).
Why it works
For a fixed number of activated experts, the number of expert combinations grows combinatorially, and Kimi's sparsity scaling law measures the gain: to reach validation loss 1.5, sparsity 48 needs 1.69×, 1.39× and 1.15× fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8, 16 and 32 [Kimi K2, §2.3].
Evidence
The scaling law is one lab's, under Muon. The shared expert has thin evidence: DeepSeekMoE's 2B model lost a lot when its shared expert was switched off at test time, but that is a probe, and Qwen3 dropped the shared expert without publishing a reason (page 30).
Cost
All-to-all bytes grow with active experts times \(d\) (page 15), each expert sees fewer tokens per batch, and V3 needed node-limited routing to bound traffic (page 27); V4 removed the limit by redesigning the parallelism (page 28).

From config.json to numbers

The interview form of this page is: "here is a config; how big is it, what does a token cost, how much does the cache take?" The recipe is five steps, each a line of the drill.

  1. Read \(L, d, n_h, n_{kv}, d_h\) (if head_dim is absent it is \(d/n_h\)), the FFN width, expert fields, vocabulary and tie_word_embeddings.
  2. Parameters. Per layer, GQA attention has \(2 d\, n_h d_h + 2 d\, n_{kv} d_h\) weights and a SwiGLU FFN \(3 d\, d_{ff}\); an MoE layer has \(3 d\, d_e\) per expert plus a router \(d \cdot E\). Add \(V d\) for each of the input table and the head.
  3. Activated. Subtract \((E - k)\, 3 d\, d_e\) per MoE layer; say whether the vocabulary matrices are in.
  4. KV per token. \(L_{\text{global}} \cdot 2\, n_{kv} d_h \cdot b\) for GQA, \(L\,(d_c + d_h^R)\, b\) for MLA, and zero for a layer whose window is full or that has no cache.
  5. FLOPs per token. \(2\,(N_{\text{act}} - Vd) + 2\, L\, n_h (d_{qk} + d_v)\, T/2\) for a causal sequence of length \(T\) with all layers global (\(N_{\text{act}}\) with both matrices; subtract the input table only when the head is a separate matrix).

Worked example: Qwen3-235B-A22B (config: \(L = 94\), \(d = 4096\), 64 query and 4 KV heads of 128, 128 experts of width 1,536 with top-8, no shared expert, \(V = 151{,}936\), untied, QK-norm with gains of size \(d_h\)).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{attention per layer} &= \underbrace{4096 \cdot 8192}_{W_Q} + \underbrace{2 \cdot 4096 \cdot 512}_{W_K, W_V} + \underbrace{8192 \cdot 4096}_{W_O} + \underbrace{2 \cdot 128}_{\text{QK-norm}} = 71{,}303{,}424 \\ \text{router} &= 4096 \cdot 128 = 524{,}288, \qquad \text{one expert} = 3 \cdot 4096 \cdot 1536 = 18{,}874{,}368 \\ \text{one layer} &= 71{,}303{,}424 + 2 \cdot 4096 + 524{,}288 + 128 \cdot 18{,}874{,}368 = 2{,}487{,}755{,}008 \\ \text{total} &= 94 \cdot 2{,}487{,}755{,}008 + 2 \cdot 151{,}936 \cdot 4096 + 4096 = 235{,}093{,}634{,}560 \end{aligned} \]

The idle experts are \(94 \cdot (128 - 8) \cdot 18{,}874{,}368 = 212{,}902{,}871{,}040\), so the activated count with both vocabulary matrices is 22,190,763,520: the report's "22B". Without either matrix it is 20.95B, with the head only 21.57B. KV per token is \(94 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 192{,}512\) B (188 KiB), 23.5 GiB for one 128K sequence. FLOPs per token at \(T = 8192\): \(2 \cdot 21{,}568{,}433{,}664 = 43.14\) GFLOPs for the matmuls plus \(94 \cdot 2 \cdot 64 \cdot (128 + 128) \cdot 4096 = 12.62\) GFLOPs of attention, 55.75 GFLOPs in all (Table 4). Attention is already 23% of the token's FLOPs at 8K and equals the matmul term at \(T \approx 28\text{K}\) (\(43.14/12.62 \cdot 8192\)).

Config traps
  • Nesting. Multimodal releases (Maverick, Gemma 3 and 4, K3, Qwen3.5) put the language model under text_config; the vision tower is excluded here (Maverick's checkpoint holds 871,932,416 parameters more than its text tensors).
  • Missing keys. No head_dim: \(d/n_h\). No num_key_value_heads: MHA. No tie_word_embeddings: the Hugging Face default is tied. Llama 2 has no rope_theta.
  • MoE layer placement. first_k_dense_replace and moe_layer_freq (DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM), decoder_sparse_step with mlp_only_layers (Qwen3), interleave_moe_layer_step and moe_layers (Llama 4), all layers (Mixtral, gpt-oss, M2).
  • Inactive fields. sliding_window is set but off in Qwen2.5 (use_sliding_window: false) and null in Mixtral 8x22B. max_position_embeddings is not the trained length: 163,840 for V2 and V3 (128K trained), 10.24M for M1 (1M trained), 40,960 for Qwen3 (32,768 native).
  • File versus model. Checkpoints hold MTP layers with their own embeddings and heads (GLM-4.5), FP8 scale tensors (Kimi K2, V3) and vision towers. Compare with the headline only after accounting for these.
  • Vocabulary size. vocab_size is the number of embedding rows and can differ from the number a report states (Gemma 2: 256,000 rows in the file, 256,128 in the report).

Common misconceptions

  • "The number in the model's name is its parameter count." It is a rounded name: 405B is 405.85B, Llama 2 70B has 68.98B, and Kimi K2's advertised 1.04T is 1.026T in its config. Recount from the config and say which pieces are in.
  • "Activated parameters means the same thing on every model card." Qwen3-235B's 22B includes both vocabulary matrices (22.19B); MiniMax-M2's 9.8B excludes them (9.80B); Maverick's 17B includes 2.07B of embedding and head parameters; Gemma 4 26B-A4B's 3.8B includes its tied table. State the convention before comparing.
  • "GQA cuts attention FLOPs." It cuts the KV cache and the K and V projections. Every query head still scores every key, so attention FLOPs are unchanged (the drill's test checks this).
  • "KV cache per token is one number per model." It is one number per layer type. Windowed layers stop growing when the window fills, linear layers have a fixed state, and cross-layer reuse shares one cache among several layers. Use the marginal growth for capacity planning and the total at your context for memory.
  • "Everyone moved to MLA." Four rows use it (V2, V3, K2, and K3 in a quarter of its layers), and Mistral Large 3 and Small 4 adopted it (page 31). Llama, Qwen, GLM, MiniMax and OpenAI stayed with GQA, and DeepSeek itself replaced MLA in V4.
  • "Head dimension is \(d/n_h\)." Only in the Llama line. Qwen3, GLM-4.5 and gpt-oss set it explicitly, and Gemma 4 uses two head dimensions in one model.
  • "max_position_embeddings is the usable context." It is the size of a table. Effective length can be far lower: Llama 4 Scout claims 10M and measures 1K on NoLiMa (page 29).
  • "More experts always means more quality." Sparsity scaling holds at fixed activated compute and ignores communication; Kimi's own law puts the gain from sparsity 32 to 48 at 1.15× in FLOPs, and V3 needed node-limited routing to keep all-to-all affordable.

Interview questions

Warm-up What does a "standard" 2025 dense LLM block look like, and where did each component come from?

A pre-norm decoder: \(x \leftarrow x + \text{Attn}(\RMSNorm(x))\), then \(x \leftarrow x + \text{FFN}(\RMSNorm(x))\), with a final RMSNorm before the output head. Attention is grouped-query with 8 KV heads of dimension 128, rotary positions, no biases, and (since 2025) QK-norm; the FFN is SwiGLU with hidden width 3.25 to 5 \(d\); the vocabulary has 128K or more entries.

Provenance: the pre-norm residual layout and the dense-decoder recipe from GPT-2 and GPT-3 (Brown et al. 2020); RMSNorm from Zhang and Sennrich (2019); SwiGLU from Shazeer (2020); RoPE from Su et al. (2021); pre-RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE and no biases together in LLaMA (Touvron et al. 2023); GQA from Ainslie et al. (2023), adopted at 70B in Llama 2; the 128K vocabulary in Llama 3 (128,256); QK-norm from ViT-22B (2023), reaching open LLMs in OLMo 2, Gemma 3 and Qwen3 in 2024 to 2025. Then say what changes at scale: the FFN becomes a fine-grained MoE and attention gets a cheaper form for long context (Tables 5 and 6). Llama 3.1 8B is the reference: 32 layers, \(d = 4096\), 32/8 heads of 128, \(d_{ff} = 14{,}336\), 8,030,261,248 parameters.

Warm-up Calculate the KV cache per token, and for a 128K sequence, of Llama 3.1 405B, Qwen3-235B-A22B and DeepSeek-V3.

bf16, 2 bytes. Llama 3.1 405B (126 layers, 8 KV heads of 128): \(2 \cdot 126 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 516{,}096\) B = 504 KiB per token; at \(2^{17}\) tokens, \(504 \text{ KiB} \cdot 131{,}072 = 63\) GiB. Qwen3-235B (94 layers, 4 KV heads of 128): \(94 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 192{,}512\) B = 188 KiB, 23.5 GiB. DeepSeek-V3 (61 layers, MLA, \(d_c + d_h^R = 512 + 64 = 576\)): \(61 \cdot 576 \cdot 2 = 70{,}272\) B = 68.6 KiB, 8.58 GiB.

V3's cache is 7.3× smaller than 405B's although V3 has 1.65× as many parameters: MLA caches 576 numbers per layer where GQA-8 caches 2,048, and V3 has 61 layers against 126. For scale, GPT-3's 96 layers of 96 heads of 128 cache 4,718,592 B (4.5 MiB) per token.

Warm-up Why do so many models use exactly 8 KV heads and a head dimension of 128?

Eight KV heads has two documented reasons (page 07). Llama 2's ablation at 30B and 150B tokens found GQA matching MHA on most tasks and beating MQA on average, and, decisive in practice, the largest models are served with 8-way tensor parallelism across the 8 GPUs of a node: MQA's single KV head would be replicated on every GPU, while 8 KV heads gives one per GPU with no replication. Llama 3 kept 8 at every size, and Mistral, Qwen3, GLM-4.5, M2, gpt-oss and Maverick followed (14 of the 30 rows).

The head dimension 128 is a convention with a clear pedigree (GPT-3 already used 96 heads of 128 at \(d = 12{,}288\), and Llama keeps \(n_h d_h = d\)), not a derived optimum. The atlas shows the exceptions and what each buys: gpt-oss uses 64, Gemma 4 and the Qwen3-Next family use 256, V4 uses one 512-dimensional KV head with 64 or 128 query heads. What you should say is the quantity that binds, bytes of cache per token, \(L \cdot 2\, n_{kv} d_h \cdot b\): halving \(d_h\) and doubling \(n_{kv}\) leaves it unchanged, so the pair \((n_{kv}, d_h)\) is a choice about quality per cached byte and about how the heads shard across GPUs.

Core Given only a config.json, how do you estimate parameters, KV cache and FLOPs per token?

Read \(L, d, n_h, n_{kv}, d_h, d_{ff}\), the expert fields, \(V\) and whether embeddings are tied. Parameters: per layer \(2 d\, n_h d_h + 2 d\, n_{kv} d_h + 3 d\, d_{ff}\) (GQA plus SwiGLU), times \(L\), plus \(Vd\) or \(2Vd\), plus small terms (norms, router). Check on Llama 3.1 8B: one layer is \(2 \cdot 4096 \cdot 4096 + 2 \cdot 4096 \cdot 1024 + 3 \cdot 4096 \cdot 14{,}336 = 218{,}103{,}808\); times 32 is 6,979,321,856; plus \(2 \cdot 128{,}256 \cdot 4096 = 1{,}050{,}673{,}152\) and 266,240 norm gains gives 8,030,261,248. For MoE, replace the FFN by \(3 d\, d_e (E + n_{\text{shared}})\) plus the router, and subtract \((E - k)\, 3 d\, d_e\) per layer for the activated count. KV per token: \(L \cdot 2 n_{kv} d_h \cdot b\), or \(L (d_c + d_h^R) b\) for MLA, counting only global layers. FLOPs per token: \(2 N_{mm}\) plus \(2 L\, n_h\, (d_{qk} + d_v)\, T/2\), where \(N_{mm}\) excludes the input table.

Then state the caveats: the file's tensor count can differ from the headline (MTP layers, vision tower, FP8 scales), "activated" has three conventions (Qwen3-235B's 22B counts both vocabulary matrices, M2's 9.8B counts neither, and if none matches within about 2% look for a vision tower, a parallel dense MLP as in Gemma 4, or an MTP module), and attention FLOPs matter mainly at long context (they equal the matmul term at about 28K tokens for Qwen3-235B). Worked: Qwen3-235B-A22B gives 235,093,634,560 total, 22.19B activated, 192,512 B of KV per token and 55.75 GFLOPs at 8K (section config). The convention matters: tokens per activated parameter and \(6 N_{\text{act}} D\) move by 5% to 14% between the non-embedding and both-matrices counts (Qwen3-235B: 20.95B against 22.19B; M2: 9.80B against 11.03B; Maverick: 15.12B against 17.18B).

Core Compare DeepSeek-V3 and Llama 3.1 405B on FLOPs per token, weight memory and KV per token. What does each tell you about serving?

FLOPs per token at 8K: V3 93.7 GFLOPs (activated 36.63B with the head, so 73.3 for the matmuls, plus 20.5 of attention at the decompressed MLA cost); Llama 405B 841.3 GFLOPs (\(2 \times 403.75\text{B} = 807.5\) plus 33.8). V3 does 9.0× less compute per token. Weight memory: the released V3 checkpoint is FP8 with 684.5B elements (the 671.03B recount plus the MTP module), about 685 GB, against 812 GB for 405B in bf16 (405.85B × 2), so both need a similar number of GPUs just to hold weights. KV per token: 70,272 B against 516,096 B, 7.3× less.

Serving: prefill is compute-bound, so V3 is about 9× cheaper per token. Decode is memory-bound and reads the weights of every expert that any sequence in the batch selects. At batch 1 V3 reads only its activated 37B parameters (about 37 GB in FP8) against 812 GB for the dense model, but once the batch reaches about a hundred nearly all experts are hit and V3 reads about 685 GB per step, the same order as 405B (pages 08, 34). MoE therefore wins training cost and prefill outright, and wins decode through its smaller KV cache and, at small batch, its fewer bytes read, provided you have enough parallelism to hold and shard the experts.

Core Which design dimensions are still unsettled, and what evidence would settle them?

Pick three and be specific. (1) The attention family for 128K to 1M contexts: GQA, MLA, windows, linear hybrids, and sparse and compressed designs all ship. The evidence is per-lab and mostly at small scale or at 32K and below; MiniMax reported deficits in multi-hop reasoning for hybrids at larger scale and went back to full attention in M2, while Qwen, Kimi and DeepSeek went the other way. What settles it: an equal-recipe comparison at 100B+ parameters and 1M context with recall-heavy, length-swept evaluations after SFT and RL. (2) MoE granularity and shared experts: Kimi's sparsity law shows gains from 8 to 48 at fixed activated compute, but under Muon and without communication cost, and the shared-expert evidence is a probe of one trained model. What settles it: a scaling study that includes all-to-all time and MFU 模型浮点运算利用率, with and without the shared expert. (3) The optimizer: Muon appears in K2, K3 and V4 together with fixes (QK-Clip, RMSNorm on q and KV), but there is no equal-token, tuned-baseline comparison at scale.

Also name the residual-stream redesigns (mHC, attention residuals, gated residuals) as the 2026 frontier, with single-lab evidence. A strong answer says what it would run, at what scale, and which metric decides.

Core Why did expert widths shrink and expert counts grow from Mixtral to DeepSeek-V3, Kimi K2 and Qwen3-Next? What limits it?

Expert width relative to \(d\) went from 2.67 (Mixtral 8x22B: 16,384 over 6,144) to 0.29 to 0.3 (V3, K2, V2, GLM-4.5) and 0.25 (Qwen3-Next), while routed experts went from 8 to 256, 384, 512 and 896 (K3), and sparsity from 4 to 32, 48, 51 and 56. The activated FFN width per token stayed at 2.4 to 4 \(d\) (V3: \(8 \cdot 2048 + 2048 = 18{,}432 = 2.57d\)), so the FLOPs per token are those of a dense FFN, and the extra experts add capacity without adding FLOPs. The reasons: many more expert subsets per token (\(\binom{256}{8} \approx 4 \times 10^{14}\) against \(\binom{8}{2} = 28\)), finer specialization, and, per the Kimi K2 report, lower loss at equal FLOPs.

The limits are systems. Each active expert adds \(d\) values of dispatch and combine traffic per layer (V3 caps it at 4 nodes per token). At decode each expert sees few tokens, so its matmuls are bandwidth-bound, and once the batch is large every expert's weights are read at every step. Load imbalance grows with the expert count. K3 answers with LatentMoE (project from 7,168 to 3,584 before routing, activate 16 of 896 at the same all-to-all payload as K2's 8), and V4 removes V3's node limit and pays with a redesigned parallelism (pages 27 and 28).

Core Why did RoPE bases rise from \(10^4\) to \(5 \times 10^5\), \(10^6\) and \(10^7\), and what does partial RoPE change?

Pair \(i\) of a head rotates at \(\theta_i = b^{-2i/d_h}\), so its wavelength is \(2\pi b^{2i/d_h}\) positions and the slowest pair of a 128-dimensional head has a wavelength of about \(2\pi b\): 54,000 at \(b = 10^4\), 2.6M at \(5 \times 10^5\), 5.1M at \(10^6\), 49M at \(10^7\) (page 05). Slow pairs are the ones that can carry long-range information; a base whose slowest wavelength is barely above the context leaves few of them. Models trained at 4K to 8K with \(b = 10^4\) (Llama 2, Mistral 7B, DeepSeek-V2 and V3, which extend with YaRN) therefore need interpolation to reach long contexts; Llama 3 raised the base to \(5 \times 10^5\) following Xiong et al.'s adjusted-base-frequency result and then rescaled by 8 for 128K (page 10); Qwen2.5, Qwen3, Mixtral and GLM use \(10^6\); the long-context hybrids and MiniMax-M1 use \(10^7\). The base and the context are chosen together, and page 10 shows the analytic rules undershoot how far to raise it.

Partial RoPE rotates only a fraction of each head (GLM 50%, Qwen3-Next 25%, M2 64 of 128, V4 64 of 512, Gemma 4 global layers 25%), leaving the other channels position-free. With MLA it is required rather than chosen: the rotary part must be a separate decoupled key so that weight absorption can fold the content path into the latent (page 07).

Deep-dive You must serve 32 concurrent 1M-token sequences with about 300 GB of HBM free for the cache. Which attention design do you choose, and why?

Budget: 300 GB over 32 sequences is 9.4 GB per sequence, about 9,000 B per token at \(2^{20}\) tokens. Use the marginal bytes of Table 4 (decimal GB). A GQA-8 model with Llama 405B's 126 layers needs 516,096 B per token, 541 GB per sequence, 58 times too big. An MLA model like V3 needs 70,272 B, 73.7 GB per sequence, 8 times too big. A 3:1 KDA hybrid on MLA (K3) needs 27,648 B, 29.0 GB per sequence, 3 times too big: 10 sequences. Only the compressed designs fit: V4-Pro caches about 4,970 B per token (page 28; 5.2 GB per sequence, so 32 sequences need about 167 GB; page 09 gets 5.18 GB with windows), and V4.1-Flash reports 890 B per token of global cache (0.93 GB per sequence).

So the arithmetic decides: at this budget you need compression and selection, not a hybrid alone. The alternatives are a hybrid plus an FP8 or FP4 cache plus more memory, or offloading cold prefixes to SSD as V4 does (page 28). Then name the trade-offs: recall after compression (needle tasks pass, multi-hop tasks need testing), an indexer that is linear in \(T\) unless keys are compressed first, kernel and prefix-cache support, and bandwidth. Every step reads the cache: 32 sequences of 5 GB is 160 GB per step, 20 GB per GPU when sharded over 8, or 6 ms at 3.35 TB/s, which is why the read set and not only the stored size bounds decode speed.

Deep-dive The report says Kimi K2 has 1.04T parameters but your recount from the config gives 1.026T. How do you resolve it, and what would you say in an interview?

First separate convention from error. The recount (1,026,408,209,408) uses the released config: 61 layers with the first dense, 384 routed and 1 shared expert of width 2,048 in the other 60, 64 heads, \(V = 163{,}840\), untied. It agrees with the checkpoint's tensor metadata (1,026,470,731,056) up to 62.5M elements of FP8 scale factors, so it describes what was released. The report's Table 2 gives 1.04T and its Table 4 gives 1,043B; the recipe returns 1,043B exactly if all 61 layers are counted as MoE layers with 385 experts, 16.6B more (page 08), and 1.04T is the rounding of that. I would say: "the config gives 1.026T; the report's figure matches an overcount of the dense first layer, so I would quote 1.026T and note the report's 1.04T."

The general procedure is (1) recount from the config, (2) compare with the checkpoint metadata and account for scale tensors and MTP or vision parts, (3) test conventions (embeddings, tied or not), and (4) if a gap remains, state it as unresolved rather than choosing. GLM-4.5 resolves exactly by MTP accounting (358.34B file, 355B named); MiniMax-M2's 1.2B gap between 229.9B and 228.69B equals two vocabulary matrices but is not confirmed.
