Back-of-envelope calculations

P0 ~45 min, plus the drills Prereqs: 04 Params & FLOPs, 06 KV cache, 07 MLA, 08 MoE, 13 Scaling laws, 15 Parallelism

Frontier-lab interviews ask you to size things on the spot: how many parameters, how many GPU-hours, how much memory, how fast does it decode. This page collects the constants to memorize, one formula card for every quantity, and 34 worked drills whose numbers agree with every earlier page, so that each answer is a short chain of multiplications you can do aloud.

What interviewers probe
  • Sizing with no notes: "How long does it take to train a 70B model on 15T tokens on 2,048 GPUs?", "How many GPUs to serve 32 users at 32K context?", "How fast does an 8B model decode?" The score is on the process (assumptions, units, arithmetic, a sanity check) more than on the last digit.
  • Which constant, exactly: dense versus sparse peak, bf16 versus fp8, GB versus GiB, bidirectional versus one-way bandwidth, and which \(N\) (total, non-embedding, activated). Each of these is a factor of 1.07 to 2 that a careless answer silently absorbs.
  • Which resource binds: compute, HBM bandwidth, HBM capacity, or the network. A strong answer computes all of them and takes the largest time.
  • Cross-checks against public numbers: Llama 3 405B's \(3.8 \times 10^{25}\) FLOPs, DeepSeek-V3's 2.788M GPU-hours, MFU in the 30% to 45% band. A number that contradicts them is a bug in your assumptions.
  • Sensitivity: "What changes if we double the batch, the context, or the MFU?" You should answer with a ratio before touching the arithmetic.

After this page you can

  • Recite the hardware constants: H100 SXM at 989.4 dense bf16 TFLOP/s and 1,979 fp8, 80 GB at 3.35 TB/s (ridge 295 FLOPs per byte), NVLink at 450 GB/s per direction, InfiniBand at 50 GB/s, with the A100, H200, B200 and GB200 counterparts.
  • Write, with its assumptions, the formula for each of: parameters (dense, MLA, MoE total and activated), FLOPs per token, training time and cost, training and inference memory, KV bytes per token, decode and prefill time, ring all-reduce, pipeline bubble, MoE all-to-all bytes, and the Chinchilla optimum.
  • Pick the binding resource for training, prefill, decode, gradient sync and MoE dispatch, and say what efficiency (MFU or fraction of roofline) to expect.
  • Reproduce the headline numbers of the earlier pages from scratch: 8,030,261,248 parameters, 128 KiB of KV per token, 208 tokens/s at batch 1, 507K GPU-hours for Llama-3-8B, 67.7 days for 405B on 16K GPUs at 40% MFU.
  • Run a five-step protocol (assume, bound, compute, check, sensitivity) and cross-check any answer against a public number.
  • Implement the formulas as code, with tests that reproduce the worked answers.

The method: assume, bound, compute, check

A sizing question is a product of a few quantities that you can look up or state: a config, a token count, a hardware peak, an efficiency, a price. What an interviewer scores is whether you choose the right quantities, keep the units straight, and notice when the result is implausible. Use the same five steps every time:

  1. Restate the quantity and the unit you will report (GPU-days, GB, tokens/s, dollars).
  2. Assume out loud, as numbers. The architecture (\(L, d, n_{kv}\), ...), bytes per element, the hardware peak (dense, in the number format you compute in), MFU 模型浮点运算利用率 or the fraction of the roofline, and the price. Where two conventions exist, name yours (for example which \(N\)).
  3. Bound. Decide which resource sets the time: FLOPs over achieved FLOP/s (compute), bytes over bandwidth (memory), bytes over link speed (network), or bytes over capacity (does it fit at all?). When unsure, compute all of them and report the largest time. That is the roofline 屋顶线模型 (page 16).
  4. Compute in scientific notation with two or three significant digits, and write each intermediate result so that a slip is easy to find.
  5. Check against an anchor you know (a public number, an earlier answer), against the units, and for sensitivity: "at 30% MFU instead of 40% this grows by 1.33×".

One or two significant digits is the right precision for most answers. What matters is the exponent and the biggest factor of two, and the factors of two hide in a short list of conventions: dense versus sparse peak (2×), bidirectional versus one-way bandwidth (2×), bits versus bytes (8×), GB versus GiB (1.074×), forward versus training FLOPs (3×), total versus activated parameters (18× for DeepSeek-V3).

Parameters and FLOPs: count shapes, name your \(N\)

Only weight matrices matter: the norm gains of Llama-3-8B are 266,240 parameters, and biases do not exist. The one real ambiguity is which \(N\). For Llama-3-8B there are three defensible values: the total (8.03B), the count without the input table (7.50B), and the non-embedding count without either vocabulary matrix (6.98B). At 15T tokens, \(6ND\) is 7.23, 6.75 or \(6.28 \times 10^{23}\) FLOPs, a 7% to 13% spread. The FLOP-correct choice is the matmul weights plus attention (page 04). The shortcut \(13Ld^2 + 2Vd\) is exact only for a model whose attention is \(2.5d^2\) and whose FFN is \(10.5d^2\), which is Llama-3-8B; rederive the coefficient for any other config (12.75 for 70B, 11.875 for 405B).

For a mixture-of-experts (MoE) 混合专家 model, memory follows the total and compute follows the activated count. DeepSeek-V3's 37B activated parameters (the report's figure; 37.55B with both vocabulary matrices) give \(6 \times 37\text{B} \times 14.8\text{T} = 3.29 \times 10^{24}\) FLOPs, 11.5 times less than Llama 3 405B, and the GPU-hour ratio of the two runs is 11.6 (page 27).

Memory: states, activations, then the KV cache

Training memory is model states (16 bytes per parameter for bf16 plus Adam) plus activations plus the fp32 logits (4.2 GB for one 8K sequence of Llama-3-8B) plus 10% for buffers and fragmentation. States dominate small clusters and activations dominate long sequences: at \(s = 8192\) the quadratic score-matrix term is 90% of the stored-everything total, which is why every serious run uses a fused attention kernel (page 16) and selective or full recomputation. Inference memory is weights plus the KV cache 键值缓存. Use 90% of HBM as usable, so the number of sequences that fit is \((72\text{ GB} - W)/(T\kappa)\) on an 80 GB GPU, and remember that capacity, not bandwidth, is what caps the batch in long-context serving.

Time: one division, and the largest of several

Training and prefill are single divisions of FLOPs by achieved FLOP/s. Decode is the maximum of a memory term and a compute term, and for a dense model at ordinary batch sizes the memory term wins: the weights are read once per step whatever the batch, while each sequence adds its own KV cache. For Llama-3-8B at 4K context one sequence costs \(0.537\text{ GB}/3.35\text{ TB/s} = 0.160\) ms per step, the weights cost 4.79 ms, and as \(B \to \infty\) throughput approaches \(\text{BW}/(T\kappa) = 6{,}240\) tokens/s per GPU. Compute overtakes memory only when \(B\) exceeds the ridge (about 295) and the context is short: with GQA-8 the attention part has arithmetic intensity 算术强度 4 FLOPs per byte, so above roughly 390 tokens of context the whole step stays memory-bound at any batch (page 06).

Communication: per-direction bandwidth, and which link

A ring all-reduce sends \(2(n-1)/n\) times the message from each GPU, so its time tends to \(2S/\text{BW}\) and does not grow with \(n\). Three things go wrong in practice: using NVLink's 900 GB/s bidirectional figure as a one-way rate (a factor of 2), forgetting the factor 2 of all-reduce itself, and forgetting which link a collective crosses (450 GB/s inside a node, 50 GB/s between nodes). Tensor parallelism's traffic relative to compute is \(\tfrac29 (t-1)\,C_{\text{eff}}/(\text{BW}\,h)\), which for Llama-3-70B at \(t = 8\) is 16% of layer compute on NVLink and 141% over InfiniBand (page 15): that ratio is the whole argument for keeping it inside a node.

Scaling laws and money

At 20 tokens per parameter, \(C = 6N \cdot 20N = 120N^2\), so \(N = \sqrt{C/120}\). This agrees with the Epoch AI refit of the parametric law within 10% from \(10^{22}\) to \(4 \times 10^{25}\) FLOPs, and the IsoFLOP valley is so flat that a factor of two in \(N\) costs only about 0.004 nats at \(10^{24}\) (page 13). Do not use the rounded constants printed in the Chinchilla paper for Approach 3: they imply 60 to 140 tokens per parameter, an artifact of rounding. Money is a product: GPU-hours times a price, or for serving, price times GPUs over tokens per second. Report the price you assumed.

Sanity checks, and how to say them out loud

  • Order of magnitude before arithmetic. \(10^{24}\) FLOPs is about \(7 \times 10^{5}\) GPU-hours. If your answer for a \(10^{24}\)-FLOP run is \(7 \times 10^{3}\) or \(7 \times 10^{8}\), stop and find the slip (usually a factor of 3600, or bits versus bytes).
  • Ratios before absolutes. "Doubling the context doubles the KV term, so throughput at large batch halves." "Going from an H100 to an H200 changes only bandwidth, so batch-1 decode speeds up 1.43×." An interviewer often wants the ratio, and you can state it in seconds.
  • Compute every bound and report the largest time. A decode answer that shows the memory time and the compute time (4.95 ms against 0.017 ms at batch 1) is more convincing than one that asserts "it is memory-bound".
  • Cross-check against a published number you can quote (table below). If your formula misses a public number by more than 30%, one of your assumptions is wrong, and finding which one is the answer.
  • Units at every step. GB or GiB, Gb or GB, FLOPs or FLOP/s, tokens or sequences, one-way or bidirectional. Say the unit next to each number as you write it.
  • State what you neglected and its size: "I ignore attention FLOPs (about 7% of \(6N\) at 8K for an 8B model), norms (0.003%), and communication that overlaps (1% to 8% of a step in page 15's plans)". Say the direction of the error: a roofline is a lower bound on time.
  • Give a range, not a point. Three MFU values (0.30, 0.40, 0.50) turn "507K GPU-hours" into "406K to 676K", which is what a planner needs.

One question, spoken through

"How many H100s would you need to train a 7B model on 2T tokens in two weeks?" Here is a complete answer, in the order you would say it.

  1. Restate. GPUs \(G\) such that \(6ND\) FLOPs finish in 14 days.
  2. Assume. \(N = 7\text{B}\), \(D = 2\text{T}\), bf16 dense peak 989.4 TFLOP/s, MFU 40%, so 396 TFLOP/s per GPU. Fourteen days is \(1.21 \times 10^{6}\) s. Price $2 per GPU-hour.
  3. Compute. \(C = 6 \times 7 \times 10^{9} \times 2 \times 10^{12} = 8.4 \times 10^{22}\) FLOPs. One GPU delivers \(3.96 \times 10^{14} \times 1.21 \times 10^{6} = 4.8 \times 10^{20}\) in that time, so \(G = 8.4 \times 10^{22}/4.8 \times 10^{20} = 175\), about 22 nodes (176 GPUs). That is \(8.4 \times 10^{22}/(3.96 \times 10^{14} \times 3600) = 59\text{K}\) GPU-hours, $118K.
  4. Does it fit? States are \(16 \times 7\text{B} = 112\) GB, more than 80 GB, so shard: ZeRO-1 over 176 GPUs leaves \(4N + 12N/176 = 28.5\) GB. Activations at 4K context with a fused kernel are \(34\,sbhL = 18\) GB for one sequence, plus 0.5 GB of fp32 logits (32K vocabulary). It fits.
  5. Network. bf16 gradients are 14 GB; a ring over 50 GB/s InfiniBand sends \(2 \times 175/176 \times 14 = 28\) GB, 0.56 s. With a 4M-token batch each GPU does 22,700 tokens, \(6N \times 22{,}700 = 9.5 \times 10^{14}\) FLOPs, 2.4 s per step, so the sync is 23% if exposed. It must overlap with the backward pass, which it can.
  6. Check and range. Llama 2 7B needed 184,320 A100-hours for 2.0T tokens, which is 39% of the A100's peak, so 40% is consistent; the same FLOPs on H100 at 40% are 59K GPU-hours. At MFU 30% I would need 234 GPUs and the bill grows to $157K. Either figure excludes restarts, ablations and the runs that chose the recipe.

Common misconceptions

  • "An H100 does 1,979 TFLOP/s in bf16." That is NVIDIA's headline with 2:4 structured sparsity. Dense bf16 is 989.4, and dense fp8 is 1,979. Using the sparse number halves every MFU you compute and understates every training time by 2×.
  • "NVLink is 900 GB/s, so a gradient all-reduce runs at 900." 900 GB/s is bidirectional per GPU. A ring uses one direction, 450 GB/s, and the all-reduce sends \(2(n-1)/n\) times the message. Using the bidirectional number, or forgetting the factor 2 of the all-reduce itself, is a factor-2 error each.
  • "6ND uses the parameter count on the model card." Which \(N\)? Total, without the input table, without both vocabulary matrices, or (for MoE) activated. For Llama-3-8B the choices span 7% to 13% of \(C\); for DeepSeek-V3 the gap between 671B and 37.55B is 18×.
  • "Decode speed follows FLOPs." It follows bytes over bandwidth. An H200 has the same tensor cores as an H100 and decodes 1.43× faster because its HBM is 1.43× faster, and at batch 1 an H100 uses 0.34% of its FLOP/s (1 FLOP per byte read, against a ridge of 295).
  • "Activated parameters set the memory and the GPU count." They set the FLOPs. DeepSeek-V3 needs 671 GB in fp8 even though a token touches 37.55B parameters, and by a batch of 64 a decode step already reads 87% of the routed experts.
  • "GB versus GiB does not matter." It is a 7.4% gap. Llama-3-8B in bf16 is 16.06 GB or 14.96 GiB, and the 32-user, 32K-context KV cache of a 70B model is 343.6 GB or 320 GiB. State the unit.
  • "40% MFU means the GPU is 40% busy." MFU counts only the FLOPs the model needs, against the dense peak of the format you compute in. Recomputation lowers it, and DeepSeek-V3's 34.6% on a bf16 basis is 17.3% of the fp8 peak it actually uses.
  • "The roofline is what I will measure." It is a lower bound on time. One measured H100 NVL decode point reaches 80% of its roofline, and page 25's engine is assumed at 64%. Quote the bound and say so.

Parameters and FLOPs

Warm-up D1. How many parameters does Llama-3-8B have? Go from the config: \(L = 32\), \(d = 4096\), \(n_h = 32\), \(n_{kv} = 8\), \(d_h = 128\), \(d_{ff} = 14{,}336\), \(V = 128{,}256\), untied embeddings, no biases.

Per layer, attention is \(2 d\, n_h d_h + 2 d\, n_{kv} d_h = 2 \cdot 4096 \cdot 4096 + 2 \cdot 4096 \cdot 1024 = 33{,}554{,}432 + 8{,}388{,}608 = 41{,}943{,}040\); the SwiGLU FFN is \(3 \cdot 4096 \cdot 14{,}336 = 176{,}160{,}768\); two norm gains add 8,192. Warm-up D2. Llama-3-8B: forward FLOPs per token, training FLOPs per token, and total training FLOPs for 15T tokens. Which \(N\) do you use?

Forward is \(2N = 2 \times 8.03 \times 10^{9} = 16.1\) GFLOPs per token (two FLOPs per weight). Training is \(6N = 48.2\) GFLOPs: forward 2, input gradient 2, weight gradient 2. Over 15T tokens, \(C = 6ND = 6 \times 8.03 \times 10^{9} \times 15 \times 10^{12} = \mathbf{7.23 \times 10^{23}}\) FLOPs.

Then state the convention. The embedding lookup costs no FLOPs, so the matmul weights are 7.505B (blocks plus LM head), giving \(2N_{mm} = 15.01\) GFLOPs. Attention scores add \(4LTd = 4.29\) GFLOPs at \(T = 8192\) (full count; 2.15 causal). With causal attention, training costs \(6 \times 7.505 \times 10^{9} + 6 \cdot 32 \cdot 8192 \cdot 4096 = 45.03 + 6.44 = 51.47\) GFLOPs per token, 6.8% above \(6N\). Choosing total, non-embedding (6.98B) or matmul-only \(N\) moves \(C\) between \(6.28\) and \(7.23 \times 10^{23}\), a 7% to 13% spread (page 04).

Core D3. Estimate Llama-3-70B's parameter count from its config (\(L = 80\), \(d = 8192\), \(n_h = 64\), \(n_{kv} = 8\), \(d_{ff} = 28{,}672\), \(V = 128{,}256\)), then say why "\(13Ld^2 + 2Vd\)" is off for it.

Per layer: attention \(2 \cdot 8192 \cdot 8192 + 2 \cdot 8192 \cdot 1024 = 134{,}217{,}728 + 16{,}777{,}216 = 150{,}994{,}944\); FFN \(3 \cdot 8192 \cdot 28{,}672 = 704{,}643{,}072\); norms 16,384. Total \(\mathbf{70{,}553{,}706{,}496}\), or 70.55B.

The rule \(13Ld^2 + 2Vd\) gives 71.89B, 1.9% too high, because the coefficient is not universal. It is (attention + FFN)/\(d^2\): for Llama-3-8B, \(n_{kv}d_h = d/4\) so attention is \(2.5d^2\) and the FFN is \(3 \times 3.5 = 10.5d^2\), total 13. Core D5. At what context length do attention scores cost as much as the linear layers in Llama-3-8B, and what share of the forward FLOPs are they at 8K and at 128K?

The linear layers cost \(2N_{mm} = 15.01\) GFLOPs per token (\(N_{mm} = 7{,}504{,}658{,}432\)). Attention per token is \(4LTd = 524{,}288\,T\) FLOPs counting every score, half that if causal. At \(T = 8192\): 4.29 GFLOPs, 22.2% of the forward pass (2.15 GFLOPs and 12.5% causal). At \(T = 131{,}072\): 68.7 GFLOPs, 82.1% (34.4 and 69.6% causal).

Memory

Warm-up D6. How much memory do the model states of a 70B dense model take with mixed-precision Adam, and how many 80 GB GPUs is that?

Sixteen bytes per parameter: 2 (bf16 weights) + 2 (bf16 gradients) + 4 (fp32 master) + 4 + 4 (Adam \(m\) and \(v\)). With \(N = 70.55\)B: \(16 \times 70.55 \times 10^{9} = 1{,}129\) GB, 1.13 TB. Divided by 80 GB that is 14.1 GPUs (15.7 at 90% usable) before a single activation is stored, so at least 16 GPUs just to hold them. Sharding, not fitting, is the issue: ZeRO-3 over 64 GPUs leaves \(1{,}129/64 = 17.6\) GB per GPU. The recipe is specific: Llama 3 accumulates gradients in fp32 (18 bytes), and DeepSeek-V3 keeps bf16 Adam moments (about 14). See page 15.

Core D7. You want to fine-tune Llama-3-8B on one 80 GB GPU. Compare training-state memory for full fine-tuning, LoRA rank 16 on all linear layers, and QLoRA.

Full: \(16 \times 8.03\text{B} = 128.5\) GB, which does not fit. LoRA: an adapter on a \(d_{in} \times d_{out}\) matrix has \(r(d_{in} + d_{out})\) parameters. Per layer the seven matrices (q, k, v, o, gate, up, down) sum to \(8192 + 5120 + 5120 + 8192 + 3 \times 18{,}432 = 81{,}920\), so \(16 \times 81{,}920 = 1{,}310{,}720\) per layer and 41,943,040 in total (0.52% of the model). Their states cost \(41.9\text{M} \times 16\) B \(= 0.67\) GB. The frozen bf16 base is 16.06 GB, so the total is 16.73 GB, 7.7× less than full.

QLoRA: the 6.98B linear parameters go to NF4 with double quantization at 4.127 bits: \(6.98 \times 10^{9} \times 4.127/8 = 3.60\) GB, plus 2.10 GB for the bf16 embedding and head (\(2 \times 525{,}336{,}576 \times 2\) B), plus 0.67 GB of adapter states: 6.37 GB, 20× less than full. The adapters are a rounding error; the frozen base is what QLoRA shrinks. Activations come on top and do not shrink (page 20).

Core D8. Activation memory for one 8,192-token sequence through Llama-3-8B: store everything, use a fused attention kernel, or recompute fully. Use Korthikanti's per-layer formula.

Per layer, storing everything costs \(sbh(34 + 5as/h)\) bytes. Here \(sbh = 8192 \cdot 1 \cdot 4096 = 33.55\)M and \(5as/h = 5 \cdot 32 \cdot 8192/4096 = 320\), so the factor is 354: \(354 \times 33.55\text{M} = 11.88\) GB per layer and \(\mathbf{380}\) GB for 32 layers (354 GiB). The \(T^2\) score term is 90% of it. A fused kernel (FlashAttention) never stores the scores, leaving \(34\,sbh\) = 1.14 GB per layer and 36.5 GB in total. Full recomputation keeps only each layer's input, \(2\,sbh = 67\) MB per layer and 2.15 GB in total, at the price of a third forward pass (\(8N\) instead of \(6N\) FLOPs per token, +33%).

Add the fp32 logits, \(8192 \times 128{,}256 \times 4\) B \(= 4.2\) GB. The constants are GPT-style (dropout, GeLU, MHA); Llama's differ, but the scaling in \(s\), \(b\) and \(L\) is what an interviewer wants (page 15).

Deep-dive D9. Llama 3 405B's training states are 6.48 TB. How many GPUs does memory force, and why did Meta use 16,384?

States are \(16 \times 405\text{B} = 6.48\) TB (6.49 TB with the exact 405.85B). At 80 GB per GPU that is 81 GPUs; at 90% usable, 90. Activations, logits and buffers push the practical minimum to the low hundreds: Llama 3's \(\text{TP}8 \times \text{PP}16 = 128\) GPUs hold one replica at \(6.48\text{ TB}/128 = 50.6\) GB of states per GPU (page 15).

Memory sets the size of a replica; time sets how many replicas. One 128-GPU replica at 40% MFU needs \(3.79 \times 10^{25}/(128 \times 3.958 \times 10^{14}) = 7.5 \times 10^{8}\) s, about 24 years. To finish in 60 days you need \(G = C/(t\,P\,\text{MFU}) = 3.79 \times 10^{25}/(5.18 \times 10^{6} \times 3.958 \times 10^{14}) = 18{,}500\) GPUs, the same order as Meta's 16K. Data parallelism (DP 128) exists for speed, not for memory.

Warm-up D10. Estimate the GPU-hours and dollar cost of pretraining Llama-3-8B on 15T tokens on H100s at 40% MFU, and the days on 1,024 GPUs. How does that compare with the model card?

\(C = 6ND = 7.23 \times 10^{23}\) FLOPs (D2). Core D11. How long does Llama 3 405B take to pretrain on 16,384 H100s at 40% MFU, and how does that compare with what the paper says?

Core D12. DeepSeek-V3 reports 2.788M H800 GPU-hours. Check the cost, and compute the MFU it implies.

Pretraining is 180K GPU-hours per trillion tokens times 14.8T \(= 2{,}664\)K exactly, which is 87.9 hours (3.7 days) per trillion tokens on 2,048 GPUs and 54.2 days in total.

It is a lower bound (attention and the MTP module are not counted). Core D13. What does 1T tokens of RL rollouts from Llama-3-8B cost, if one H100 sustains 2,500 output tokens/s?

On 64 GPUs that is \(4 \times 10^{8}/64 = 6.25 \times 10^{6}\) s, 72 days; on 1,024 GPUs, 4.5 days. Sanity-check the rate: 2,500 tokens/s is 64% of the 3,919 tokens/s roofline of a 64-sequence decode batch with a 4.6K average context (each step reads 16.06 GB of weights plus 38.7 GB of KV, 16.3 ms), and it is \(2N \times 2500 = 40\) TFLOP/s, about 4% MFU.

The same tokens cost half as much to train on: \(10N\) FLOPs per trained token (forward, backward, two log-prob passes) at 40% MFU is 0.20 ms per token, against 0.40 ms per generated token. So generation is about two thirds of the GPU-seconds of an RL step, despite five times fewer FLOPs (page 25).

Deep-dive D14. Design question. You must pretrain a 70B dense model (Llama-3-70B shape) on 15T tokens in 30 days on H100s. How many GPUs, what does it cost, and what else must you check?

Size. \(C = 6 \times 70.55 \times 10^{9} \times 15 \times 10^{12} = 6.35 \times 10^{24}\) FLOPs. At 40% MFU one GPU delivers \(3.958 \times 10^{14}\) FLOP/s, so \(C/3.958 \times 10^{14} = 1.60 \times 10^{10}\) GPU-seconds, 4.46M GPU-hours. Over 30 days (720 h), \(G = 4.457\text{M}/720 = \mathbf{6{,}190}\) GPUs, about 775 nodes of 8. That is 6.0× the 1,024 GPUs of page 15's 181-day plan, as it should be. Check the layout, which is a second problem. Memory is fine (states \(16N = 1.13\) TB shard over thousands of GPUs). But with TP8 × PP4, 6,144 GPUs give \(N_d = 192\) replicas, and a 16.8M-token batch (2,048 sequences) leaves only \(2048/192 = 10.7\) micro-batches per replica, so the pipeline bubble 流水线气泡 \((p-1)/m = 3/10.7 = 28\%\), against 4.7% in page 15's 1,024-GPU plan. Inference latency and throughput

Warm-up D15. How fast can an H100 decode Llama-3-8B at batch 1? What would an A100, H200 and B200 do?

Decode is bandwidth-bound: every step streams all weights once. In bf16 they are \(2 \times 8.03\text{B} = 16.06\) GB, so a step takes at least \(16.06/3350 = 4.79\) ms, at most 208 tokens/s. With a 4K-token KV cache (\(4096 \times 128\) KiB \(= 0.537\) GB) it is 16.60 GB, 4.95 ms, 202 tokens/s. The compute time is \(2N_{mm}/P = 15.0\text{ GFLOPs}/989.4\text{ TFLOP/s} = 0.015\) ms, 0.3% of the step: the tensor cores idle. Real engines reach a fraction; expect roughly 130 to 180 tokens/s on an SXM part (page 06).

Same formula, other HBM: A100 80GB (2.04 TB/s) 7.87 ms, 127 tokens/s; H200 (4.8 TB/s) 3.35 ms, 299; B200 (8 TB/s) 2.01 ms, 498. Only bandwidth matters at batch 1, so the H200 is 1.43× the H100 with identical FLOP/s.

Core D16. Llama-3-8B decoding with batch 64 at 4K context on one H100: step time, per-user speed, total throughput, and is it compute-bound? How many sequences fit?

KV per sequence 0.537 GB, so 64 sequences hold 34.36 GB. A step reads \(16.06 + 34.36 = 50.42\) GB, taking \(50.42/3350 = \mathbf{15.05}\) ms. Per user that is 66.4 tokens/s; in total \(64/0.01505 = 4{,}252\) tokens/s, 21× the 4K-context batch-1 throughput of D15 (202 tokens/s) for 3.0× its step time. Compute per step is \(64 \times (15.0 + 2.15)\) GFLOPs (linear layers plus attention over a 4K cache) \(\approx 1.1\) TFLOPs, 1.1 ms, 7% of the step: still memory-bound.

Capacity: at 90% of 80 GB, \((72 - 16.06)/0.537 = 104\) sequences. The ceiling as \(B \to \infty\) is \(\text{BW}/(T\kappa) = 3.35\text{ TB/s}/0.537\text{ GB} = 6{,}240\) tokens/s per GPU, which no batching can beat: it is Shazeer's point that the cache is not shared across a batch (page 06). \(B = 64\) reaches 68% of it.

Core D17. Prefill a 2,048-token prompt on Llama-3-8B on one H100. What is the time to first token, and how does the prompt rate compare with decode?

Prefill 预填充 is compute-bound. FLOPs \(= 2NT = 2 \times 8.03 \times 10^{9} \times 2048 = 3.29 \times 10^{13}\). At peak that is \(3.29 \times 10^{13}/989.4 \times 10^{12} = 33.2\) ms; at a planning MFU of 50%, 66.5 ms, which is 30,800 prompt tokens/s, about 150× the 202 tokens/s of one decode stream. The weights are read once (4.8 ms), small next to 33 ms, so the pass is compute-bound. TTFT on an idle GPU is about 70 ms, plus queueing and network.

\(2NT\) is a slight overestimate at 2K (exact counting, with no FLOPs for the embedding lookup, causal attention and the LM head on the last position only, gives \(2.97 \times 10^{13}\), 10% less), and a big underestimate at long context: at \(T = 32{,}768\), attention is 38% of the FLOPs and the pass takes 1.5 s at 50% MFU (page 06).

Core D18. Design question. A service promises a time per output token (TPOT) of at most 20 ms for Llama-3-8B at 4K context on one H100. What is the largest batch, and what throughput does that give? What if the target is 10 ms?

Step time is \((16.06 + 0.537\,B)/3350\) GB per ms, which must be at most 20 ms: \(16.06 + 0.537B \le 67.0\), so \(B \le 94.9\), i.e. \(B = 94\). The step is then \((16.06 + 50.47)/3350 = 19.86\) ms and throughput is \(94/0.01986 = \mathbf{4{,}733}\) tokens/s, or $0.117 per million tokens at $2 per hour. Capacity would allow 104, so the latency target binds first.

At 10 ms: \(0.010 \times 3350 = 33.5\) GB, so \(B \le (33.5 - 16.06)/0.537 = 32.5\), i.e. 32, giving 3,225 tokens/s. Halving TPOT costs 32% of throughput, and per-user speed doubles from 50 to 101 tokens/s. That trade curve (throughput up with \(B\), each user slower) is what a serving team tunes (page 17).

Core D19. Price per million output tokens for Llama-3-8B on one H100 at $2 per GPU-hour, at batch 1 and at the 4K-context capacity limit (\(B = 104\)). Why the difference?

\(\$/\text{M tokens} = p\,G/(3600 \cdot \text{tokens/s}) \times 10^{6}\). At batch 1 the rate is 202 tokens/s: \(2/(3600 \times 202) \times 10^{6} = \$2.75\). At \(B = 104\) a step reads \(16.06 + 55.83 = 71.9\) GB, 21.46 ms, so 4,846 tokens/s: \(\$0.115\). Batching made a token 24× cheaper because one 16 GB weight read is shared by 104 sequences.

Context reverses it: at 128K context the KV cache is 17.2 GB per sequence, only 3 fit, and the cost at capacity is $3.74 per million tokens, 32× the 4K figure, because the KV read is per sequence and cannot be shared (page 17). These are roofline bounds, so real costs are higher. For scale, DeepSeek's published accounting works out to about $0.30 per million output tokens on its decode nodes at $16 per node-hour (page 17), within 3× of the 8B roofline despite a 671B model.

Deep-dive D20. At what batch size does decode of an 8B model become compute-bound on an H100 in bf16, in fp8, and with 4-bit weights? Does it ever, once the KV cache is counted?

A weight matmul with \(m\) tokens sharing each weight read does \(2m\) FLOPs per weight. bf16: a weight is 2 bytes, so intensity is \(m\) FLOPs/byte against a ridge of \(989.4/3.35 = 295\): \(m^{*} = 295\). fp8 (W8A8): 1 byte per weight, so intensity \(2m\), against a ridge that doubled to 591: \(m^{*} = 295\) again, at twice the FLOP rate. W4A16 (4.125 bits, 0.516 bytes per weight, computed on bf16 tensor cores after dequantization): intensity \(2m/0.516 = 3.88m\), so \(m^{*} = 295/3.88 = 76\). Four-bit weights hit the compute wall at a 4× smaller batch, which is why the measured speedup of 4-bit kernels fades beyond batch 32 to 128 (page 17).

The KV cache changes the answer. Attention has intensity \(n_h/n_{kv} = 4\) FLOPs per byte at any batch, so the whole step's intensity at large \(B\) is \(4 + 2N_{mm}/(\kappa T)\). That reaches 295 only if \(T \le 2 \times 7.5\text{B}/(131{,}072 \times 291) \approx 390\) tokens. Above that, GQA-8 decode stays memory-bound however large the batch.

Deep-dive D21. Speculative decoding with per-token acceptance \(\alpha = 0.8\) and \(\gamma = 4\) draft tokens: expected tokens per target pass, and the wall-clock speedup if a draft pass costs 5% of a target pass? When does it stop helping?

In speculative decoding 投机解码 with independent acceptance, a step emits the accepted drafts plus one token: \(\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens}] = (1 - \alpha^{\gamma+1})/(1 - \alpha) = (1 - 0.8^5)/0.2 = (1 - 0.32768)/0.2 = \mathbf{3.36}\). The step costs one target pass plus \(\gamma\) draft passes, \(1 + \gamma c = 1 + 4 \times 0.05 = 1.2\) target-pass units, so the speedup is \(3.36/1.2 = \mathbf{2.80\times}\). The ceiling at \(\alpha = 0.8\) is \(1/(1-\alpha) = 5\) tokens. At \(\alpha = 0.9\): 4.10 tokens and 3.41×.

It works because verifying \(\gamma + 1\) tokens reads the weights once, and decode is bandwidth-bound, so extra tokens are nearly free. It stops when \(B(\gamma + 1)\) nears the ridge (about 295), which is \(B \approx 59\) at \(\gamma = 4\), and it is worse for MoE, where 5 verified tokens touch about 37.6 distinct experts instead of 8 (page 17).

KV cache

Warm-up D22. What is Llama-3-8B's KV cache per token, and for one 128K-token sequence? What would plain multi-head attention cost?

Per token: \(2 \cdot L \cdot n_{kv} \cdot d_h \cdot b = 2 \cdot 32 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 131{,}072\) bytes \(= 128\) KiB (2 for K and V, 2 bytes for bf16). One 128K-token sequence (131,072 tokens) is \(128\text{ KiB} \times 131{,}072 = 16\) GiB (17.2 GB), more than the weights (16.06 GB). With MHA (32 KV heads) it would be 4× larger, 64 GiB; with an fp8 cache, 8 GiB. Note the unit: 16 GiB is 17.2 GB, and a careless mix of the two is a 7% error (page 06).

Core D23. Design question. How much KV cache do 32 concurrent users at 32K context need on a 70B GQA model (80 layers, 8 KV heads of 128, bf16), and how many H100s do you need to serve them?

Per token \(2 \cdot 80 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 327{,}680\) B \(= 320\) KiB. Tokens: \(32 \times 32{,}768 = 1{,}048{,}576\). KV \(= 320\text{ KiB} \times 2^{20} = \mathbf{320\text{ GiB}} = 343.6\) GB, which is 10.7 GB per user. Weights in bf16 are \(2 \times 70.55\text{B} = 141.1\) GB. Total 484.7 GB.

At 72 GB usable per GPU (90% of 80) that is 6.7, so 7 GPUs. Tensor parallelism wants a power of two that divides 64 query heads and 8 KV heads, so use one 8-GPU node: 576 GB usable, 434.9 GB left after weights, room for 40 sequences of 32K (32 fit with 8 to spare). With an fp8 KV cache the cache halves to 171.8 GB and the node fits 81 sequences.

Then check speed: each decode step reads all 484.7 GB, over 8 GPUs at 26.8 TB/s aggregate: 18.1 ms, so 55 tokens/s per user and 1,770 tokens/s in total. The KV cache is 71% of the bytes read, so KV quantization or an MLA-style cache speeds up decode as well as adding capacity.

Core D24. Compare DeepSeek-V3's MLA cache with a GQA-8 cache of the same shape per token and for a 128K sequence, and count how many 128K sequences fit next to the fp8 weights on 8 H200s.

MLA caches \(d_c + d_h^R = 512 + 64 = 576\) numbers per layer: \(61 \times 576 \times 2 = 70{,}272\) B \(= 68.6\) KiB per token, and for 131,072 tokens 9.21 GB (8.58 GiB). GQA-8 with 128-dim heads is \(2 \cdot 61 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 249{,}856\) B \(= 244\) KiB per token, 32.7 GB (30.5 GiB) at 128K, 3.56× larger. MHA with 128 heads would be 3.81 MiB per token, 488 GiB at 128K (page 07).

Eight H200s hold \(8 \times 141 = 1{,}128\) GB. The fp8 weights take 671 GB, leaving 457 GB: \(457/9.21 = 49\) sequences of 128K with MLA, against \(457/32.7 = 13\) with GQA-8 (this counts all of HBM; under the 90% rule of the other drills 344 GB is left, which is 37 and 10 sequences). A 671B model has a smaller cache per token than the 8B Llama (68.6 versus 128 KiB).

Deep-dive D25. Disaggregated serving moves the KV cache from a prefill GPU to a decode GPU. How long does the transfer take for a 2,048-token prompt on Llama-3-70B over 400 Gb/s InfiniBand, over 25 Gb/s Ethernet, and with DeepSeek-V3's MLA cache? Compare with the prefill compute.

Llama-3-70B: \(320\text{ KiB} \times 2048 = 671\) MB. Over one 400 Gb/s link (50 GB/s) that is \(671/50{,}000 = 13.4\) ms (with TP8 each GPU holds one of the 8 KV heads and can send it over its own NIC, up to 8× faster); over 25 Gb/s Ethernet (3.125 GB/s), 215 ms. DeepSeek-V3: \(70{,}272 \times 2048 = 144\) MB, 2.9 ms on InfiniBand, 4.7× smaller than the GQA cache for the same prompt.

Prefill compute for the 70B model is \(2NT = 2 \times 70.55 \times 10^{9} \times 2048 = 2.89 \times 10^{14}\) FLOPs, which on 8 H100s at 50% MFU takes \(2.89 \times 10^{14}/(8 \times 0.5 \times 989.4 \times 10^{12}) = 73\) ms. So on one InfiniBand link the transfer is 18% of prefill if it is not overlapped (about 2% with eight NICs), and on slow Ethernet it is 3× the prefill and dominates. Systems stream the cache layer by layer while the next layer computes; Splitwise reports about 5 ms of visible overhead on H100 (page 17). This is one more reason to shrink the cache per token.

Communication

Warm-up D26. How long does a gradient all-reduce take for an 8B-parameter model over 8 GPUs connected by NVLink (H100)?

The bf16 gradients are \(S = 2 \times 8.03\text{B} = 16.06\) GB. A ring all-reduce sends \(2(n-1)/n \times S = 2 \times 7/8 \times 16.06 = 28.1\) GB from each GPU. The usable bandwidth is one direction of NVLink 4, 450 GB/s (900 GB/s is bidirectional), so \(t = 28.1/450 = \mathbf{62.5}\) ms. Using 900 by mistake gives 31 ms. If gradients are accumulated and reduced in fp32, as Llama 3 does, the message is 32.1 GB and the time 125 ms.

Put it in context: at 65,536 tokens per GPU per step the compute is \(6 \times 8.03 \times 10^{9} \times 65{,}536 = 3.16 \times 10^{15}\) FLOPs, 8.0 s at 40% MFU, so the all-reduce is 0.8% of the step even with no overlap. Real collectives reach a fraction of the link peak, so treat 62 ms as a floor. (Plain DDP would not fit an 8B model anyway, D6: real setups shard the states.)

Core D27. The same gradient sync, now across 64 GPUs on 8 nodes over 400 Gb/s InfiniBand. What fraction of a 4M-token step does it take, and what happens with ZeRO-2 or full FSDP?

Each GPU sends \(2 \times 63/64 \times 16.06 = 31.6\) GB. On the 50 GB/s network that is \(0.632\) s (a flat ring on the slowest link; a hierarchical collective would use NVLink inside nodes and do better). A 4M-token step is 65,536 tokens per GPU, 8.0 s at 40% MFU (D26), so the sync is 7.9% of the step if exposed. It overlaps with the backward pass, which is two thirds of the compute, so most of it hides.

Sharding costs traffic. ZeRO-2 must reduce-scatter after each of the 8 micro-batches: \(8 \times 15.8 + 15.8 = 142\) GB, 2.8 s, still overlappable with backward. Full FSDP re-gathers parameters for every micro-batch, 2 all-gathers and a reduce-scatter of 15.8 GB each, 47.4 GB per micro-batch, so \(8 \times 47.4 = 379\) GB and 7.6 s, as long as the whole step unless parameters stay resident, which is why Llama 3 does not reshard after the forward pass (page 15).

Core D28. A pipeline has \(p = 16\) stages and \(m = 64\) micro-batches. What is the bubble? How many micro-batches for under 5%, and what does interleaving buy?

State which ratio you mean. Relative to the ideal compute time the bubble is \((p-1)/m = 15/64 = \mathbf{23.4\%}\). As the fraction of wall-clock time a GPU sits idle it is \((p-1)/(m + p - 1) = 15/79 = 19.0\%\). GPipe and 1F1B have the same bubble (1F1B only saves activation memory).

Under 5% relative needs \(m > 15/0.05 = 300\) micro-batches, which usually means a global batch that hurts optimization. Interleaving with \(v\) model chunks per GPU divides the bubble by \(v\): \(v = 2\) gives 11.7% (idle 10.5%), \(v = 4\) gives 5.9% (idle 5.5%), at the price of \(v\) times more point-to-point messages. Since \(m = \text{sequences}/(N_d \times \text{micro-batch})\), the bubble grows as you add data-parallel replicas at a fixed batch: 2,048 sequences over 32 replicas gives \(m = 64\) (page 15's 70B plan), over 192 replicas only 10.7 (D14).

Deep-dive D29. MoE all-to-all for DeepSeek-V3 (\(d = 7168\)): bytes per token per layer and per forward pass, and the InfiniBand bandwidth this needs at the training throughput of 180K GPU-hours per trillion tokens.

V3 dispatches in fp8 (1 byte per element) and combines in bf16 (2 bytes), so one destination costs \(7168 \times (1 + 2) = 21{,}504\) B per token per layer. Routing is limited to 4 nodes, so at most \(4 \times 21{,}504 = 86{,}016\) B per token per MoE layer (an upper bound: one target may be the token's own node, and NVLink forwards to further GPUs inside a node). Over 58 MoE layers: 4.99 MB per token, forward.

Throughput is 1,543 tokens/s per GPU (D12), so the forward pass generates \(1543 \times 4.99\text{ MB} = 7.7\) GB/s per GPU, and the backward pass mirrors it (fp8 gradient dispatch, bf16 combine), about 15.4 GB/s in all, 31% of the 50 GB/s InfiniBand link, as a time average. It is an upper bound and ignores recomputation and the MTP module. With no node limit (8 nodes per token) it would be 62%. That is why the all-to-all must overlap with compute (DualPipe) and why the limit of 4 nodes matters (page 08).

Scaling laws

Core D30. What is the compute-optimal model at \(10^{23}\) FLOPs, and what does training it cost?

With the 20 tokens per parameter rule for a compute-optimal 计算最优 model, \(C = 6N \cdot 20N = 120N^2\), so \(N = \sqrt{10^{23}/120} = \sqrt{8.33 \times 10^{20}} = 2.89 \times 10^{10}\), about 28.9B parameters, and \(D = 20N = 577\)B tokens. Check: \(6 \times 28.9 \times 10^{9} \times 577 \times 10^{9} = 1.0 \times 10^{23}\).

The closed form with the Epoch AI refit (\(A = 482.01\), \(B = 2085.43\), \(\alpha = 0.3478\), \(\beta = 0.3658\)) gives 29.4B parameters and 566B tokens (19.2 per parameter), about 2% from the rule; the two allocations differ in predicted loss by about \(5 \times 10^{-6}\) nats, so the valley is flat. Do not use the rounded constants printed for Chinchilla's Approach 3: they give 14.6B and 1.14T tokens, 78 tokens per parameter, an artifact (page 13). Cost: an H100-hour at 40% MFU delivers \(1.42 \times 10^{18}\) FLOPs, so \(10^{23}/1.42 \times 10^{18} = 70\text{K}\) H100-hours, about $140K at $2.

Core D31. Llama-3-8B saw 15T tokens. How many tokens per parameter is that, how far is it from Chinchilla, and what model would be compute-optimal for the same compute? Why do labs do this?

\(15 \times 10^{12}/8.03 \times 10^{9} = 1{,}868\) tokens per parameter, 93× the compute-optimal 20. Its compute is \(7.23 \times 10^{23}\) FLOPs (D2), for which the rule gives \(N = \sqrt{7.23 \times 10^{23}/120} = 77.6\)B on 1.55T tokens: a model 9.7× larger trained on 9.7× fewer tokens. For comparison, Llama 3 405B is at 38.5 tokens per parameter, and DeepSeek-V3 at 400 per activated parameter (using the report's 37B; 394 with 37.55B) and 22 per total.

The reason is inference. Serving costs \(2N\) FLOPs per token, so total cost is \(6ND + 2N\,T_{\text{served}}\), and a model that will serve trillions of tokens should be smaller and trained longer than the training-optimal point. That is over-training 过度训练, a cost decision and not a mistake (page 13). Caveat: the fitted law is extrapolated far outside the range where it was fitted, so quote the ratio, not a predicted loss.

Deep-dive D32. (a) With the Epoch fit, by what factor does the reducible loss fall each time compute doubles? (b) A 60B model trained on 3T tokens reaches the loss of a 96B compute-optimal model. When does the smaller model win?

(a) At the optimum \(L^{*} = E + K C^{-\gamma}\) with \(\gamma = \alpha\beta/(\alpha + \beta) = 0.3478 \times 0.3658/0.7136 = 0.178\). Doubling compute multiplies the reducible part by \(2^{-0.178} = 0.884\). From \(10^{24}\) FLOPs (\(L^{*} = 1.9597\), reducible part \(1.9597 - 1.8172 = 0.1425\)) to \(2 \times 10^{24}\): \(0.1425 \times 0.884 = 0.1260\), so \(L^{*} = 1.9431\), a gain of 0.017 nats for twice the compute.

(b) The 96B model is trained on 1.74T tokens (\(C = 1.0 \times 10^{24}\)). To reach the same loss the 60B model needs 3.05T tokens (\(6 \times 60\text{B} \times 3.05\text{T} = 1.10 \times 10^{24}\), 10% more compute). Serving costs \(2N\) per token, so total FLOPs are \(6ND + 2N\,T_s\). Setting the two equal (subscript 1 for the 96B model, 2 for the 60B one) gives \(T^{*} = 3(N_2 D_2 - N_1 D_1)/(N_1 - N_2) = 3 \times (1.832 - 1.667) \times 10^{23}/(35.9 \times 10^{9}) = 1.4 \times 10^{12}\) tokens. If you expect to serve more than about 1.4T tokens, the 60B model is cheaper overall, and since decode is bandwidth-bound in practice, the real threshold is lower. The unknown is the demand forecast, not the arithmetic (page 13).

Mixture of experts

Core D33. Serve DeepSeek-V3 (671B parameters in fp8). What is the minimum number of 80 GB GPUs, what fits on 8 H200s, and what is the floor on step time and tokens/s per user at large batch?

At one byte per parameter the weights are 671 GB: \(671/80 = 8.4\), so at least 9 GPUs for weights alone, and more once KV cache and activations count (DeepSeek's real units are 32 GPUs for prefill and 320 for decode). Eight H200s hold \(8 \times 141 = 1{,}128\) GB, leaving 457 GB for KV and activations (443 GB if the MTP module is loaded, page 17; 344 GB under the 90% usable rule).

Bandwidth: \(8 \times 4.8 = 38.4\) TB/s. By a batch of about 100 nearly every expert is read each step (87% at 64, 98% at 128, D34), so a step streams about the whole 671 GB: \(671/38{,}400 = 17.5\) ms, at most 57 tokens/s per user. That is the ceiling; page 17's 17.2 ms and about 58 tokens/s are the exact batch-128 figures, where 98% of the routed experts are touched (\(17.117 + 0.9828 \times 653.9 = 659.8\) GB). At batch 1 only the activated 37.55 GB is read: 0.98 ms per step. So a 128× larger batch costs 18× the step time, and the 37B "active" advantage has largely gone (idealized: perfect sharding, no communication; page 17).

Deep-dive D34. Why does MoE decode need a far larger batch than a dense model to become compute-bound? For DeepSeek-V3 (256 routed experts, top-8) compute the fraction of experts read at batch 64, the tokens per expert, and the batch at which each expert's matmul reaches the ridge.

With \(b\) tokens in flight and uniform independent routing, the expected number of distinct experts read in a layer is \(E\,[1 - (1 - k/E)^b]\) with \(k/E = 1/32\): \(b = 1\) reads 8 experts (3%), \(b = 8\) reads 57 (22%), \(b = 64\) reads 222 (87%), and \(b = 512\) all 256. Each expert receives \(bk/E = b/32\) tokens, so 2 tokens per expert at \(b = 64\). Real routing is correlated, which lowers the touched count at small \(b\).

An expert matmul with \(n\) tokens reads \(P\) bytes (fp8) and does \(2nP\) FLOPs: intensity \(2n\) against the fp8 ridge of 591, so it is compute-bound at \(n \approx 295\) (the same threshold as bf16). With \(n = b/32\) that needs \(b = 295 \times 32 = 9{,}440\) tokens in flight, 32× the batch of 295 that a dense model needs. DeepSeek reports decode batches per expert usually within 256 tokens, so the MoE part is memory-bound, which is why serving pools tokens from many replicas (DP80) into a wide expert-parallel group (EP320): 9,440 tokens is about 118 sequences per replica (page 08, page 17).
