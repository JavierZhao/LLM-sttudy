How to use this list
  • Interviewers ask about papers as designs, not titles. "Walk me through how DeepSeek-V3 balances its experts", "why did Chinchilla disagree with Kaplan", "what would you ablate first?" For every paper below the list names the sections that answer such questions, so you read the method and the evidence, not the related work.
  • Numbers come from tables, not abstracts. Expect "what was the baseline?" and "what does that figure compare against?". The reading pitfalls below collect the standard traps.
  • Recency is scored. Frontier labs expect you to know the current generation. The newest reports in the course are DeepSeek-V4 (2026) and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (September 2026); say "as of September 2026" when you call any model the latest.
  • Fluency in English. The interview is in English. The glossary gives each concept's English term first and the Chinese term second. Cover the definition column and check that you can state each concept in one English sentence.
  • Budgets. Tier 1 (12 papers) takes about 6 hours and all 25 about 11. With only two hours, read Vaswani et al., Chinchilla, PPO, DPO and DeepSeek-V2 (about 110 minutes): attention, scaling, RL, preferences and modern KV and expert design.

A · Architecture, scaling and the reference recipe

Start here. Seven papers that every later page assumes: the Transformer, the position encoding and KV-cache trade-offs that shaped modern models, the two scaling-law papers, and one complete dense recipe.

  1. Vaswani et al. (2017). Attention Is All You Need. Why read it: source of scaled dot-product and multi-head attention, sinusoidal positions and the Post-LN block: every variant on pages 07 to 11 is a change to it.
    Supports: 03 Attention, 04 Transformer block, 05 RoPE. Read: §3 (model architecture) and the ablation Table 3 in §6.2. Skim: §4 (why self-attention). Time: about 20 min (5 pages read, 1 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 5 course pages.
  2. Su et al. (2021). RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding. Why read it: the position scheme of the Llama, Qwen, Mistral, DeepSeek and Gemma families: rotate queries and keys so the score depends only on the offset; YaRN and other context extensions modify it.
    Supports: 05 RoPE, 10 Long context. Read: §3.1 to §3.3 (formulation, the rotation, properties). Skim: §3.4 (theoretical explanation). Time: about 10 min (2 pages read, 2 skimmed) · Tier 2 · linked on 1 course page.
  3. Ainslie et al. (2023). GQA: Training Generalized Multi-Query Transformer Models from Multi-Head Checkpoints. Why read it: grouped-query attention and the mean-pooling recipe to convert an MHA checkpoint: the default KV-cache reduction in the Llama and Qwen families and the baseline MLA is compared with.
    Supports: 07 KV-efficient attention, 29 Llama, 30 Qwen. Read: §2 (uptraining, GQA) and §3.3 (ablations). Skim: §3.2 (main results). Time: about 10 min (2.5 pages read, 1 skimmed) · Tier 2 · linked on 1 course page.
  4. Dao et al. (2022). FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness. Why read it: tiling plus online softmax makes exact attention memory-linear and IO-optimal; interviews ask you to derive the online-softmax recurrence and say which HBM traffic disappears.
    Supports: 03 Attention, 16 GPUs & kernels. Read: §2 (hardware and standard attention), §3.1 to §3.2 (algorithm, IO complexity), App. B.3 to B.4 (forward and backward pass). Skim: §4 (experiments). Time: about 25 min (5 pages read, 3 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 2 course pages.
  5. Kaplan et al. (2020). Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models. Why read it: origin of power-law scaling, \(C \approx 6N\) FLOPs per token and the critical batch size, and the baseline Chinchilla corrected: expect "why did they disagree?"
    Supports: 04 Transformer block, 13 Scaling laws, 34 Back-of-envelope. Read: §1 (summary of the laws), §2.1 (parameter and FLOP counts), §6 (optimal allocation of compute). Skim: §3 and §4 (figures). Time: about 40 min (8 pages read, 4 skimmed) · Tier 2 · linked on 4 course pages.
  6. Hoffmann et al. (2022). Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models. Why read it: the IsoFLOP method and the rule of about 20 tokens per parameter, the yardstick for any "why so many tokens?" answer; read it with the replications on page 13 before trusting its constants.
    Supports: 13 Scaling laws, 29 Llama, 34 Back-of-envelope. Read: §3 (three estimation approaches), §5 (discussion), App. D.4 (comparison with Kaplan). Skim: §4 (the Chinchilla model). Time: about 25 min (6 pages read, 2 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 4 course pages.
  7. Llama Team (2024). The Llama 3 Herd of Models. Why read it: the fullest public account of a dense frontier pipeline: data, an IsoFLOP study, 4D parallelism with an MFU table, staged long-context extension, then SFT, rejection sampling and DPO.
    Supports: 04 Transformer block, 12 Pretraining data, 13 Scaling laws, 15 Parallelism, 29 Llama, 34 Back-of-envelope. Read: §3.1 to §3.2 (data, architecture, scaling laws), §3.3.2 (parallelism), §3.4 (recipe), §4.1 (post-training modeling). Skim: §3.3.4 (reliability and failure statistics). Time: about 40 min (9 pages read, 2 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 17 course pages.

B · Training systems

Two short papers whose arithmetic (what is sharded, what is communicated) appears in almost every systems question.

  1. Shoeybi et al. (2019). Megatron-LM: Training Multi-Billion Parameter Language Models Using Model Parallelism. Why read it: tensor parallelism in three pages: column- then row-parallel matrices, with two all-reduces per layer in the forward pass and two in the backward pass.
    Supports: 15 Parallelism. Read: §2.3 (data and model parallelism), §3 (model parallel transformers), §5.1 (scaling analysis). Skim: §5.2 (GPT-2 results). Time: about 15 min (3.5 pages read, 0.5 skimmed) · Tier 2 · linked on 1 course page.
  2. Rajbhandari et al. (2020). ZeRO: Memory Optimizations Toward Training Trillion Parameter Models. Why read it: the \(2N + 2N + KN\) memory accounting (\(K = 12\) for mixed-precision Adam) and the three sharding stages: the arithmetic behind FSDP and every "will it fit" question.
    Supports: 15 Parallelism, 34 Back-of-envelope. Read: §3 (where did the memory go), §4 to §5 (ZeRO-DP stages), §7 (communication analysis). Skim: §10 (evaluation). Time: about 35 min (7.5 pages read, 3 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 2 course pages.

C · Post-training and reinforcement learning

The line from SFT and RLHF to critic-free RL with verifiable rewards. Read in this order: each paper modifies the previous one.

  1. Ouyang et al. (2022). Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback. Why read it: the SFT, reward-model and PPO pipeline that later recipes modify, with its numbers: dataset sizes, PPO-ptx, the alignment tax, and a 1.3B model preferred to the 175B GPT-3.
    Supports: 21 Preference optimization, 22 RL foundations, 23 RLHF, 19 SFT & distillation. Read: §3.4 to §3.5 (human data, models), App. C.1 to C.4 (SFT, RM and RLHF training details). Skim: §4.1 to §4.2 (results). Time: about 30 min (5.5 pages read, 4 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 5 course pages.
  2. Schulman et al. (2017). Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms. Why read it: the clipped surrogate objective in 8 pages: write it from memory and say what the clip does for positive and negative advantages; GRPO and DAPO edit it.
    Supports: 22 RL foundations, 23 RLHF. Read: §2 to §5 (background, clipped objective, adaptive KL, algorithm). Skim: §6.1 (comparison of surrogate objectives). Time: about 15 min (3 pages read, 1 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 1 course page.
  3. Rafailov et al. (2023). Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model. Why read it: the closed-form KL-regularized optimum turned into a classification loss on preference pairs: re-derive it on a whiteboard and know its limits (offline data, likelihood displacement).
    Supports: 21 Preference optimization. Read: §4 to §5 (the objective and its analysis), App. A.1 to A.4 (derivations and gradient). Skim: §6.1 (how well does DPO optimize the RLHF objective). Time: about 20 min (5 pages read, 1 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 1 course page.
  4. Shao et al. (2024). DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models. Why read it: origin of GRPO (PPO without a critic, group-mean baseline); §5.2 casts SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO and GRPO as one gradient with different data and weights.
    Supports: 24 RLVR, 26 DeepSeek I, 22 RL foundations. Read: §4.1 (GRPO), §5.2 (insights of reinforcement learning). Skim: §2.1 (math corpus collection). Time: about 35 min (8 pages read, 2 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 3 course pages.
  5. DeepSeek-AI (2025). DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning. Why read it: R1-Zero shows reasoning emerging from outcome-reward RL alone; R1 adds a cold start, a second RL stage and rejection-sampling SFT. Read the arXiv v2 text (January 2026): some numbers differ from v1.
    Supports: 24 RLVR, 28 DeepSeek III, 19 SFT & distillation. Read: §2 (R1-Zero), §3 (R1 pipeline), App. A.3 (GRPO versus PPO), App. F.1 and G.2 (distillation versus RL, unsuccessful attempts). Skim: App. B.4 (hyperparameters). Time: about 45 min (10.5 pages read, 1 skimmed) · Tier 2 · linked on 7 course pages.
  6. Yu et al. (2025). DAPO: An Open-Source LLM Reinforcement Learning System at Scale. Why read it: four fixes to GRPO at scale (clip-higher, dynamic sampling, token-level loss, overlong reward shaping) with a cumulative ablation: the checklist for "why did the RL run collapse?"
    Supports: 24 RLVR. Read: §3 (the four techniques), §4.3 (training dynamics). Skim: §2 (preliminaries). Time: about 20 min (4 pages read, 2 skimmed) · Tier 2 · linked on 2 course pages.

D · The DeepSeek line: MoE, MLA, sparse and compressed attention

Six reports read in release order. V3 is the anchor for the course's worked numbers, and V4 and V4.1-Flash are the current reference for long-context efficiency.

  1. Dai et al. (2024). DeepSeekMoE: Towards Ultimate Expert Specialization in Mixture-of-Experts Language Models. Why read it: fine-grained expert segmentation plus shared experts, the MoE design that V2 and V3 scale up and Qwen3 and Kimi K2 follow; its §4 ablations are what interviewers ask about.
    Supports: 08 MoE, 26 DeepSeek I. Read: §3 (architecture), §4.4 (ablations). Skim: §4.5 (expert specialization analysis). Time: about 20 min (4 pages read, 3 skimmed) · Tier 2 · linked on 3 course pages.
  2. DeepSeek-AI (2024). DeepSeek-V2: A Strong, Economical, and Efficient Mixture-of-Experts Language Model. Why read it: multi-head latent attention (a latent KV cache with decoupled RoPE) and device-limited routing; its 93.3% KV-cache reduction is against dense DeepSeek 67B, not an isolated MLA ablation (page 07).
    Supports: 07 KV-efficient attention, 26 DeepSeek I, 27 DeepSeek II. Read: §2.1 (MLA), §2.2 (DeepSeekMoE, device-limited routing), App. C (full MLA formulas). Skim: §3.2.3 (efficiency claims). Time: about 30 min (7 pages read, 1 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 7 course pages.
  3. DeepSeek-AI (2024). DeepSeek-V3 Technical Report. Why read it: the anchor for the course's worked numbers: MLA, a 256-expert MoE with auxiliary-loss-free balancing, multi-token prediction, DualPipe and FP8 training, plus the cost accounting.
    Supports: 07 KV-efficient attention, 08 MoE, 11 Stability, 15 Parallelism, 27 DeepSeek II, 34 Back-of-envelope. Read: §2 (architecture, MTP), §3.2.1 (DualPipe), §3.3 (FP8 training). Skim: §4.2 to §4.3 (hyperparameters, long-context extension). Time: about 40 min (10 pages read, 1.5 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 18 course pages.
  4. DeepSeek-AI (2025). DeepSeek-V3.2: Pushing the Frontier of Open Large Language Models. Why read it: DeepSeek Sparse Attention (a lightning indexer selects the top-k keys; added to V3.1-Terminus by continued training) and the four stabilizers for scaling GRPO; only 17 pages of main text.
    Supports: 09 Beyond full attention, 24 RLVR, 25 RL systems, 28 DeepSeek III. Read: §2 (DSA, parity evaluation, inference cost), §3.1 (scaling GRPO). Skim: §3.2 (thinking in tool use). Time: about 25 min (5 pages read, 4 skimmed) · Tier 2 · linked on 8 course pages.
  5. DeepSeek-AI (2026). DeepSeek-V4: Towards Highly Efficient Million-Token Context Intelligence. Why read it: the course's most-cited source (19 pages): CSA and HCA compressed attention for 1M tokens (abstract: V4-Pro needs 27% of V3.2's single-token inference FLOPs and 10% of its KV cache), mHC, Muon and on-policy distillation.
    Supports: 07 KV-efficient attention, 09 Beyond full attention, 14 Optimizers, 19 SFT & distillation, 28 DeepSeek III. Read: §2.2 (mHC), §2.3 (CSA and HCA), §2.4 (Muon), §3.5.1 (KV cache structure), §5.1.2 (on-policy distillation). Skim: §4.2.3 (mitigating training instability). Time: about 40 min (10 pages read, 1 skimmed) · Tier 1 · linked on 19 course pages.
  6. DeepSeek-AI (2026). DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash: Pushing the Limits of KV Cache Compression. Why read it: the newest KV-compression design (September 2026): cross-layer KV and index reuse (CSA2), an FP4 main KV cache (890 bytes per token in HBM, per the abstract) and SWA bounded replay; closest to your research, so read it after V4.
    Supports: 28 DeepSeek III, 09 Beyond full attention. Read: §2.2 to §2.3 (causal encoder-decoder, CSA2), §2.4.4 (FP4 KV cache), §3.2 (inference system). Skim: §2.4.2 to §2.4.3 (Engram, DSpark). Time: about 25 min (5.5 pages read, 1.5 skimmed) · Tier 3 · linked on 3 course pages.

E · Long context, hybrids and open frontier models

Context extension, the linear-attention layer that hybrids use, and two open model families that made different choices from DeepSeek.

  1. Peng, Quesnelle, Fan, Shippole (2023). YaRN: Efficient Context Window Extension of Large Language Models. Why read it: the RoPE extension recipe (frequency-dependent interpolation, NTK-by-parts, plus an attention temperature) that DeepSeek-V3 uses from 4K to 128K; read it with the reference code.
    Supports: 10 Long context. Read: §2.1 (RoPE), §3 (methodology). Skim: §4 (experiments). Time: about 20 min (4 pages read, 3 skimmed) · Tier 2 · linked on 1 course page.
  2. Yang et al. (2024). Gated Delta Networks: Improving Mamba2 with Delta Rule. Why read it: the gated delta rule: Mamba-style decay (fast erasure) plus the delta-rule update (targeted overwrite), the layer in Qwen3-Next's 3:1 hybrid. Chosen for your long-context research.
    Supports: 09 Beyond full attention, 30 Qwen. Read: §2 (preliminaries), §3 (gated delta rule, S-NIAH case study, chunkwise algorithm, hybrids). Skim: §4 (experiments). Time: about 25 min (4.5 pages read, 3 skimmed) · Tier 3 · linked on 1 course page.
  3. Qwen Team (2025). Qwen3 Technical Report. Why read it: a full open family (0.6B dense to 235B-A22B MoE): QK-norm, global-batch load balancing, three-stage pretraining, and post-training that fuses thinking and non-thinking modes and distills strong to weak.
    Supports: 19 SFT & distillation, 30 Qwen. Read: §2 (architecture), §3.1 to §3.2 (data, pretraining stages), §4.1 to §4.5 (post-training). Skim: §4.7 (discussion). Time: about 25 min (5.5 pages read, 1 skimmed) · Tier 3 · linked on 9 course pages.
  4. Kimi Team (2025). Kimi K2: Open Agentic Intelligence. Why read it: MuonClip (Muon plus QK-Clip), a sparser MoE than V3 (384 experts, 8 active), and the agentic data synthesis and RL infrastructure; shows what an independent team kept from V3 and what it changed.
    Supports: 11 Stability, 14 Optimizers, 25 RL systems, 27 DeepSeek II, 32 Frontier open models. Read: §2.1 (MuonClip), §2.3 (model architecture), §3.2.3 and §3.3 (RL algorithm and infrastructure). Skim: §3.1.1 (agentic data synthesis). Time: about 25 min (5 pages read, 2 skimmed) · Tier 3 · linked on 11 course pages.

Common reading mistakes

  • "The abstract's number is the paper's result." Abstracts quote the most favorable comparison. DeepSeek-V2's "93.3% smaller KV cache and 5.76× generation throughput" compares against the dense DeepSeek 67B, an end-to-end system comparison and not an isolated MLA ablation (page 07). Before quoting a number, find the table, the baseline, the metric and the setting.
  • "A benchmark gap is a real gap." Ask for error bars: for accuracy \(\hat p\) on \(n\) questions the standard error is \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\) (page 18). DeepSeek-R1's AIME 2024 figures of 79.8% pass@1, 86.7% cons@64 and 90.0% pass@64 are three different quantities, so read which one a comparison uses.
  • "An ablation transfers across labs and scales." DeepSeek's own ablations put MLA ahead of MHA on 7 of 8 comparisons, while Llama 2's 30B ablation found GQA roughly matching MHA; parameter matching, training length and benchmark choice differ (page 07). Small-scale evidence is weak evidence about frontier scale: ST-MoE notes that small models are rarely unstable, so stability tricks are hard to test cheaply (page 11).
  • "If a report gives no reason for a choice, the choice was validated." Technical reports skip ablations. Qwen3's MoE drops the shared expert, and the report gives no reason and no ablation (page 30). Mark such choices "unexplained", not "proven", and say so in an interview.
  • "Read the newest report first." DeepSeek-V4 and V4.1-Flash build on V2 and V3: to see what CSA and HCA replace you need MLA's cache model and V3's expert layout. Read in release order, as the list is sequenced.
  • "Numbers from different papers are comparable." Check which \(N\) (total, non-embedding, activated), which peak (dense or with sparsity) and which unit (GB or GiB) each paper uses. Using the sparse 1,979 TFLOP/s peak instead of the dense 989 halves every MFU you compute (page 04, page 34).
  • "An arXiv link is one fixed document." Versions change numbers. DeepSeek-R1's January 2026 arXiv v2 differs from v1 in places, so cite the version you read (page 24, page 28).
  • "Having read it means being able to say it." Interviews test recall under pressure. After a paper, close it and rebuild one equation or table from memory: the DPO loss, the \(2N + 2N + KN\) memory accounting of ZeRO, the KV bytes per token of GQA.

Interview questions: can you explain it in one minute?

Self-test prompts built from the glossary. Each answer is what you would say in about a minute; try it aloud before opening the answer, and follow the link if any number is not yours yet.

Warm-up Explain the KV cache and how large it is for Llama-3-8B.

Causal attention never changes the keys and values of earlier tokens, so a decode step stores them and computes only the new token's query, key and value. The cost is memory: \(2\,n_{kv}\,d_h\,L\) elements per token. For Llama-3-8B (\(n_{kv}=8\), \(d_h=128\), \(L=32\), bf16) that is \(2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 32 \cdot 2 = 131{,}072\) bytes, 128 KiB per token, so an 8K context is exactly 1 GiB per sequence. Every decode step must also stream this cache from HBM, which is why decode is memory-bound. See page 06 and page 07.

Warm-up What is perplexity, and why is it not comparable across tokenizers?

Perplexity is \(\exp\) of the mean per-token loss in nats: the effective number of equally likely next tokens. It is per token, so a tokenizer that cuts the same text into fewer, longer tokens has a higher per-token loss without being a worse model. To compare models across tokenizers, normalize by the text itself: total negative log-likelihood divided by the number of bytes, in bits per byte. See page 01.

Warm-up Define MFU, and say what value is normal.

Model FLOPs utilization (模型浮点运算利用率) is the model FLOP throughput you observe divided by the hardware's dense peak, counting about \(6N\) FLOPs per token for training. An H100 SXM's dense bf16 peak is 989 TFLOP/s; the 1,979 figure on the datasheet assumes sparsity and halves every MFU if used by mistake. Llama 3 405B reports 430 TFLOP/s per GPU, 43%, on 8,192 GPUs; values from 30% to 45% are typical at scale. See page 04 and page 34.

Core Compare the KV cache of MHA, GQA and MLA with numbers.

Cached elements per token per layer: MHA \(2n_hd_h\), GQA \(2n_{kv}d_h\), MLA \(d_c + d_h^R\). Llama-3-8B with GQA caches \(2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 = 2048\) elements per layer, so 128 KiB per token over 32 layers; with MHA it would be 4 times that (\(n_h/n_{kv} = 4\)). DeepSeek-V3 with MLA caches \(512 + 64 = 576\) per layer, 70,272 bytes (68.6 KiB) per token over 61 layers in bf16, about 57 times less than MHA at its 128 heads (\(2 \cdot 128 \cdot 128 = 32{,}768\) elements). GQA's savings come from sharing heads, MLA's from a low-rank latent shared by all heads. See page 07.

Core How do you decide model size and token count, and why do labs over-train?

Use \(C \approx 6ND\) and the compute-optimal rule of about 20 tokens per parameter (Chinchilla), so \(N_{\text{opt}} \approx \sqrt{C/120}\). For \(C = 10^{24}\) FLOPs that is about 91B parameters and 1.8T tokens. That rule minimizes loss at fixed training compute and ignores inference. If the model will serve trillions of tokens, a smaller model trained much longer gives the same quality for less serving cost: Llama 3 8B saw 15T tokens, about 1,900 tokens per parameter, roughly 90 times the optimum. See page 13.

Core Total versus activated parameters in an MoE: what does sparsity save, and what does it not?

The FFN is replaced by \(n_r\) routed experts plus shared ones, and a router sends each token to \(k\) of them. FLOPs per token follow the activated parameters, \(C \approx 6N_{\text{act}}D\), but every expert must still be stored, so memory follows the total. DeepSeek-V3: 671B total, 37B activated (5.5%), so training FLOPs are about 18 times below a dense 671B model, while the weights still occupy about 671 GB at one byte per parameter (fp8). The price is load balancing (routing collapse, stragglers, overflow) and all-to-all communication under expert parallelism. See page 08.

Core Derive the DPO loss in one minute.

The KL-regularized objective \(\max_\pi \E[r] - \beta\,\KL(\pi \Vert \pi_{\text{ref}})\) has the optimum \(\pi^\star(y|x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}/Z(x)\). Invert it: \(r = \beta\log\frac{\pi^\star}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} + \beta\log Z(x)\). Bradley-Terry needs only the reward difference \(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l)\), so the intractable \(Z(x)\) cancels. Substituting and maximizing the likelihood of the preferences gives \(-\log\sigma\big(\beta[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}]\big)\): a classification loss with no reward model and no RL loop. Its limits are offline data and likelihood displacement. See page 21.

Core What does GRPO change relative to PPO, and what do DAPO's fixes address?

PPO estimates advantages with a learned critic, a second model as large as the policy (a 7B critic adds about 112 GB of training state at 16 bytes per parameter). GRPO samples a group of responses per prompt and uses the group's mean reward as the baseline (normalized by the standard deviation), so it needs no critic; the clipped ratio and the KL term stay. At scale, runs suffer entropy collapse and batches full of zero-advantage groups, and DAPO's four fixes target these and related problems: clip-higher (against entropy collapse), dynamic sampling (drop groups whose rewards are all equal), a token-level loss (normalize by total tokens, not per sample), and overlong reward shaping (against truncation noise). The reward is often a verifier (RLVR). See page 24.

Core How many bytes per parameter does mixed-precision Adam need, and what does each ZeRO stage shard?

bf16 weights (2) plus bf16 gradients (2) plus fp32 master weights and two Adam moments (4 + 4 + 4 = 12): \(2N + 2N + KN\) with \(K = 12\), so 16 bytes per parameter before activations. ZeRO stage 1 shards the optimizer states across the \(N_d\) data-parallel ranks, stage 2 adds the gradients, stage 3 adds the parameters, giving \(16N/N_d\) bytes per GPU. A 70B model needs 1.12 TB of states, so 17.5 GB per GPU on 64 GPUs at stage 3. Stages 1 and 2 keep the communication of plain data parallelism; stage 3 costs about 1.5 times as much. See page 15.

Deep-dive Why is batch-1 decode slow, and how does speculative decoding help without changing the output?

One decode step reads all weights and the KV cache and does only about one FLOP per byte, far below the H100 ridge of 295 FLOPs per byte, so it is memory-bound and the time is about (weight bytes + KV bytes)/HBM bandwidth. Llama-3-8B in bf16: 16.06 GB at 3.35 TB/s is 4.8 ms, about 208 tokens per second at batch 1. Speculative decoding uses the slack: a cheap draft \(q\) proposes \(\gamma\) tokens, the target \(p\) scores all \(\gamma + 1\) positions in one pass (intensity rises to about \(\gamma + 1\), still far below 295), and an acceptance rule keeps a prefix so that the output distribution is exactly \(p\). See page 17 and page 34.

Deep-dive How does a linear-attention or hybrid layer differ from full attention in memory and in recall?

Full attention caches \(2d_h\) numbers per token per KV head, growing with context. Linear attention keeps a fixed \(d_k \times d_v\) state per head (16,384 numbers for \(d_k = d_v = 128\), the cache of 64 tokens for one KV head), updated by a recurrence, so memory and per-token cost do not grow. The price is exact recall: any causal recurrent model needs \(\Omega(N)\) bits of state to solve multi-query associative recall over \(N\) tokens. Hybrids keep a few softmax layers to repair this (Qwen3-Next: 3 Gated DeltaNet layers to 1 softmax layer), and the gated delta rule adds decay (fast erasure) to the delta update (targeted overwrite). DeepSeek-V4 takes the other route: keep softmax attention but compress the KV cache along the sequence (CSA compresses every \(m\) tokens into one entry and then selects the top-\(k\) entries; HCA compresses every \(m' \gg m\) tokens into one). See page 09.

Deep-dive Explain RoPE and how YaRN extends its context.

RoPE rotates each pair of dimensions of \(q\) and \(k\) at position \(t\) by the angle \(t\theta_i\) with \(\theta_i = b^{-2i/d_h}\), so \(q_t \cdot k_j\) depends only on \(t - j\). Beyond the trained length it fails for two reasons: angles the network never saw, and attention dilution over more keys. Position interpolation divides every position by the scale \(s\), which also squeezes the high-frequency pairs that resolve local order. YaRN treats pairs by wavelength: pairs that turn many times inside the trained window are left alone, pairs with wavelength at least \(L\) are divided by \(s\), and pairs in between are blended. It also multiplies the logits by \((0.1\ln s + 1)^2\) to counter dilution. DeepSeek-V3 uses it to go from 4K to 128K. See page 05 and page 10.

Cheat sheet: the 30 terms to be able to say in a minute

  • Autoregressive language model 自回归: \(p(x_{1:T})=\prod_t p(x_t\mid x_{<t})\); trained with mean next-token cross-entropy; perplexity \(=e^{\text{loss}}\), per token, so not comparable across tokenizers.
  • BPE tokenizer 字节对编码: merge the most frequent adjacent pair; byte-level BPE starts from the 256 bytes, so there is no unknown token.
  • Attention and causal mask 因果掩码: \(\softmax(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_h} + M)V\) with \(M = -\infty\) above the diagonal; \(O(T^2)\) compute; heads work in separate subspaces.
  • Modern block 预归一化: Pre-LN, RMSNorm and a SwiGLU FFN; the FFN holds about 70% of Llama-3-8B's parameters.
  • RoPE 旋转位置编码: rotate each (q, k) pair by \(t\theta_i\), \(\theta_i = b^{-2i/d_h}\); the score \(q_t\cdot k_j\) depends only on \(t-j\).
  • KV cache 键值缓存: \(2\,n_{kv}d_hL\) elements per token; Llama-3-8B in bf16 is 128 KiB per token, 1 GiB at 8K.
  • Prefill and decode 预填充: prefill is parallel over the prompt and compute-bound; decode reads all weights and the cache every step and is memory-bound.
  • Roofline 屋顶线模型: attainable FLOP/s \(=\min(P,\, I\beta)\); the H100 ridge is \(989.4/3.35 \approx 295\) FLOPs per byte.
  • MFU 模型浮点运算利用率: observed model FLOP/s over the dense peak (989 TFLOP/s bf16 on an H100; 1,979 assumes sparsity); training costs about \(6N\) FLOPs per token.
  • GQA 分组查询注意力: \(n_{kv}\) shared KV heads, cache smaller by \(n_h/n_{kv}\) (4× for Llama-3-8B); MQA is \(n_{kv}=1\).
  • MLA 多头潜在注意力: cache one latent plus a decoupled-RoPE key: \(512 + 64 = 576\) elements per layer in DeepSeek-V3 (68.6 KiB per token).
  • MoE and activated parameters 混合专家: \(C \approx 6N_{\text{act}}D\) but memory follows the total \(N\); DeepSeek-V3: 671B total, 37B activated.
  • Load balancing 负载均衡: auxiliary loss \(\alpha\, n_r \sum_i f_iP_i\), or a per-expert routing bias with no auxiliary loss (V3); failure modes: collapse, stragglers, overflow.
  • Sliding-window and sparse attention 稀疏注意力: cost \(O(TW)\) or \(O(Tk)\) instead of \(O(T^2)\); \(\ell\) sliding-window layers reach about \(\ell(W-1)\) tokens back.
  • Linear attention and hybrids 线性注意力: fixed \(d_k \times d_v\) state per head; a constant state cannot recall \(N\) items exactly, so hybrids keep a few softmax layers (3:1 in Qwen3-Next).
  • Position interpolation and YaRN 位置插值: PI divides positions by \(s\); YaRN keeps high-frequency pairs, interpolates low-frequency ones and adds a logit temperature \((0.1\ln s + 1)^2\).
  • Scaling laws 缩放定律: \(C \approx 6ND\); compute-optimal is about 20 tokens per parameter; over-train small models (Llama 3 8B: 15T tokens) to cut serving cost.
  • Optimizer and schedule 优化器: AdamW with warmup, a hold and a decay; batch size counted in tokens and limited by the critical batch size.
  • Mixed precision 混合精度训练: bf16 weights and gradients plus an fp32 master copy and two Adam moments: \(2 + 2 + 12 = 16\) bytes per parameter.
  • ZeRO and FSDP 零冗余优化器: shard optimizer states (stage 1), gradients (2), parameters (3): \(16N/N_d\) bytes per GPU at stage 3, about 1.5× the communication of plain DP.
  • Parallelism 张量并行: tensor parallelism inside a node (all-reduce per layer), pipeline across nodes (bubble \((p-1)/m\) of ideal time), expert (all-to-all), context (long sequences), data (replicas).
  • FlashAttention 算子融合: tile \(Q, K, V\) in SRAM with online softmax: exact, never writes the \(T \times T\) matrix to HBM, recomputes tiles in the backward pass.
  • Speculative decoding 投机解码: draft \(\gamma\) tokens with a cheap \(q\), verify \(\gamma+1\) in one target pass; the output distribution is exactly \(p\).
  • Quantization 量化: named WxAy plus a KV format; PTQ converts a trained model, QAT trains with the quantizer; block scales cost bits (MXFP4: 4.25 per element).
  • SFT 监督微调: cross-entropy on response tokens only, in a chat template; behavior cloning, so it cannot say one answer beats another.
  • LoRA 低秩适配: \(W_0 + \tfrac{\alpha}{r}BA\), mergeable so serving is free; QLoRA adds an NF4 base and double quantization.
  • Reward model 奖励模型: Bradley-Terry: \(P(y_1 \succ y_2) = \sigma(r_1 - r_2)\); optimizing it hard causes over-optimization (Goodhart's law).
  • PPO and RLHF 近端策略优化: maximize \(\min(\rho A,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho, 1\pm\epsilon)A)\) with a KL penalty; four models in memory (policy, reference, reward, critic).
  • DPO 直接偏好优化: \(-\log\sigma\big(\beta[\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}]\big)\); \(Z(x)\) cancels.
  • GRPO and RLVR 组相对策略优化: group-mean baseline and no critic, with a program as the reward; watch for entropy collapse (DAPO's four fixes).
